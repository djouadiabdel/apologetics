# Step 1: Establish the parallel terminology

You first have to make the Muslim see what their own scripture says.

Hand him the Arabic:

> إِنَّمَا الْمَسِيحُ عِيسَى ابْنُ مَرْيَمَ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ وَكَلِمَتُهُ أَلْقَاهَا إِلَىٰ مَرْيَمَ وَرُوحٌ مِّنْهُ
> 
> _"The Messiah, Jesus son of Mary, was only a messenger of Allah, and **His Word** which He cast into Mary, and **a Spirit from Him**."_ (Q 4:171)

And from Q 3:45:

> إِذْ قَالَتِ الْمَلَائِكَةُ يَا مَرْيَمُ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يُبَشِّرُكِ بِكَلِمَةٍ مِّنْهُ اسْمُهُ الْمَسِيحُ عِيسَى ابْنُ مَرْيَمَ
> 
> _"O Mary, indeed Allah gives you good news of **a Word from Him**, whose name is the Messiah, Jesus son of Mary."_

Key features to make him notice:

1. _Kalimatuhu_ — "**His** Word." Not "_a_ word." The possessive pronoun refers to Allah. This is Allah's Word.
2. _Alqāhā ilā Maryam_ — "He **cast** it into Mary." The Word is _put into_ Mary. This is not "Allah said 'Be' and Jesus appeared from nowhere." The Word is the _thing inserted_ into Mary that becomes Jesus.
3. _Wa rūḥ minhu_ — "and a Spirit from Him." Stacked title. Jesus is _also_ identified with a divine Spirit.
4. These titles are **unique to Jesus**. No other prophet is called _kalimat Allāh_ in the Quran. Not Muhammad. Not Moses. Not Abraham. Only Jesus.

The Muslim has to admit this. The text is the text.

# Step 2: Force the question of identity

Now press: **what is the relation between Jesus and God's eternal Word?**

The standard Sunni position holds that the Quran is God's eternal Word. The Quran _also_ says Jesus is God's Word. So:

- Are these two different "Words of God"? If so, God has two eternal Words. (Polytheism creep.)
- Are these the same Word, manifesting in two modes (as book and as Jesus)? Then God's Word has two incarnations — one literary, one human. (This is _worse_ than Christian doctrine — at least we say one incarnation.)
- Is "Word" being used equivocally — meaning one thing for the Quran (the eternal Word) and a different thing for Jesus (something else called "Word" metaphorically)? Then the Quran is using its _highest theological title_ in a misleading way for the figure it most exalts among prophets.

These are the only three options. None of them are comfortable for the Muslim.

# Step 3: The equivocation trap (the most likely Muslim move)

The Muslim will almost certainly take option 3: _kalima_ applied to Jesus means something different from _kalām_ applied to the Quran. They'll say:

- The Quran is _kalām Allāh_ — God's eternal speech
- Jesus is _kalimat Allāh_ — God's "word" in the sense of "command," because God said _kun_ ("Be") and Jesus came into being

Note: _kalimat_ (the form used for Jesus) and _kalām_ (the form used for the Quran) are from the same root (k-l-m). They are not unrelated words. The Muslim will try to drive a wedge between them.

**Press here.** Three problems with the equivocation:

**Problem A: The Quran could have used the actual word for "command."** Arabic has _amr_ (command), _qawl_ (saying), _ḥukm_ (decree). The Quran does not use any of these for Jesus. It uses _kalima_ — the same root as _kalām_. If God meant "command," he chose his vocabulary poorly. If God is the author of the Quran (as Muslims affirm), the choice of _kalima_ is _deliberate_ and significant.

**Problem B: The "Be and it is" formula doesn't fit.** Q 3:59 says of Jesus: _"His example is like that of Adam — He created him from dust, then said to him 'Be' and he was."_ If "Word" just meant "the command 'Be,'" then Adam would _also_ be called _kalimat Allāh_. He is not. Adam is created by God's command but is not called God's Word. The Quran reserves _kalima_ for Jesus specifically. The Adam comparison breaks if "Word" means just "the command 'Be.'" You said this yourself earlier — the analogy only goes one way.

**Problem C: "Cast into Mary" doesn't fit "command."** The verb _alqā_ means to throw, to cast, to deposit. "He cast _the command_ into Mary" is nonsense — commands aren't cast into wombs. But "He cast _the Word_ into Mary" makes sense if the Word is a thing God can place. The grammar of Q 4:171 requires _kalima_ to be a _substantive_ — something that can be placed in a location — not just an _act of commanding_.

So the equivocation fails on three independent grounds. The Muslim cannot reduce _kalimat Allāh_ (Jesus) to "the command 'Be'" without doing violence to the text.

---

# Step 4: The Spirit problem stacks on top

Even if the Muslim somehow gets past the _kalima_ problem, they still have _rūḥ minhu_ — "a Spirit from Him."

This is harder to explain away. In the Quran, _rūḥ_ (spirit) is used in a very limited way:

- Of God's own Spirit (_rūḥ al-qudus_, the Holy Spirit, Q 2:87, 2:253, 5:110, 16:102)
- Of the spirit breathed into Adam (Q 15:29, 38:72)
- Of Jesus specifically (Q 4:171)

Notice: the Quran calls Jesus _rūḥ minhu_ — "a Spirit _from_ Him" (Allah). Not "a spirit _created by_ Allah." Not "a spirit _like other spirits_." A Spirit _from_ Him. The preposition _min_ (from) implies _origin in_, not _creation by_.

The standard Muslim response is that _rūḥ_ here means Jesus has a special spirit, not that he is divine. But the construction _min Allāh_ / _minhu_ is theologically loaded. It is used in the Quran for things that _proceed from_ God in a way that distinguishes them from ordinary created things. Allah does not say _khalaqahu_ (he created him) here — he says _minhu_ (from him).

This parallels exactly the Christian language of the Holy Spirit "proceeding from the Father." The Nicene Creed says the Holy Spirit "proceeds from the Father" (_ek tou patros ekporeuomenon_) — same theological structure. The Muslim has, in the Quran's grammar of _rūḥ minhu_, the same procession-language Christians use for the Holy Spirit, applied to Jesus.

So Jesus is, in the Quran's own grammar:

- The Word of God (cast into Mary)
- A Spirit from God (proceeding from him)

These are exactly the two divine processions the Christian doctrine of the Trinity affirms — the Son as eternal Word, the Spirit as proceeding from the Father. The Quran has, in 4:171, deposited the entire Trinitarian grammar onto Jesus.
