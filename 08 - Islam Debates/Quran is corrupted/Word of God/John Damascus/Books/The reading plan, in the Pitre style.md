## The reading plan, in the Pitre style

I'm going to give you a sequence. The principle: each book builds on the last, each one tells a story, each one has the _narrative-revealing_ quality you respond to. The goal is to arrive — by the end — at the place where the Logos doctrine and the Trinity feel as obvious and beautiful as the Eucharist now does.

I'll group them by phase, each phase building the next.

### Phase 1: The Jewish background of the Logos and divine identity

This is where the argument actually starts — not in Greek philosophy, but in Second Temple Judaism. The Christian doctrine of the Logos is not a Hellenistic intrusion onto Jewish monotheism. It is the _fulfillment of patterns already present_ in Jewish thought about God's Word, God's Wisdom, God's Spirit, and the divine identity. Pitre's book on the Eucharist showed you this method. We're now applying it to the doctrine of God.

**Read in this order:**

**1. Brant Pitre, _Jesus and the Jewish Roots of the Eucharist_** — you've already read this. It's the model. Notice how Pitre's method works _as a method_. You're not just learning about the Eucharist; you're learning _how Pitre reads_. This will pay off in everything else.

**2. Brant Pitre, _The Case for Jesus_** — Pitre's follow-up book, doing the same thing for the Gospels and Jesus' divine identity claims. He shows how Jesus' self-identification (the "I AM" sayings, the divine prerogatives, the application of OT YHWH-texts to himself) emerges naturally from the Jewish background. This is the _introduction_ to high Christology done in the Pitre method. Short, accessible, devastating.

**3. Richard Bauckham, _Jesus and the God of Israel_** — this is the _crucial_ book. Bauckham develops what he calls "divine identity Christology." His thesis: Second Temple Jews defined God not by abstract attributes (which leaves room for "highest creature" positions like Arius and Islam) but by _what only God does_ (creates, rules, receives worship, has the divine name). The New Testament places Jesus within this divine identity — not as a second God, but as included in the unique identity of YHWH. This is the _positive_ form of the argument you want against Islam. Bauckham is a scholar's scholar but writes accessibly. This is the book that, when you finish it, the Quranic Christology will look impossible from the inside.

**4. Larry Hurtado, _Lord Jesus Christ: Devotion to Jesus in Earliest Christianity_** — Hurtado's massive study of how early Christians worshipped Jesus. The shocking thesis, established with overwhelming evidence: the worship of Jesus as Lord did not develop over centuries. It was already present in the Aramaic-speaking Jewish community in the 30s AD. The high Christology _is_ the original. The Ebionite/Arian/Islamic "low Christology" is later sectarian deviation. This is dense but transformative. If you can only read one book on the historical Christology question, read this.

**5. Daniel Boyarin, _The Jewish Gospels: The Story of the Jewish Christ_** — Boyarin is a _Jewish_ scholar (not Christian), writing on how the Christian doctrines of Christ are not foreign impositions on Judaism but emerge from a genuine stream within Second Temple Jewish thought. His work on the "Two Powers in Heaven" tradition is essential. The shock value of a non-Christian Jewish scholar arguing this is enormous. You can use this against any Muslim who claims Christianity is a Greek corruption of Jewish monotheism.

After phase 1, you will have something you don't currently have: **the felt sense that high Christology is the natural reading of the Old Testament and the only reading that makes sense of Jesus**. The Logos doctrine will start to feel inevitable rather than philosophical.

### Phase 2: The patristic development of the doctrine

Now we move to how the early Church _worked out_ what the apostolic witness implied. This is where the philosophical machinery gets developed — but through the lens of churchmen actually wrestling with Christ, not as detached philosophy.

**6. Khaled Anatolios, _Retrieving Nicaea: The Development and Meaning of Trinitarian Doctrine_** — this is the best modern book on how the Nicene doctrine actually developed and what it means. Anatolios does the Pitre-style narrative work for the patristic period — he doesn't just give you "Arius said X, Athanasius said Y." He shows you the _intellectual stakes_, what each side was trying to protect, why the Nicene solution was discovered. By the time you finish, the doctrine of the Trinity is not a creedal abstraction but a _hard-won insight_ that you can feel the weight of.

**7. Athanasius, _On the Incarnation_** — the actual ancient text, short, readable, beautiful. Read it with the introduction by C.S. Lewis (which is itself one of the great short essays on theology). Athanasius wrote this in the 320s while in exile for opposing Arius. It is the foundational text of orthodox Christology. Lewis says reading a single book by an old master is worth ten books by modern interpreters — start trusting that principle here.

**8. Fr. John Behr, _The Way to Nicaea_ (volume 1 of _The Formation of Christian Theology_)** — Behr is the Orthodox patristic scholar whose lectures you already have on your list. His book on how Nicaea was reached is patient, narrative-driven, and shows the _story_ of doctrinal development. Read this. He's also from your tradition (Orthodox), which matters because the Orthodox patristic style is more story-driven than scholastic.

### Phase 3: The Logos doctrine in its full philosophical form

Now and only now do we get to the philosophical machinery — but you'll come to it with everything else already in place.

**9. John of Damascus, _On the Orthodox Faith_** (book 1 especially, on God and the Trinity) — the man himself. Read him after you've done phases 1 and 2. He'll feel like the natural culmination. You'll see him doing what you've been trying to do, with the tools of his time. Available free in English translation (Eerdmans' _Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers_ series, volume 9).

**10. David Bentley Hart, _The Experience of God: Being, Consciousness, Bliss_** — Hart is contemporary, brilliant, and writes about classical theism and the divine attributes in a way that addresses _exactly_ the philosophical machinery the Logos doctrine requires. This is where the divine simplicity argument becomes natural to you. Hart is also rhetorically merciless against bad philosophy, which you'll enjoy.

**11. Andrew Louth, _Introducing Eastern Orthodox Theology_** — short, accessible, written by a master. Louth is Orthodox, English, and writes beautifully. He'll help you integrate everything from a specifically Orthodox angle. Useful preparation for entering parish life in France.

### Phase 4: The polemical capstone, if you want it

Only if and when you want the Islam-specific polemic, _after_ the rest is internalized:

**12. Daniel Janosik, _John of Damascus, First Apologist to the Muslims_** — modern study of John's Islamic polemic. Read this last. By the time you reach it, you'll be reading it as someone who already knows the doctrine deeply, not as someone learning the doctrine through its polemical deployment.
