**The OT lock you want: Psalm 110:1.**

"The LORD says to my Lord: 'Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies your footstool.'"

Why this is the one:

- Jewish tradition unambiguously reads it as Messianic. _Midrash Tehillim_ on Psalm 18, _Midrash Tehillim_ on Psalm 110 itself, and the Talmud (_Sanhedrin_ 38b in context of two-thrones discussion, _Nedarim_ 32b) all apply it to the Messiah. The medieval shift to David-as-author-speaking-of-himself is a _late_ anti-Christian read; the older Jewish tradition is Messianic and the Muslim cannot use the late read without admitting the older tradition exists.
- Jesus uses it himself in Mark 12:35-37 / Matt 22:41-46 against the Pharisees, and _they cannot answer him_ — the Gospels record their silence, which is exegetically significant because a hostile-tradition record of being silenced is hard to fabricate.
- The Messiah is invited to _sit at God's right hand_. Not stand before God, not be honored by God — _sit at the right hand_. In Ancient Near Eastern and biblical idiom, the right hand of the throne is co-regency, shared rule, shared authority.
- Islam's Messiah cannot do this. The Quran's Jesus is a servant prophet who explicitly disclaims any such status (Q 5:116-117). But Psalm 110 says the Messiah _does_ this. So either the Messiah is not what Islam says he is, or Psalm 110 is not about the Messiah — and the Jewish tradition itself blocks that exit.

This is sharper than Genesis 49:10 because it isn't about _timing_, it's about _ontology and function_. It tells you something the Messiah does that the Islamic Messiah categorically cannot do, and the Jewish tradition agrees it's about the Messiah.

The honest caveat: the Muslim's escape attempt will be "well, 'sit at my right hand' is metaphorical honor, not co-regency." Your counter: the same Psalm continues (v. 4) "You are a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek" — a _priest-king_ fused figure, which the Islamic Messiah also cannot be. The two halves of Psalm 110 reinforce each other and both block the Islamic slot. Pair this with your Daniel 7:13-14 material from before (the son of man who _receives_ worship and an everlasting kingdom from the Ancient of Days) and you have two OT texts giving the Messiah a function that Islam's framework cannot accommodate.

**The NT lock you want: yes, the death-and-resurrection prediction is in all three Synoptics and triply attested.**

The triple tradition (Mark + Matthew + Luke independently) records the prediction three times:

- First: Mark 8:31 / Matt 16:21 / Luke 9:22
- Second: Mark 9:31 / Matt 17:22-23 / Luke 9:44
- Third: Mark 10:33-34 / Matt 20:18-19 / Luke 18:31-33

The third one is the most detailed and the hardest to escape. Mark 10:33-34: "Behold, we are going up to Jerusalem, and the Son of Man will be delivered to the chief priests and the scribes, and they will condemn him to death and deliver him to the Gentiles. And they will mock him and spit on him, and flog him and kill him. And after three days he will rise." Specifics: Jerusalem location, betrayal to Jewish authorities, transfer to Gentiles (Romans), mocking, spitting, flogging, killing, three-day resurrection. This is not vague.

There is also a fourth, even simpler version Muslims often miss: Matthew 12:39-40 — "an evil and adulterous generation seeks for a sign, but no sign will be given to it except the sign of the prophet Jonah. For just as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the great fish, so will the Son of Man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth." Jesus _names the sign of his prophethood_ — and the sign is death-and-burial-and-rising. By Deut 18, the prophet's sign is what validates him. Jesus stakes his prophetic validation on this specific event.

So the argument as you want to construct it:

Jesus, by his own clearly recorded words across multiple independent strands of the Gospel tradition, predicted he would be killed and rise on the third day, and he named this as the sign of his prophethood. Islam grants Jesus is a true prophet. Deuteronomy 18:21-22 says a prophet whose prediction does not come to pass is a false prophet. Therefore either (a) the prediction came to pass — he died and rose — and Christianity is correct, or (b) the prediction did not come to pass and Jesus is a false prophet, contradicting Islam. There is no third option.

The Muslim escape route is what you flagged: "those aren't Jesus's words, they're narrator's narration." But Matthew 12:39-40 is direct speech to interlocutors, not narrator commentary. And the consistency across three independent Gospels predicting the same event in the same shape is exactly the criterion historians use for authentic Jesus material — even _secular_ critical scholarship grants the predictions are authentic Jesus-tradition, the only debate being whether they were post-event retrojection (which is irrelevant to your argument, because Islam holds the Gospels were corrupted at the start, not edited after events Islam denies happened).

The cleanest deployment: lead with Matthew 12:39-40 because it is single, simple, in direct discourse, and Jesus stakes his own prophetic credentials on it. If they try to dispute it, point to the triple attestation in Mark/Matt/Luke. If they try "all four are corruptions," you are back at Q 5:47 and the _tahrif_ problem you already solved in the prior conversation.

The combined play: Psalm 110:1 says the Messiah does something Islam's Messiah cannot do; Matthew 12:39-40 says Jesus's own prophethood stands or falls on death and resurrection. Together they collapse the Islamic position from both ends — the OT and the NT, the Messianic role and the prophetic credential.

# Counters they may try:

**1. The Psalm 110 superscription problem.** The psalm is titled _le-David_ — "of/to/for David." If David wrote it, then "The LORD says to my Lord" has David calling someone _else_ "my Lord," which is the Christian (and old Jewish) Messianic read. But the Hebrew preposition _le-_ is ambiguous — it can mean "by David," "for David," "concerning David," or "dedicated to David." A genuine Muslim (or Jewish) interlocutor can argue: this psalm was written _about_ David by a court poet, addressing David as "my Lord" (the king), and "the LORD says to my Lord [the king David]: sit at my right hand" is enthronement language for the Davidic king, not a second divine figure. On this reading, Psalm 110 is royal-coronation poetry, not Messianic prophecy. This is a _legitimate_ exegetical move. Your counter is that (a) the older Jewish tradition reads it Messianically anyway, (b) Jesus's argument in Mark 12 presupposes Davidic authorship and his interlocutors don't dispute it, and (c) v. 4's "priest forever after the order of Melchizedek" does not fit any Davidic king — David was not a priest, and Israelite kings were forbidden the priesthood (cf. Uzziah, 2 Chr 26). But you should know the superscription argument is real and not stupid.

**2. The "Son of Man" referent problem.** When Jesus predicts the Son of Man's death and resurrection, a Muslim can ask: does "Son of Man" _necessarily_ mean Jesus himself? In Aramaic _bar enasha_ can be a circumlocution for "I," but it can also mean "a human being" generically, or it can be the apocalyptic figure of Daniel 7. Some critical scholars (not Muslims, scholars) have argued Jesus used "Son of Man" to refer to a coming figure _other than himself_. On that reading, Jesus is predicting _someone else's_ death and resurrection, not his own, and Deut 18 doesn't catch him. This is a real reading in the academic literature. Your counter is that the Gospel texts make the identification explicit in context ("the Son of Man _must_ suffer many things... and _be killed, and after three days rise again_" followed immediately by Peter rebuking _Jesus_, Mark 8:31-32 — the rebuke only makes sense if Peter heard Jesus referring to himself). But the Muslim can press: that's the Gospel writer's framing, not necessarily Jesus's intent.

**3. The _vaticinium ex eventu_ problem and what it does to your argument.** Critical scholarship widely holds the death-and-resurrection predictions were composed or sharpened _after_ the events, retrojected into Jesus's mouth by the evangelists. A genuinely engaged Muslim who has read any critical NT scholarship will raise this. Now here's the thing — _for your specific argument_ this actually doesn't matter, because Islam's claim is not "the predictions were retrojected after a real death," it is "Jesus didn't die at all." The retrojection hypothesis presupposes the death happened. So the Muslim cannot use this argument without conceding the crucifixion. But you should _know_ the argument exists, because if you wave it off without understanding it, you look uninformed. The Matthew 12:39-40 Jonah saying is the strongest because most critical scholars grant it as authentic Jesus material — it's awkward, it uses an obscure typology, and it doesn't match post-Easter formulations cleanly (three days and three _nights_, which doesn't fit Friday afternoon to Sunday morning), all of which are criteria of authenticity.

**4. The Jonah-saying timing problem — and this one is interesting.** Matt 12:40 says "three days and three nights." Jesus dies Friday afternoon, rises Sunday morning. That's two nights (Friday and Saturday) and parts of three days (Friday, Saturday, Sunday). Not three full nights. A Muslim can press: by Jesus's own stated sign, the prediction failed on its own terms. Your counter is the standard Jewish idiomatic reckoning where any part of a day counts as a day (cf. 1 Sam 30:12-13, Esther 4:16 vs 5:1), which is well-attested. But the Muslim's complaint is not crazy — it requires you to appeal to idiom rather than literal reading, and you should grant that the literal reading creates tension.

**5. The deeper hermeneutical complaint.** A thoughtful Muslim can say: "Your entire argument depends on reading the OT _forward_ through the NT — finding Messianic content where Jewish interpreters for two millennia have not found it, or where they have found different content. You're claiming Psalm 110 and Daniel 7 and Isaiah 53 all converge on a divine-Messiah figure, but each text in isolation is far more ambiguous than your synthesis suggests. The synthesis is doing the work, not the texts. And the synthesis is _Christian_, retrojected onto the Hebrew Bible. The Muslim position is that the Hebrew Bible's actual content is consistent with Islamic monotheism, and the Christian reading is exactly the kind of _tahrif al-ma'na_ (interpretive distortion) the Quran warns against."

This is the deepest objection and you should respect it. The honest answer is: yes, Christian reading _does_ synthesize across texts, and yes, the synthesis is shaped by the resurrection event. But (a) the older Jewish tradition itself was doing exactly this kind of synthesis pre-Christianity — the two-powers material, the Targumic Messianic readings, the Melchizedek speculation at Qumran (11Q13), the Similitudes of Enoch's heavenly Son of Man — Christianity inherited a Jewish tradition that _already_ read these texts together and _already_ expected a high-Messianic figure. The Christian claim is not "we invented a new synthesis," it is "we identified the synthesis Jewish tradition was already making with the figure who fulfilled it." (b) The Muslim's counter-claim is itself a synthesis — reading the OT consistent-with-Tawhid is also a retrojective reading, just retrojecting Quranic theology rather than NT theology. There is no neutral reading. The question is which retrojective synthesis better fits the texts in their pre-Christian, pre-Islamic state. And the pre-Christian Jewish evidence (Segal, Boyarin, the Qumran finds, the Targums, the Similitudes) shows the high-Messianic-divine-figure synthesis was already there in Second Temple Judaism — which Islam, arriving six centuries later, cannot account for.

**6. The "but the Gospels disagree among themselves" complaint.** A Muslim can point to genuine tensions in the death-and-resurrection accounts — the women at the tomb, the order of appearances, John's chronology versus the Synoptics on Passover, and so on. The argument is: if the Gospels can't agree on the details of the central event, why should we trust them on the prediction of it? Your counter is that variation in peripheral detail is the _signature of independent attestation_, not its opposite — coordinated agreement would suggest collusion. The core kerygma (he died, he was buried, he rose, he appeared) is stable across every NT stratum including the pre-Pauline creed in 1 Cor 15:3-7, which dates to within five years of the event.

**My honest take for you.** The strongest genuine Muslim complaint is #1 (the Psalm 110 superscription) and #5 (the hermeneutical synthesis objection). The others have answers that are real but require argumentation. If a Muslim raises #1 and #5 thoughtfully, you should _acknowledge_ them as serious — that earns the credibility to press your case. The mistake to avoid is treating every Muslim objection as bad-faith deflection. Some of them are real exegetical questions that Jewish, Christian, and academic scholars have argued about for centuries. Your job in apologetics is not to crush every objection but to show that the _cumulative weight_ of the evidence favors the Christian reading once you grant the data Islam itself accepts (the prophethood of Jesus, the integrity of the Torah and Gospel as God-sent, the historical reality of the first century).

One last thing. You should be ready for the move where the Muslim says: "Okay, I cannot answer this fully right now, but I trust the Quran and I trust that there is an answer." That is not a bad-faith move — it is the same move you and I and every Christian makes when we cannot fully answer a hard objection but trust the cumulative case. Respect it when you hear it. The apologetic goal is not to force conversion in one conversation; it is to plant doubts that work over months and years, and to be the kind of interlocutor whose arguments your friend remembers when he is alone with the text.


# Leviticus:
The hermeneutical complaint says: "Your Christian synthesis is retrojective; the Jewish/Islamic reading is also a synthesis; neither is neutral." That sounds initially like a stalemate. But it isn't, because Leviticus 17:11 is _not_ synthesis — it's legislation. It is the text saying, in its own voice, in non-typological prose, _this is how atonement works in My economy_. Blood. Substitution. Life-for-life. The text is not ambiguous, it is not symbolic, it is not retrojected — it is a legal stipulation embedded in the central cultic code of the Torah, which Islam affirms as God's word.

So the move forces the Muslim to answer one question: **what happens to the atonement system?**

There are exactly three available answers, and each one breaks Islam:

**Answer 1: It was abrogated.** But the Quran does not teach this. The Quran does not say the Levitical sacrificial system was repealed. It simply has no atonement theology at all — sin is forgiven by repentance and works, without blood. If Islam claims abrogation, it must produce the verse or the prophet who abrogated it. There is none. And the principle of _naskh_ (abrogation) within Islam works _within_ the Quranic corpus and between Quranic and earlier revelation, but it requires the abrogating revelation to _say so_. Silence is not abrogation.

**Answer 2: It is still in force.** But the Temple has been destroyed for nineteen and a half centuries. The system is _physically impossible_ to operate. There is no altar, no priesthood functioning in the Aaronic line with verified genealogy, no Holy of Holies. If the system is in force and inoperable, then either God legislated a system He then made impossible to fulfill (which makes God incoherent), or the system was meant to point beyond itself to something that _would_ fulfill it.

**Answer 3: It was fulfilled.** This is the Christian answer. The blood-substitution-life-for-life pattern of Leviticus is completed in the figure of Isaiah 53 who bears iniquity vicariously as an _asham_ (guilt offering), and the historical event in 30 AD does what the system pointed toward. The Temple's destruction in 70 AD then makes sense — the shadow ends when the substance arrives (cf. the _theological_ point Hebrews makes, which the Muslim need not grant, but the _historical_ point that the Temple fell precisely after the Christian claim that it was no longer needed is empirically striking).

Islam has no answer 3 available because Islam denies the crucifixion. So Islam is forced into answer 1 or answer 2, and both fail on Islam's own premises.

This is why Leviticus 17:11 is the load-bearing argument and why your instinct in the earlier turn was correct. It is not a Christian synthesis imposed on the OT; it is the OT speaking in its plainest legislative voice, and Islam has no slot to put it in. The hermeneutical objection from #5 dissolves because Leviticus is not asking to be interpreted typologically — it is asking to be _obeyed_, and the question is what happens when obedience becomes impossible.

The sequencing you now have is genuinely strong:

First, **Q 5:43-47** locks the Torah and Gospel as God-sent and not textually corrupt — your prior conversation already secured this ground.

Second, **Leviticus 17:11** poses the atonement question Islam cannot answer.

Third, **Psalm 110 and Daniel 7** show that Jewish tradition itself was reading a high-Messianic figure into the texts before Christianity arrived — defusing the "Christian retrojection" objection.

Fourth, **Matthew 12:39-40 plus Mark 10:33-34** make Jesus's own prophetic credentials stand or fall on death and resurrection, which Islam grants Jesus is a prophet but denies the event — Deut 18 catches the contradiction.

Fifth, when the interlocutor reaches for "the Gospels are corrupted," you are back at Q 5:47 and _tahrif al-lafz_ vs _tahrif al-ma'na_, which is closed ground.

You have built a vise with four jaws — the OT legislative requirement, the OT Messianic expectation, the NT prophetic self-claim, and the Quran's own affirmation of the prior scriptures — and there is no exit from the middle. That is what a good apologetic structure looks like: not one knockout argument, but a topology in which every move the interlocutor tries leads back to a position he cannot hold.


# Matthew 24:14:

You've turned Matt 24:14 from a "look how impressive the fulfillment is" argument into a **forced-disjunction argument** with no neutral middle. The shape is:

Jesus, in his own prophetic voice, predicts that _his gospel_ will be preached to all nations as testimony before the end. This is a prediction about a specific message — not religion in general, not monotheism, not eventual truth — _this gospel_, the one he is proclaiming, the one his followers will carry. Deut 18 binds: prediction fulfilled = vindicated prophet; prediction failed = false prophet. There is no third state.

Now the disjunction:

**Option A:** The gospel that spread _is_ the one Jesus predicted would spread. Then Christianity is vindicated. The crucified-and-risen Jesus message, proclaimed by martyrs across two millennia, is what Jesus himself was pointing to. Islam is wrong because the gospel-that-spread is the gospel-Jesus-vouched-for, and that gospel directly contradicts Islam on the crucifixion, the resurrection, and the divinity.

**Option B:** The gospel that spread is _not_ the one Jesus predicted. Then Jesus's prediction failed — his gospel did not reach the nations, a different gospel did. By Deut 18, Jesus is a false prophet. Islam, which calls Jesus a true prophet, collapses.

**Option C** (the one you closed): "Islam is the fulfillment of Matt 24:14." This fails on three independent grounds, any one of which is sufficient:

First, Islam does not claim to be the _injil_. The Quran consistently distinguishes the injil (given to Jesus, in the past tense) from the Quran (given to Muhammad). Islam does not say "the gospel Jesus preached is what we are preaching" — it says Jesus preached the injil, the injil was corrupted or lost, and the Quran now stands in its place. So Islam structurally cannot claim to be the gospel-Jesus-predicted-would-spread.

Second, Islam has no extant injil. There is no Islamic text that is "the gospel of Jesus." The Quran references it, affirms it was given, and then leaves Muslims with no copy of it. So even if Islam wanted to claim fulfillment, it has nothing to point to.

Third, Islam's expansion mechanism is wrong for Matt 24:14. The prediction specifies _kerusso_ (proclamation) and _marturion_ (testimony, which becomes martyrdom). Christianity's first three centuries match this exactly — verbal preaching under persecution, martyrs as the seed of the church. Islam's first three centuries were political and military expansion under state power from Medina onward. The mechanisms don't match.

So Option C is closed, and the Muslim is forced into A or B. And A _is the Christian conclusion_, so the Muslim must take B — which destroys his own position because B makes Jesus a false prophet, and Islam needs Jesus to be a true prophet.

This is genuinely tight. The vise has no exit.

**Your second framing — Paul.**

"If Paul is a false prophet then he won and Jesus lost." This is doing real work and you should keep it, but let me sharpen the logic, because there's a precise way to deploy it.

The Muslim move (and frankly the move of a lot of skeptical scholarship) is to drive a wedge between Jesus and Paul: Jesus was a true monotheistic prophet, Paul invented the divine-Christ-substitutionary-atonement religion that became Christianity. On this reading, the "gospel" that spread to all nations is _Paul's_ gospel, not Jesus's, and Matt 24:14 is fulfilled by a corruption rather than by Jesus's actual message.

Your counter is exactly right and worth stating cleanly: **if that's true, Jesus lost and Paul won, and Jesus is a false prophet by his own Matt 24:14 prediction**. Because Jesus said _this gospel_ — the one he was preaching — would reach all nations. If what actually reached all nations is Paul's invention, then Jesus's gospel did not reach the nations, Paul's did, and Jesus's prediction failed. Deut 18 applies. The Muslim cannot rescue Jesus as a true prophet by attributing the spread to Paul, because that attribution is _itself_ a Deut 18 indictment of Jesus.

There's an even sharper point inside this. The Muslim position requires Jesus to have preached a non-divine, non-atoning, prophetic message that was then _immediately_ and _universally_ replaced by Paul's invented version within roughly twenty years — so completely that no trace of the original survives in any source, and the entire first-generation church including Jesus's own brother James and the Jerusalem apostles either converted to Paul's invention or were erased from history. This requires Jesus's actual message to have failed _instantly_ and _totally_ among his own followers. By Deut 18, a prophet whose message fails _with his own disciples within his own generation_ is not a vindicated prophet. So even granting the Muslim the Paul-corruption thesis, the position destroys itself: it makes Jesus a prophet whose mission collapsed before it left Jerusalem.

The Christian counter-claim is that Paul did not invent the high Christology — he inherited it. The pre-Pauline creed in 1 Cor 15:3-7, datable by every critical scholar to within five years of the crucifixion, already contains "Christ died _for our sins_ according to the Scriptures, he was buried, he was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures, and he appeared." Atonement, resurrection, scriptural fulfillment — all present before Paul converted. The Philippians 2:6-11 hymn, also pre-Pauline, has Christ "in the form of God" who "did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped." High Christology is not a Pauline invention; it is the _received tradition_ Paul inherited from the Jerusalem church within five years of the cross. So the Muslim's Paul-corruption thesis fails empirically on the texts Islam itself cannot date away.

**The third framing — "if Muhammad is correct Jesus is over."**

This is the cleanest of the three because it states the structural incompatibility most directly. Islam's claim and Christianity's claim are not "two compatible religions with different emphases" — they are _mutually annihilating_ on the specific points where they meet. If Muhammad is right, Jesus did not die, did not rise, did not atone, is not divine, and his followers worship a falsehood. If Jesus is right, Muhammad's revelation contradicts the prior revelation Muhammad himself claimed to confirm, and Muhammad fails his own claimed standard. There is no peaceful coexistence in the propositional content. One of them is over.
