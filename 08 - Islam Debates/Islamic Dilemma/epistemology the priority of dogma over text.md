# The Memra Argument — Why You Are Right To Press It

The **Memra** (מֵימְרָא — "the Word") is the Aramaic theological concept developed in the Targums — the Aramaic translations and paraphrases of the Hebrew Bible used in synagogue worship from the Second Temple period onward. The Targums (Onkelos, Pseudo-Jonathan, Neofiti, Jonathan ben Uzziel) repeatedly insert "the Memra of YHWH" where the Hebrew text has YHWH acting directly. The Memra creates the world, makes covenants, saves Israel, appears to the patriarchs, gives the Law, and forgives sin. The Memra _is_ YHWH in his self-revealing aspect — distinct in some sense, yet fully divine.

Examples worth memorizing:

- **Targum Onkelos on Genesis 1:27** — "And the Memra of the Lord created man in his image"
- **Targum Neofiti on Genesis 1:1** — "From the beginning, with wisdom, the Memra of the Lord created and perfected the heavens and the earth"
- **Targum Onkelos on Exodus 19:17** — Moses brings the people to meet "the Memra of God"
- **Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Deuteronomy 4:7** — "What nation is so great as to have the Memra of the Lord sitting on the throne, high and lifted up, hearing our prayers"
- **Targum Jonathan on Isaiah 45:17** — "Israel shall be saved by the Memra of the Lord"
- **Targum Neofiti on Exodus 12:42** — describes four nights of salvation, the fourth being the redemption through the Memra

The Memra is not a mere word, not a created intermediary — it is presented as YHWH himself in his manifest, communicative aspect. The Jewish liturgical tradition was using this language for centuries before Christ and continued using it into the Talmudic period.

Now connect this to John 1:1: Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ Λόγος, καὶ ὁ Λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν Θεόν, καὶ Θεὸς ἦν ὁ Λόγος — "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." John is writing in Greek for a Jewish-and-Gentile audience, and he is taking the _exact_ structural slot that the Memra occupies in the Targums — the divine Word who is with God and is God — and identifying Jesus with it. To anyone familiar with the Targumic tradition, John 1 is unmistakable: this is the Memra, and the Memra is Christ.

The Qur'an applies to Jesus the title that the entire Jewish-Aramaic theological tradition had reserved for the divine self-revelation of YHWH.

## The Classical Tafsir on Jesus as _Kalimat Allah_

The classical Muslim _mufassirun_ (exegetes) recognized that this title was a problem and tried to handle it. Their attempted explanations are themselves evidence for your argument.

**Tafsir al-Tabari** (d. 923) on Surah 4:171 records multiple opinions among the early Muslims. One opinion attributed to Qatada and al-Suddi says Jesus is called _kalimat Allah_ because he came into existence by the word _kun_. But al-Tabari himself notes the difficulty: this reading does not distinguish Jesus from any other creature. Adam was also created by _kun_, yet not called _kalimat Allah_. Tabari acknowledges the uniqueness of the title but cannot fully account for it within Islamic theology.

**Tafsir al-Razi** (Fakhr al-Din al-Razi, d. 1210) — and this is the one to memorize — devotes significant space in his _Mafatih al-Ghayb_ on Surah 4:171 to the Christian use of this term. Razi explicitly acknowledges that Christians use _kalimat Allah_ in their Trinitarian sense and that the term has theological weight. He then has to argue _against_ the Christian reading. The fact that one of the greatest Sunni theologians has to write an extended polemic against the natural reading of the verse is itself the proof: the natural reading favors the Christian.

Razi offers several alternative meanings: Jesus is called Word because (1) he was created by the word _kun_; (2) he was a fulfillment of God's prior promises ("words"); (3) he confirmed prior revelations; (4) he was a _sign_ (a "word" in the sense of a token or message). Notice what is happening: Razi cannot give _one_ settled meaning. He has to offer multiple alternative interpretations because none of them on its own is convincing. This is the hermeneutic mark of a reading that is _constructed against_ the natural meaning rather than derived from it.

**Tafsir al-Baydawi** (d. 1286), one of the most respected mainstream Sunni _tafsirs_, similarly equivocates on Surah 4:171. Baydawi acknowledges the term but constrains it to "created by the word." He cannot explain why this title is uniquely Jesus's.

**Tafsir Ibn Kathir** (d. 1373) takes the same line but is even more defensive. Ibn Kathir spends substantial space attacking the Christian reading, which again signals: the natural reading is the Christian one, and Muslim exegetes know it.

The pattern across the _tafsir_ tradition is consistent: every major _mufassir_ recognizes that the term _kalimat Allah_ is theologically loaded, recognizes that Christians read it in a Trinitarian sense, and has to construct a defensive reading to avoid that conclusion. The defensive readings vary, contradict each other, and rely on linguistic moves that flatten the uniqueness of the title.

This is exactly the pattern you identified in the Avery debate. The _tafsir_ tradition is doing the same ad hoc work: protecting a conclusion against the text rather than letting the text speak.
