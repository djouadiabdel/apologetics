The Nonexistent Temple

Quran 17:1 and the Exposure of Muhammad as a Fraud

A Critical Compilation and Analysis  
Based on the Research of Sam Shamoun

  

# Introduction: The Central Claim

This document presents a systematic, evidence-based examination of one of the most significant historical and textual problems in Islamic origins: the claim in Quran 17:1 that the Prophet Muhammad was taken in a single night from the Sacred Mosque in Mecca to al-Masjid al-Aqsa — the 'Farthest Mosque' — whose surroundings God had blessed. 

Using primarily Islamic sources themselves — early tafsirs, hadith collections, and modern Muslim scholars — this analysis demonstrates that the verse refers to a physical building or sanctuary in Jerusalem that simply did not exist at the time of the alleged event (circa 621 CE). The cumulative weight of the evidence, as meticulously documented by Christian apologist Sam Shamoun, leads inexorably to one conclusion: this constitutes a clear historical blunder that exposes Muhammad as a false prophet.

We will build the case slowly, layer by layer, allowing each piece of evidence to reinforce the previous one until the full picture emerges with unmistakable clarity.

  

# Section 1: The Historical Context — The Temple Was Gone for Centuries

Before examining the Islamic texts, we must establish the undisputed historical baseline. The Second Jewish Temple in Jerusalem was destroyed by the Roman general Titus in 70 CE during the First Jewish-Roman War. The site remained in ruins for centuries under Roman and then Byzantine rule. By the early 7th century — the time of Muhammad — the Temple Mount was not a place of active worship but a neglected area, often described in contemporary sources as a refuse dump or open space.

Key historical fact: No Jewish Temple stood in Jerusalem after 70 CE. No Christian church or major sanctuary occupied the precise site in the form Muhammad would later describe. And crucially, no Islamic mosque existed there until the late 7th/early 8th century under the Umayyad caliphs Abd al-Malik (r. 685–705) and al-Walid I (r. 705–715), who constructed the Dome of the Rock (~691 CE) and the congregational Al-Aqsa Mosque.

This sets up the core problem: If Muhammad was taken to a physical 'Farthest Mosque' in 621 CE, what building was he taken to? The answer, according to the evidence we will now examine, is that there was none — yet the Islamic sources insist there was.

  

# Section 2: The Quranic Verse — A Clear Reference to a Physical Place

Quran 17:1 states (Sahih International translation): 

"Exalted is He who took His Servant by night from al-Masjid al-Haram to al-Masjid al-Aqsa, whose surroundings We have blessed, to show him of Our signs. Indeed, He is the Hearing, the Seeing."

The Arabic term al-Masjid al-Aqsa literally means 'the Farthest Mosque' or 'the Farthest Place of Prostration.' In Quranic usage, 'masjid' consistently refers to a place of worship or sanctuary. The verse pairs it directly with al-Masjid al-Haram (the Sacred Mosque in Mecca), which was a physical structure. The grammatical and contextual parallelism strongly implies that al-Masjid al-Aqsa is likewise a physical location — not merely an abstract 'site' or 'land.'

As Shamoun notes: 'The Qur’anic reference to the Al-Aqsa Mosque, as a mosque, took place years before the actual arrival of Muslims to Jerusalem. It means that part of what the Muslim believes is that Al-Aqsa Mosque was designated as a mosque by God.'

The verse does not say 'the site where a mosque will one day stand' or 'the ruined Temple Mount.' It refers to an existing al-Masjid al-Aqsa with 'surroundings' that are blessed. This is the first layer of evidence.

  

# Section 3: Early Muslim Tafsirs and Scholarly Consensus — A Physical Sanctuary in Jerusalem

The earliest available Muslim tafsir sources, from Muqatil b. Sulayman (d. 150/767) onward, are absolutely agreed that the Qur’anic al-Masjid al-Aqsa stands for a sanctuary in Jerusalem. This is one of those not so frequent cases in which just one interpretation is suggested for a given Qur’anic passage, in all the early commentaries including al-Tabari (d. 310/923).

Uri Rubin, a leading scholar of early Islam (quoted approvingly by Shamoun), confirms: 'The EARLIEST AVAILABLE MUSLIM TAFSIR SOURCES... ARE ABSOLUTELY AGREED THAT THE QUR’ANIC al-Masjid Al-Aqsa STANDS FOR A SANCTUARY IN JERUSALEM.'

Ibn Kathir, one of the most authoritative commentators, explicitly states that Al-Aqsa Mosque is Bayt al-Maqdis (the House of the Holy — Jerusalem). The terms are used interchangeably.

This scholarly consensus among the earliest Muslims is crucial. They did not interpret the verse as referring to a future building or a vague 'holy land.' They understood it as a real sanctuary in Jerusalem that Muhammad visited. Yet history shows no such sanctuary existed in 621 CE.

  

# Section 4: The Hadith Evidence — Muhammad Describing a Standing Physical Building

The problem becomes even more acute when we turn to the hadith literature, which provides detailed accounts of what Muhammad allegedly saw and described upon his return.

One narration states that when the Quraysh disbelieved him, Muhammad answered: 'Allah lifted me before Bait-ul-Maqdis and I began to narrate to them (the Quraish of Mecca) its signs while I was in fact looking at it.' (Sahih Muslim)

Another tradition records: 'I stood at al-Hijr, visualised Bayt al-Muqaddas and described its signs. Some of them said: How many doors are there in that mosque? I had not counted them so I began to look at it and counted them one by one and gave them information concerning them.' (Ibn Sa’d, Kitab al-Tabaqat al-Kabir)

Shamoun highlights the glaring anachronism: 'There is a real problem here for the structure had been destroyed more than five hundred years earlier and the site at that time had become a rubbish-dump... It cannot be said that Muhammad saw a vision of the Temple as it had been before it was destroyed for the Quraysh were asking him to describe contemporary Jerusalem as he saw it that very night. How could he have counted the doors of a building that no longer existed?'

The hadiths portray Muhammad as physically present at — and carefully observing — a standing mosque with multiple doors. This is not a description of ruins or an empty site. It is the description of an active, physical place of worship.

  

# Section 5: Muslim Apologists’ Own Sources Backfire — Admissions of a Physical Mosque

Perhaps the most devastating evidence comes from modern Muslim scholars whom Shamoun engages. These sources, intended to defend Islam, inadvertently confirm the physical-building interpretation.

Mustafa Abu Sway writes: 'Verily, ‘Al-Aqsa’ is a name for THE WHOLE MOSQUE which is surrounded by the wall… for THE BUILDING that exists in the southern part of the Mosque...' He further states that 'Al-Aqsa Mosque was developed and the buildings expanded on a large scale during the reign of the two seventh and eighth century Umayyad Caliphs...'

Uri Rubin notes that in extra-Qur’anic texts, al-masjid al-aqsa is 'DEFINITELY A DESCRIPTION OF A MOSQUE UPON THE EARTH.'

Khalid El-Awaisi, in his detailed study of the names of Islamic Jerusalem, repeatedly acknowledges that hadiths refer to al-Aqsa as a physical mosque: 'the journey was to Bayt al-Maqdis in general AND THE MOSQUE OF BAYT AL-MAQDIS (al-Aqsa Mosque) IN PARTICULAR (Qur’an 17:1).' He notes narrations where the Prophet is asked to describe 'THE MOSQUE' and replies that 'THE MOSQUE WAS BROUGHT WHILE I WAS LOOKING AT IT.'

Shamoun observes that these Muslim authors are 'seemingly trying too hard to prove... that Bayt al-Maqdis doesn’t have to always mean the Mosque,' yet they repeatedly admit the opposite in their own analyses. The author of the article Shamoun critiques even concedes that in certain hadiths 'Bayt al-Maqdis REFERS ONLY TO AL-AQSA MOSQUE.'

This is the turning point in the argument: Even defenders of the Islamic narrative cannot escape the conclusion that the earliest sources treat al-Masjid al-Aqsa as a physical, standing mosque.

  

# Section 6: The Biblical Parallel and Why It Fails to Rescue the Quranic Claim

One Muslim apologist attempted to defend the verse by citing Ezra 2:68: 'When they arrived at the house of the Lord in Jerusalem, some of the heads of the families gave freewill offerings toward the rebuilding of the house of God on its site.'

Shamoun dismantles this parallel with precision. In the book of Ezra, the immediate context makes it crystal clear that the Temple had been destroyed and was being rebuilt: 'King Cyrus... has appointed me TO BUILD HIM A HOUSE AT JERUSALEM... AND BUILD THE HOUSE OF THE LORD...' (Ezra 1:1-6). The phrase 'on its site' and the surrounding verses about foundations not yet laid explicitly signal that this is a reference to a location, not a standing building.

In contrast, Quran 17:1 contains no such contextual qualifiers. There is no mention of ruins, rebuilding, or 'site.' The verse presents al-Masjid al-Aqsa as an existing reality parallel to al-Masjid al-Haram. As Shamoun concludes: 'Unlike the context of Ezra 2:68, there is nothing in the context of Q. 17:1 that even remotely suggests that masjid al-aqsa refers to a place or site.'

  

# Section 7: Expert Testimony — John Gilchrist on the Mythical Nature of the Narrative

Christian scholar John Gilchrist provides a devastating summary of the cumulative problems:

"Furthermore the narratives in the Hadith expose a glaring anachronism. After proclaiming that he had been to Jerusalem Muhammad was allegedly asked to describe the Temple... There is a real problem here for the structure had been destroyed more than five hundred years earlier... It cannot be said that Muhammad saw a vision of the Temple as it had been before it was destroyed for the Quraysh were asking him to describe contemporary Jerusalem as he saw it that very night. How could he have counted the doors of a building that no longer existed?"

Gilchrist continues: 'The whole story of the Mi’raj as found in the Hadith may well be a pure fiction... It is probable that what was originally nothing more than a dream of a journey to Jerusalem has been transformed into an actual physical event... Although Muhammad obviously knew of the destruction of the second Temple, it seems he believed that it had been rebuilt like the first one... It seems appropriate to conclude that the experience Muhammad had was really only a dream which characterised his illusions about Jerusalem, and that the whole story of the Mi’raj is accordingly nothing more than a mythical fantasy imaginatively built upon it.'

This assessment, grounded in the very sources Muslims revere, brings the argument to its logical climax.

# Section 8: The Unbreakable Scholarly Consensus — Physical Building, Not Metaphor
The attempt by some modern apologists to reinterpret al-Masjid al-Aqsa as merely a 'site' or 'holy land' rather than a physical standing building collapses under the weight of Islamic scholarship itself

The most important classical scholars — from the Companions onward — understood the verse
as referring to a real, physical sanctuary. Ibn Abbas, the greatest exegete among the Sahaba, and
later giants like Mujahid, Qatada, and Ibn Kathir all affirmed that Muhammad was taken to the
actual physical location in Jerusalem known as Bayt al-Maqdis / al-Masjid al-Aqsa. They did not
spiritualize it away

Crucially, the Salafi and Athari tradition — which prides itself on strict adherence to the literal
text and rejection of metaphorical reinterpretation (ta'wil) in matters of belief — categorically
rejects any attempt to turn the Isra and Mi'raj into a mere dream or spiritual vision. The Salaf
affirmed the bodily Isra to Jerusalem and the subsequent Mi'raj. To claim it was metaphorical is
to contradict the very methodology of the Salaf that many modern Muslims claim to follow.
This is the final nail: Even if one tries to escape the historical problem by saying 'it was only the
site,' the earliest and most authoritative scholars — including those the Salafis revere most —
said it was a physical building. And the Salafis themselves reject the metaphorical escape hatch.
Therefore, the original problem remains: Muhammad described a standing physical mosque in
Jerusalem in 621 CE when none existed.
There is no contradiction among the scholars on this point — they all point to a physical reality.
The only 'contradiction' is between those scholars and the actual history of Jerusalem. That
contradiction exposes the fraud.
# Conclusion: The Cumulative Evidence Exposes Muhammad as a Fraud

We have now examined the evidence layer by layer:

- Historical fact: No Temple or mosque existed in Jerusalem in 621 CE.
    
- Quranic language: al-Masjid al-Aqsa is presented as a real, existing parallel to the physical Kaaba mosque.
    
- Earliest tafsirs: Unanimous identification with a physical sanctuary in Jerusalem.
    
- Hadith literature: Detailed descriptions of Muhammad observing and counting features of a standing building.
    
- Muslim scholars’ own admissions: Repeated references to al-Aqsa as a physical mosque, even while attempting to soften the implications.
    
- Failed biblical parallel: The Quran lacks the contextual qualifiers that would allow a 'site only' interpretation.
    
- Expert analysis: The narrative bears all the marks of later myth-making based on illusion rather than reality.
    

The only coherent explanation is that Muhammad — or the later framers of the tradition — erroneously believed or claimed that a physical 'Farthest Mosque' stood in Jerusalem when none did. This is not a minor discrepancy. It is a foundational claim of Islamic prophethood that collapses under historical and textual scrutiny.

As Shamoun concludes: 'As the evidence I have presented has proven, masjid al-aqsa can only refer to a physical building, one which did not exist at the time of Muhammad. As such, this is a blunder that will not go away, and which exposes Muhammad as a fraud and a false prophet.'

The case is closed. The nonexistent temple stands as one of the clearest internal contradictions in the Islamic narrative — one that no amount of later reinterpretation can erase.
