**Before (651–762 CE range):** The earliest Chinese descriptions of the Arab movement describe it as a political-religious community with laws and customs. Du Huan, who literally lived among them for eleven years in the 750s, describes dietary rules, a weekly holiday, worship of heaven alone, and martial culture. He categorizes it as "the confession of the Arab" — a _confessional identity_, like he categorizes Christianity as "the confession of the Byzantines." He doesn't mention a founding prophet, a revelation in a cave, a migration from Mecca to Medina, or any biographical narrative. And this is someone who was _embedded_ in the Abbasid heartland for over a decade.

**After (late 8th century onward):** As Abbasid diplomatic contact with China intensifies and the Abbasid state consolidates its own religious identity, the Chinese sources begin reflecting the prophetic narrative — because that's what the Abbasids are now _telling_ foreign courts about themselves.

**Why this matters for the argument:**

If Muhammad's biography — the cave, the revelation, the hijra, the battles, the death in 632 — was the founding story of the movement from the beginning, you would expect _every_ external observer to mention it. It's the kind of thing you lead with. When Christian envoys showed up anywhere in the ancient world, they talked about Christ. When Buddhist missionaries arrived in China centuries earlier, they talked about the Buddha. A founding prophet is not a minor detail you forget to mention — it's the _entire point_.

Du Huan spent eleven years in the Abbasid Caliphate in the 750s and either didn't hear a prophetic biography or didn't consider it important enough to record. Either option is devastating for the SIN. 

**If he didn't hear it**, that suggests the biography wasn't yet the central organizing narrative of the movement — which supports the thesis that it was still being constructed. If he heard it but didn't record it, that's harder to explain but still suggests it wasn't the dominant feature of how the community presented itself.

Now compare this with what happens a few decades later: the Abbasids are fully in power, the Sira literature is being written (Ibn Ishaq died 767, Ibn Hisham edited his work in the early 800s), and the hadith collections are being compiled. _This_ is when the biographical narrative becomes the movement's official self-description. And _this_ is when Chinese sources start reflecting it — because the Abbasid state is now _exporting_ that narrative through diplomatic channels.

The timeline correlation is the point. 

***The prophetic biography doesn't appear in external sources until the same period when it's being _composed_ in internal sources.**

That's not what you'd expect if the biography was historical memory being faithfully transmitted. That's what you'd expect if the biography was being _constructed_ as a political-theological project and then projected outward.

So the Chinese evidence doesn't prove the biography is fabricated. What it does is remove the assumption that "everyone always knew about Muhammad the Prophet from the beginning." It shows that there was a window — roughly 651 to the late 700s — where the movement existed, expanded, conquered, governed, and interacted with foreign powers _without_ the prophetic biography being its calling card. That window is exactly the gap the redaction thesis needs to be credible.
