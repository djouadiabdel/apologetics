# The MHMD Thesis: A Cumulative Case Against the Standard Islamic Narrative

---

## Methodological Framework

This argument uses abductive reasoning — inference to the best explanation. It does not start with a conclusion and prove it deductively. It starts with a pool of evidence from independent sources, generates competing hypotheses, and asks which hypothesis accounts for the most evidence with the fewest ad hoc assumptions. The hypothesis that does that wins — not as certainty, but as the most reasonable explanation given what we have.

The argument applies five evaluative criteria:

**Feasibility.** Does the hypothesis require anything physically or historically impossible?

**Simplicity.** Which hypothesis requires fewer auxiliary assumptions?

**Exhaustiveness.** Which hypothesis accounts for more of the data?

**Consistency.** Does the hypothesis contradict itself?

**Superiority.** Is there a third hypothesis that does better than both?

These are the same criteria historians use to evaluate competing reconstructions of any ancient period. The only novelty here is applying them to the origins of Islam — a field that has been largely exempt from the historical-critical scrutiny applied to Christianity for over two centuries.

---

## The Core Problem

When you apply the same evidentiary standards that historians apply to any other ancient figure — privileging contemporary sources over later literary traditions, privileging material evidence over narrative accounts, privileging epigraphy and numismatics over hagiography — what emerges from the 7th-century evidence does not match the figure described in the 9th-century biography.

The argument runs on three levels.

**What the early sources show.** A Messianic political-military movement using the title MHMD ("The Praised One") alongside Christian symbols, led by figures who behave like Christian sectarians — praying at Golgotha, retaining crosses on state currency, using Christological vocabulary. No contemporary source — not a single inscription, coin, chronicle, or foreign report from the actual 7th century — describes an Arabian prophet who received a book in a cave near Mecca and founded a new religion.

**What the late sources show.** Starting around 750–800 AD, a fully formed biographical narrative appears: birth in Mecca, revelation in a cave, migration to Medina, military campaigns, death in 632. This narrative is compiled by authors writing under Abbasid political patronage, 150–200 years after the alleged events, and it involves a 98% filtration of available oral reports.

**The gap between the two.** The 7th-century evidence and the 9th-century biography are not just incomplete versions of the same story — they are incompatible. The early evidence points to a Christological title used by a sectarian coalition. The late evidence points to a singular Arabian prophet founding a distinct religion. The standard narrative asks you to trust the late sources and ignore the early ones. The question is: why would you do that for Islam when you would never do it for any other historical claim?

The analogy: if we found 1st-century Roman coins with "CHRISTVS" inscribed next to Jupiter symbols, and then the first biography of Christ only appeared in the 3rd century written by imperial scribes, every historian alive would say "the biographical narrative is a later political construction layered onto an earlier syncretic movement." That is precisely the evidential situation with Islam — and precisely the conclusion that the evidence supports.

## Section 1: Philological Foundations — The Pre-Islamic Origins of the Title

### Core Thesis

The word "Muhammad" entered the 7th-century Arabian milieu not as a proper name but as a pre-existing Messianic title with a traceable Semitic lineage spanning two millennia. The argument is not that no historical figure existed, but that the name itself functioned as a descriptor before it was retroactively anchored to a biography.

### Evidence Chain

The Semitic root H-M-D is attested as early as 1400 BC in Ugaritic texts, where it describes mineralogical desirability — precious metals, gold, silver. This root is traced in Volker Popp's study "From Ugarit to Samarra" in the volume _Early Islam: A Critical Reconstruction Based on Contemporary Sources_ (ed. Ohlig, Prometheus Books, 2013).

The root migrated into Biblical Hebrew with the same semantic range. Song of Solomon 5:16 uses the plural form _mahmadim_ ("altogether lovely/desirable") as a descriptor of the beloved. For roughly 700 years before the emergence of Islam, Jewish and Christian exegetical traditions uniformly applied this term to Solomon or to Christ — never to an anticipated Arabian prophet.

The critical bridge is patristic exegesis. In the 4th century, Church Fathers including Ambrose of Milan commented on Song of Solomon and glossed the H-M-D root — rendered in Latin as _desiderabilis_ — as a Christological descriptor. This was not idiosyncratic. It reflected a broader Near Eastern tradition of using H-M-D derivatives as Messianic honorifics applied to Christ. The connection is at the root level: the same Semitic root that later becomes the Arabic name/title "Muhammad" was applied to Jesus in patristic exegesis through its Hebrew cognate. By the 7th century, any literate actor in the Syro-Palestinian religious landscape would have encountered H-M-D-derived descriptors as Christological vocabulary before encountering "Muhammad" as a personal name.

Karl-Heinz Ohlig develops this argument in detail in his chapter "From muhammad Jesus to Prophet of the Arabs — the Personalization of a Christological Epithet" (in _Early Islam_, ed. Ohlig, 2013), tracing how _muhammad_ functioned as a Christological epithet parallel to the Latin _Benedictus qui venit_ ("Blessed is he who comes") before being personalized into a prophetic biography.

### Distinguishing Criterion: Name vs. Title

A fair objection is that many Semitic names derive from honorific roots without functioning as titles. Every "Abd-Allah" is not a "title in disguise." The criterion that separates the Muhammad case is threefold.

First, there is virtually no contemporary pre-Islamic attestation of "Muhammad" as a common personal name in the Hijaz — it appears suddenly and exclusively in religious-political contexts. The handful of pre-Islamic "Muhammads" in Arabic genealogies are preserved almost entirely in sources compiled in the 9th century or later (notably Ibn Habib's _Kitab al-Muhabbar_), making them part of the same literary tradition they are supposed to independently confirm.

Second, the earliest material evidence (coins, inscriptions) pairs MHMD with Christological symbols rather than biographical markers.

Third, the Quran itself uses "Muhammad" only four times, and in each case the grammatical and theological context is more consistent with a title than a name (developed in Section 3).

### Strongest Objection and Response

**Objection:** The name Muhammad does appear in pre-Islamic Arabic poetry and genealogies, suggesting it was a known personal name.

**Response:** The pre-Islamic attestations are almost entirely preserved in sources compiled in the 9th century or later — the same period that produced the Sira. The circularity problem applies: the sources that confirm "Muhammad" as a pre-Islamic name are the same sources that construct the prophetic biography. No contemporary 6th-century inscription or document independently attests "Muhammad" as a personal name in the Hijaz. Some scholars (notably Christian Robin) have argued for South Arabian epigraphic evidence, but these cases remain disputed and do not establish common usage in the Hijazi context that the standard narrative requires.

## Section 2: The Silent Stones — 7th-Century Material Evidence

### Core Thesis

When we privilege contemporary material evidence — coins, inscriptions, chronicles — over literary traditions composed 150–200 years later, the 7th-century movement associated with "MHMD" appears not as a new religion but as an anti-Trinitarian movement operating within a broadly Christian framework.

### Evidence Chain

**The Coins**

Two distinct series of coins are relevant, and they must be distinguished carefully because they come from different regions and carry different designs.

_Arab-Sassanian coins (eastern territories — Iraq, Iran):_ These coins imitate the Sassanian silver drachm template. They feature a bust derived from the Sassanian emperor Khusrau II on the obverse and a Zoroastrian fire altar with attendants on the reverse. Under Muawiya (r. 661–680), the name "Maawia" appears in Middle Persian (Pahlavi) script with the title _amir i-wruishnikan_ ("Commander of the Believers"), alongside the Arabic marginal inscription _bism Allah_ ("In the name of God"). These coins are catalogued in J. Walker's _A Catalogue of the Muhammadan Coins in the British Museum, Volume I — Arab-Sassanian Coins_ (1941) and are held in major collections including the British Museum. They contain no crosses, no shahada, and no mention of Muhammad — only _bism Allah_ and the caliph's name.

_Arab-Byzantine coins (western territories — Syria, Palestine):_ These coins imitate Byzantine prototypes and retain much of the original Christian imagery, including crosses. The Maronite Chronicle (c. 664 AD) records that Muawiya minted gold and silver coins that were rejected by the Syrian population because they omitted the cross — implying that crosses were expected and standard on coins circulating in the region. Many Arab-Byzantine coins from this period are anonymous and lack Arabic inscriptions entirely, though some do combine Arabic formulae with modified Byzantine designs. Clive Foss's work on Arab-Byzantine coinage and Stefan Heidemann's analysis of the Standing Caliph series are the key scholarly references.

The theological problem emerges from the combined picture. In the eastern territories, the caliph retains Zoroastrian imagery (fire altars) on state currency. In the western territories, crosses persist on coinage for decades. The earliest coins bearing MHMD-related inscriptions pair them with either Zoroastrian or Christian visual language. In neither case does the coinage reflect a distinct Islamic identity. The first explicit Islamic confessional content on coins — the shahada — does not appear until Abd al-Malik's reforms around 691–696 AD, roughly sixty years after the traditional date of Muhammad's death.

If Islam already existed as a fully formed, distinct religion in the 660s, the absence of Islamic content on state currency for three decades requires explanation. The standard response — pragmatic economic continuity — has force for the first few years of transitional coinage, but it collapses over time. Pragmatic continuity explains why you might keep a Byzantine design for a year or two after conquest. It does not explain why you would actively mint new dies retaining foreign religious symbols for thirty years — unless those symbols were not foreign to you.

**The Maronite Chronicle (c. 664 AD)**

This anonymous Syriac chronicle, written shortly after 664 AD by a Maronite author, is one of the most important near-contemporary sources for the early Umayyad period. It survives in a single damaged manuscript (British Library Add. 17,216). The chronicle correctly names the days of the week for particular dates, strongly suggesting that its entries were written close to the events described.

The chronicle records that in 660 AD, many Arabs gathered at Jerusalem and made Muawiya king. He then went up and sat on Golgotha — the site of Christ's crucifixion — and prayed there. He then went to Gethsemane and went down to the tomb of the Blessed Mary to pray in it.

These are the actions of a Christian sectarian leader, not a Muslim caliph as later tradition would define one. The received text of the Quran explicitly rejects the reality of the crucifixion (Q. 4:157). What meaning could Golgotha — the place of Christ's crucifixion — hold for the leader of a community that denied the crucifixion occurred? The simplest explanation is that Muawiya's community had not yet adopted that denial.

Mainstream scholars have accepted this report as historically likely. Jack Tannous notes that the chronicle was written only a few years after the events, and James Howard-Johnston judges it as well-informed regarding developments in the leadership of the early movement. Stephen Shoemaker's recent work has used this and similar evidence to argue that "Jerusalem and the biblical Holy Land seem to have been the primary focus of the Believers' sacred geography, holding far greater significance than Mecca and Medina in the Hijaz."

**The Hammat Gader Inscription (663 AD)**

A Greek inscription discovered at the hot springs of Hammat Gader near the Sea of Galilee, dated to 663 AD. It refers to the caliph as "Abd Allah Mu'awiya, amir al-mu'minin" ("God's Servant Mu'awiya, Commander of the Faithful"). The caliph's name is preceded by a cross. The inscription is in Greek, not Arabic, uses no Islamic religious vocabulary, does not mention Muhammad or the Quran, and describes the era simply as that of "the Arabs" — not the Muslims.

**The Inscriptional Silence**

Muawiya was the first caliph whose name appeared on coins, inscriptions, or documents of the nascent empire. The inscriptions from his reign lack any explicit reference to Islam or Muhammad. The only titles that appear are "servant of God" and "commander of the faithful." This has led several modern historians to question the nature of the early movement's religious identity. The historian Robert Hoyland, while not a revisionist, notes that the evidence is consistent with a movement that had not yet fully differentiated itself from the broader Abrahamic landscape.

### Addressing the Pragmatic Pluralism Objection

**Objection:** Early Umayyad rulers retained Byzantine and Sassanian coin designs for economic continuity — to maintain trade confidence in newly conquered territories. The Christian and Zoroastrian symbols are pragmatic, not theological.

**Response:** This objection has force for the first few years of transitional coinage, but it becomes increasingly strained over time. The retention of crosses and fire altars persists for nearly three decades (661–691 AD). Furthermore, when Abd al-Malik finally reformed the coinage around 696 AD, the removal of the cross coincides precisely with the construction of the Dome of the Rock and its explicitly anti-Trinitarian inscriptions. The reform marks the beginning of differentiation from Christianity, not the continuation of an already-distinct Islamic identity. The Maronite Chronicle itself notes that Muawiya's coins without crosses were rejected — meaning the pressure was to include crosses, not to remove them. This is the opposite of what you would expect if the ruling community was already Muslim in the later sense.

## Section 3: The Four Quranic Muhammads

### Core Thesis

The Arabic Quran contains exactly four instances of the word "Muhammad." When analyzed through exegesis (drawing meaning from the text) rather than eisegesis (projecting the 9th-century Sira onto the text), all four are at minimum equally consistent with a Christological referent as with an Arabian biographical one.

### Methodological Note

The claim here is not that these verses prove Muhammad = Jesus. The claim is that these verses are at minimum equally consistent with a Christological reading, and that the biographical reading depends entirely on external sources (Sira, Hadith) composed 150–200 years after the events they describe. If the external sources are themselves the product of redaction (Section 5), then the Quranic text alone cannot establish the biographical reading.

### Verse-by-Verse Analysis

**Surah 3:144** — "Muhammad is not but a messenger. Other messengers have passed on before him."

Now compare with **Surah 5:75** — "The Messiah, son of Mary, is not but a messenger. Other messengers have passed on before him."

The structural parallelism is exact. Same grammatical construction (_ma ... illa rasul_). Same theological content (correcting an exaggerated view of the figure). Same rhetorical function (emphasizing the figure's mortality and continuity with prior messengers).

This is the single strongest textual observation in the argument. If two titles receive identical Quranic descriptions using identical corrective formulas, the simplest explanation is that they describe the same referent under two names: "The Messiah" (_al-Masih_) and "The Praised One" (_Muhammad_). The alternative — that the Quran coincidentally uses the exact same corrective formula for two entirely different figures — requires an explanation that the standard narrative has never provided.

**Surah 47:2** — "Those who believe and do righteous deeds and believe in what has been sent down upon Muhammad... He will remove from them their misdeeds."

The operative theological function attributed to the Muhammad figure here is the mediation of forgiveness. The verse does not say Muhammad himself forgives — it says belief in what was sent down upon him results in God removing misdeeds. This mediating structure is precisely the Gospel claim about Jesus: that faith in his revelation results in divine forgiveness (cf. Matthew 4:17, Acts 10:43). The question is whether this mediating function is more naturally attributed to a 7th-century political-military leader or to the figure who, across the entire Near Eastern religious landscape, was already associated with exactly this function.

**Surah 48:29** — "Muhammad is the Messenger of God; and those with him are forceful against the disbelievers, merciful among themselves... their mark is on their faces from the trace of prostration. That is their description in the Torah and their description in the Gospel — like a seed which sends forth its shoot..."

This verse explicitly states that the description of "those with Muhammad" is found in the Torah and in the Gospel. It then uses agricultural imagery — a seed sending forth its shoot, strengthening, standing firm on its stem — that resonates with Gospel teaching, particularly the Parable of the Sower and related agricultural parables in the Synoptics. If the Quran is citing Gospel language to describe the companions of its "Muhammad" figure, the most parsimonious reading is that the Muhammad figure is connected to the teacher of that tradition — Christ. The standard reading, which requires a 7th-century Arabian prophet's followers to be described in a 1st-century Gospel, demands an explanation for how that description got into the Gospel centuries before the prophet allegedly existed.

**Surah 33:40** — "Muhammad is not the father of any of your men, but the Messenger of God and the Seal of the Prophets."

The standard narrative reads this as a reference to the Prophet's lack of surviving male heirs. A Christological reading notes that Jesus was celibate and fatherless-of-men by definition. The stronger textual argument is the theological content: "Seal of the Prophets" (_Khatam an-Nabiyyin_) maps directly onto the Christological claim of finality — that Christ is the fulfillment and closure of the prophetic tradition. This is standard Christian theology (cf. Hebrews 1:1-2: "In the past God spoke to our ancestors through the prophets at many times and in various ways, but in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son"), not a novel Islamic claim. That said, this is the weakest of the four parallels and should be treated as supporting rather than primary evidence.

## Section 4: The Political Context — Coalition, Conquest, and the MHMD Title

### Core Thesis

The 614 AD Persian-Jewish-Arab conquest of Jerusalem created the political conditions in which a shared Messianic vocabulary — including the MHMD title — became strategically useful. The early movement originated not as a new religion but as a Jewish-Arab military-theological coalition against Byzantium.

### Evidence Chain

The historical kernel is well-attested: in 614 AD, the Sasanian Empire, allied with Jewish militias and Arab tribal forces, conquered Jerusalem from the Byzantines. The Jewish Exilarchs — Nehemiah ben Hushiel and his successor — played a leadership role. This is attested in near-contemporary sources including the Strategius account and the Sefer Zerubbabel.

The Doctrina Jacobi (a Greek anti-Jewish dialogue written c. 634 AD) describes a "prophet" who had appeared among the Saracens, who came "with the keys of paradise" and preached the coming of the "anointed one" — i.e., the Messiah/Christ. This is one of the earliest external references to the movement, and it describes a Messianic movement, not a new religion with its own distinct prophet.

Fred Donner's work (_Muhammad and the Believers_, Harvard University Press, 2010), while not revisionist in the Ohlig/Popp sense, argues that the early movement was an ecumenical "Believers' movement" that included Jews and Christians alongside Arabs, and only gradually differentiated into a distinct religion. This mainstream scholarly position independently supports the coalition model — the early movement was not initially the exclusive, distinct Islam of later centuries.

### The Chinese Source

The Jiu Tang Shu (Old Book of Tang), compiled around 945 CE, records that an embassy from the "Dashi" (Arabs) arrived at the Tang court in 651 CE. The standard reading identifies this as an embassy from Caliph Uthman. Some revisionist scholars (notably in the Inârah group) have proposed alternative readings of the Chinese terminology that describe the movement's leadership in terms that align with a Persian or Exilarchic connection. However, this must be stated honestly: the Jiu Tang Shu was compiled nearly 300 years after the embassy it describes, and the revisionist reading of the Chinese text is an interpretation, not the plain meaning. The standard reading — an Arab embassy from the Caliph — is the more straightforward one. The revisionist reading gains whatever plausibility it has from the convergent evidence in other sources, not from the Chinese text alone.

### Honest Limitation

This section is the most speculative in the argument. Its function is to provide a plausible political mechanism for how a Christological title became attached to a military-political movement. It does not stand alone — it depends on Sections 1–3 establishing that the title preceded the biography, and on Section 5 establishing that the biography was a later construction.

## Section 5: The Great Redaction — From Messianic Title to Arabian Prophet

### Core Thesis

The Abbasid Revolution of 750 AD created the political need for a distinct prophetic identity. The transformation of "Muhammad" from a Christological title to an Arabian biographical name was not a conspiracy but a predictable imperial standardization process — the same kind of narrative consolidation that every empire in history has performed.

### The Timeline

This is the primary evidence. Lay the dates side by side:

**632 AD** — Traditional date of Muhammad's death.

**661–680 AD** — Muawiya's caliphate. No mention of Muhammad on any inscription or coin. Crosses on coins. Prayers at Golgotha. Greek inscriptions with crosses preceding the caliph's name. The era described as "the era of the Arabs," not the Muslims.

**685–705 AD** — Abd al-Malik's caliphate. The word MHMD first appears on coins, initially in Pahlavi, then in Arabic. The shahada appears on coinage for the first time around 691–692 AD. The Dome of the Rock (691 AD) contains anti-Trinitarian inscriptions that argue about Jesus — they are Christological in vocabulary even as they deny the Trinity. This marks the beginning of differentiation from Christianity.

**696–698 AD** — Abd al-Malik's full coinage reform. All figural imagery removed. Quranic inscriptions replace Christian and Zoroastrian symbols. The fully aniconic Islamic dinar, dirham, and fals are introduced. This is the moment when the numismatic record begins to look like what we would recognize as "Islamic."

**750 AD** — The Abbasid Revolution. The Umayyads are overthrown. The new dynasty needs to delegitimize everything that came before while anchoring its own authority in a clean origin story traced back to the Prophet's family.

**c. 760s AD** — Ibn Ishaq composes the earliest known Sira (prophetic biography). It does not survive independently — it is preserved only through the later recension of Ibn Hisham (d. 833 AD).

**c. 830–870 AD** — The hadith collections are compiled and canonized. Al-Bukhari reportedly evaluates approximately 600,000 circulating oral reports and retains roughly 7,275 (including repetitions). Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj produces his own collection in the same period. This is the moment when the biographical tradition gets locked in and everything else gets discarded permanently.

### What the Timeline Shows

Between the death of the prophet (632, per the standard narrative) and the first biography (760s), there is a gap of 130 years. Between the death and the canonization of the hadith corpus (830–870), the gap is 200–240 years. During the first 60 years of this gap, the material evidence shows no distinctly Islamic identity on coins, inscriptions, or foreign reports. During the next 40 years (Abd al-Malik's period), an Islamic identity begins to emerge on state media but in a form that is still arguing with Christianity, not independent of it. Only after the Abbasid Revolution does a fully formed biographical narrative appear — written under the patronage of a dynasty that had just seized power by violence and needed a founding myth.

### The Hadith Filtration

The 98% filtration is a structural fact, not a conspiracy theory. Whether al-Bukhari was an honest scholar or a political operative, the structural outcome is identical: the surviving corpus represents a radically curated sample, and the 98%+ that was discarded is permanently inaccessible. Any historian working with a dataset that has undergone 98% attrition should treat the surviving material with extreme caution rather than as a transparent window onto the past.

Consider what this means in practice. If 600,000 reports were circulating in the 9th century, it means that the oral tradition had expanded enormously over two centuries — a predictable result of a living oral culture generating new material to meet new needs. The filtration did not simply remove "weak" reports. It selected one version of the past and permanently deleted all competitors. The reports that didn't make the cut — the 592,725 that were discarded — included not just fabrications but also alternative memories, competing narratives, and inconvenient traditions. We will never know what they contained.

## Section 6: The Cumulative Case

The argument does not rest on any single piece of evidence. It is a cumulative case built on convergent lines from independent disciplines.

**Philology** establishes that the H-M-D root functioned as a Messianic descriptor for centuries before Islam, and that patristic tradition applied it to Christ.

**Numismatics** establishes that 7th-century authorities paired Arabic monotheistic inscriptions with Christian and Zoroastrian imagery on state currency for decades, and that distinctly Islamic confessional content does not appear on coins until the 690s — sixty years after Muhammad's traditional death.

**Epigraphy** establishes that the earliest inscriptions mentioning Muawiya contain no Islamic content, begin with crosses, and describe the era as that of "the Arabs," not the Muslims.

**Contemporary chronicles** establish that the first Umayyad caliph prayed at Christian holy sites and that the earliest external descriptions of the movement describe it in Messianic, not Islamic, terms.

**Quranic textual analysis** establishes that the four internal uses of "Muhammad" are at minimum equally consistent with a Christological referent, and that the structural parallel between 3:144 and 5:75 is a fact that demands explanation.

**Source criticism** establishes that the biographical narrative was consolidated 150–200 years after the events it describes, under political conditions that incentivized the creation of a distinct prophetic identity, and that the hadith corpus underwent a 98%+ attrition that permanently eliminated competing traditions.

Each line of evidence independently raises questions. Together, they constitute a coherent alternative model: "Muhammad" began as a Christological title, not a personal name, and the Arabian prophetic biography was a product of 8th–9th century imperial consolidation, not 7th-century historical memory.

## Applying the Five Criteria

**Feasibility.** Does the Christological-title hypothesis require anything historically impossible? No. Messianic titles migrating across religious communities is well-documented behavior in the ancient Near East. Titles becoming names is a common linguistic phenomenon. Imperial narrative construction is universal in ancient history.

**Simplicity.** The Standard Islamic Narrative (SIN) requires you to assume that the 9th-century sources accurately preserved 7th-century events despite 150–200 years of oral transmission, despite a violent dynastic revolution in between, despite 98% of the source material being discarded, and despite the contemporary material evidence failing to confirm the later narrative. The title hypothesis requires you to assume one thing — that a known Messianic title continued functioning as a title until it was personalized into a biography.

**Exhaustiveness.** The SIN cannot explain: why Muawiya prayed at Golgotha; why coins retained crosses for thirty years; why the earliest inscriptions contain no Islamic content; why the Quran uses the same corrective formula for "Muhammad" and "al-Masih"; why the earliest external sources describe a Messianic movement rather than a new religion. The title hypothesis accounts for all of these naturally.

**Consistency.** The SIN requires a Muslim caliph minting crosses on state currency, praying at the site of a crucifixion his religion denies, and being identified by a cross on a Greek inscription — for thirty years. This is internally strained at minimum. The title hypothesis has no internal contradictions.

**Superiority.** The only serious competitor is the "pragmatic pluralism" reading — that early rulers simply didn't care about religious symbols on coins and prayed at holy sites out of political calculation. This explanation works for any individual piece of evidence but becomes increasingly strained as evidence accumulates. Pragmatic tolerance does not explain why you would actively mint new dies combining your own religious inscription with a foreign religious symbol for decades. It does not explain why the removal of crosses coincides precisely with the emergence of anti-Trinitarian inscriptions (Dome of the Rock, 691 AD). And it does not address the Quranic textual evidence at all.

## What This Argument Does and Does Not Claim

**It does not claim** that no historical figure led the early movement.

**It does not claim** a grand conspiracy.

**It does not claim** certainty.

**It does not claim** to prove Christianity true.

**It claims** that the Standard Islamic Narrative, when tested against contemporary material evidence rather than later literary traditions, fails to account for the data — and that a Christological-title model accounts for more of the evidence with fewer ad hoc assumptions.

**It claims** that the evidentiary foundation for the biography of Muhammad is weaker than the evidentiary foundation for any other major religious founder, and that applying the same historical-critical standards that have been applied to Christianity for 250 years renders the standard narrative untenable as established fact.

**It claims** that the 7th-century evidence is more consistent with a Christological movement that was later repackaged than with a new religion that was fully formed from the beginning.

New evidence could shift the balance. A 7th-century inscription from Mecca clearly naming Muhammad as a prophet who founded a new religion would seriously damage this thesis. The fact that no such inscription has been found despite decades of searching is itself part of the evidence — but absence of evidence, while evidentially relevant, is not the same as impossibility.
