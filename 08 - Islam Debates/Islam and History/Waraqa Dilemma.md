# The Progress Before and After Christ - Talmud:

Segal (who was Jewish, not Christian, which matters for credibility) documented that the rabbinic "two powers in heaven" heresy — the idea that there are two divine authorities — was something the rabbis explicitly condemned. But the whole point is they were condemning something that _already existed within Judaism_. 
They weren't fighting a phantom. They were fighting an established interpretive tradition that read figures like the Angel of the Lord, the Son of Man in Daniel 7, and the enthroned Wisdom/Logos as a second divine power alongside God. Segal traces this "heresy" back to pre-Christian Jewish exegesis.

Why did the rabbis push back? Almost certainly because Christians were using these exact texts and traditions to argue for Christ's divinity. 

So the Talmudic-era rabbis had a theological motivation to _flatten_ the Angel of the Lord into a created being. But their own polemic reveals that the earlier reading was different.

**Pre-rabbinic Jewish sources that support the divine intermediary**

Philo of Alexandria (first century, Jewish, pre-Christian or contemporary with earliest Christianity) speaks of the Logos as a "second God," as an archangel, and explicitly connects this figure to the Angel who appeared to the patriarchs. Philo isn't Christian. He has no Trinitarian agenda. He's reading his Jewish scriptures and arriving at a divine intermediary figure.

The Targumim (Aramaic paraphrases of the Hebrew Bible used in synagogues) do something remarkable. Where the Hebrew text says "the Angel of the Lord" or describes God appearing visibly, the Targums frequently substitute "the Memra (Word) of the Lord." The Memra creates, reveals, saves, and mediates — it functions as a divine intermediary distinct from the Father yet fully divine. Targum Onkelos, Targum Neofiti, and Targum Pseudo-Jonathan all do this. These aren't Christian documents.
