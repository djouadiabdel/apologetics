_the Sharia installs the removal of the transgressor as permanent divine order, where the Gospel exposes and ends the community's right to purify itself through the transgressor's removal._ That's the locked claim. It doesn't require you to call anyone a murderer, it doesn't collapse under "but that's justice," and it keeps you on the trajectory-and-self-critique ground where the argument actually holds.

---
The scapegoat mechanism is: _a community under stress restores its unity by turning on a victim, and then tells itself the victim deserved it._

A myth _perfects the secret_ — it hides the mechanism so well the community never knows it's doing it.

The Gospel _breaks it_ — it says out loud, "we turned on an innocent man," and the spell stops working.

The Sharia, in Girard's structural terms, does a third thing: it _keeps the mechanism running, but makes it legal, permanent, and holy._ It doesn't hide the victim like myth, and it doesn't expose the victim like the Gospel. It _codifies_ the act of removing the transgressor and writes it into divine law forever. The thief's hand, the apostate's penalty, the law of retaliation — the community still discharges its disorder onto a designated body, but now it's not a hidden mob impulse and not an exposed injustice; it's _commanded by God._ That's what "perfects" means here — not "makes better," but "completes and installs permanently." The mechanism is no longer a thing that happens _to_ the community in a crisis. It's the standing legal order, sanctified.

Plainest version for you: _Myth hides the scapegoating. The Gospel exposes it. The Sharia legalizes it._

**What's missing — and it's the whole hinge:** you wrote "the Gospel ends the _meaning_ of a transgressor." That's not quite it, and the slip matters. The Gospel does not abolish sin, or transgression, or the reality that people do wrong. It doesn't say there are no sinners. What it ends is **the use of the sinner as the instrument of the community's purity.** Let me draw the exact difference.

In every scapegoat system — mob, myth, or sacred law — the logic is: _the community is made clean by expelling the bad one._ Purity flows from removal. We are righteous _because_ we cut off the transgressor. The sinner's destruction is what restores us. The victim is the drain through which the community's disorder is flushed out. That's the mechanism, whether it's a stoning mob or a court sentence "commanded by God." The community's holiness is _purchased_ by the victim's removal.

Now watch what the Gospel does. It doesn't deny the sin. It relocates where purity comes from. Purity no longer flows from _expelling_ the sinner — it flows from the victim _absorbing_ the violence without passing it on. Christ doesn't restore the community by being the one they successfully cast out and feel clean about. He restores it by being cast out and _not returning the accusation_ — "Father, forgive them." The cycle that needs a victim to discharge disorder is broken because the victim refuses to play the part of the drain _and_ refuses to become the next accuser.

So here's the exact thing you were missing:

- The sacred-law system **keeps the mechanism and changes the operator** — from mob to God-commanded court. The sinner is still the instrument of the community's purification. Removing him is still how the group stays clean.
- The Gospel **keeps the sinner and removes the mechanism** — it stops using the sinner's expulsion as the source of communal purity. Sin is still real, the sinner still exists, but he is no longer the sacrificial drain. The famous scene is John 8 — the woman caught in adultery. The Law says stone her; that's the mechanism, the community purified by removing the transgressor. Jesus doesn't say "she didn't sin." He says "let him who is without sin throw the first stone" — which _dissolves the mob_ by turning the accusation back on each accuser, and then "neither do I condemn you; go and sin no more." Sin is named (go and _sin_ no more — it's real). But the scapegoat machine is disarmed. The crowd disperses. Nobody gets to become clean by killing her.

That's the line you were reaching for. Not "the Gospel ends transgressors." It's: _the Gospel ends the community's right to purify itself by destroying the transgressor._ Sin stays. The sacrificial use of the sinner ends.

So the contrast, exact:

- Sacred law: _we become holy by removing the sinner, and God commands the removal._
- Gospel: _the sinner remains, and we are forbidden the holiness that comes from removing him; the only one who could throw the stone — the sinless one — refuses to._

One feeds the mechanism by sanctifying it. The other starves the mechanism by removing its food, which was always the community's appetite to be made clean by someone else's blood.
