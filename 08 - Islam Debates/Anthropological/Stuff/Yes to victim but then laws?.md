  

**Half one: the Quran's stance toward the victim.** I gave you this last message — it sides with the victim but vindicates through violence rather than breaking it. That stands. But your second question goes somewhere deeper and more interesting.

**Half two: the Law itself.** Here's what Girard actually says, and it's not what you'd expect. In _Violence and the Sacred_ and _Things Hidden_, the entire apparatus of archaic religion — the prohibitions, the ritual repetitions, the dietary rules, the purity codes, the sacrificial calendar — is not arbitrary and it's not evil. It's _protective._ It exists to keep the community from sliding back into the mimetic crisis that the founding murder once resolved. Prohibitions fence off the objects and behaviors that trigger rivalry. Rituals re-enact the original scapegoating in controlled, substitute form so the real violence doesn't return. The Law is the scar tissue around the founding wound. It's the mechanism's _maintenance system._

Now hold that against the two texts.

The **Torah** is full of this — purity codes, sacrifice, the scapegoat ritual of Leviticus 16 literally named (the goat for Azazel, sent into the wilderness bearing the people's sin). Girard's claim is that the Hebrew Bible _contains_ the sacrificial system but is simultaneously _cracking it open from inside_ — the prophets keep attacking sacrifice (Hosea 6:6, "I desire mercy and not sacrifice"; Isaiah 1, "I am full of the burnt offerings"; Psalm 51, "you take no delight in sacrifice"). The Law is present, but a counter-voice inside the same canon is dismantling it. The text is at war with its own ritual system. That tension is the engine of revelation.

Then the **Gospel** completes the crack. Hebrews says the sacrifices are finished — one final victim ends the need for the mechanism entirely. The Law as protective scar tissue is declared fulfilled and surpassed, because the thing it was protecting against has been exposed and disarmed. You don't need ritual substitutes for the scapegoat once the real scapegoat has been revealed as innocent and the cycle broken. Paul's whole fight in Galatians and Romans is precisely this: the Law was a guardian for a time, but its function ends when the mechanism it managed is exposed.

Now the **Quran and the Sharia.** And here is where your instinct is exactly right, and it's the most important thing in this whole exchange. The Islamic system moves in the _opposite direction from the Gospel's trajectory._ Where the biblical arc runs Law → prophetic critique of Law → fulfillment-and-transcendence of Law, the Quranic-and-Sharia arc runs toward the _intensification and permanent installation_ of the protective system. The dietary codes, the purity rules, the prohibitions, the ritual repetition (the five prayers, the fast, the pilgrimage re-enacting Abraham and Hagar), the legal penalties that fence off the community and resolve disorder through prescribed punishment — this is, in Girard's exact terms, **the archaic protective apparatus, restored and made permanent and made total.**

That's not an insult. Read it as Girard would: it's a system doing what religious systems have always done — managing mimetic crisis through prohibition, ritual, and controlled sacralized violence against designated transgressors. The hadd punishments, the law of retaliation (_qisas_), the treatment of the apostate and the transgressor — these are the community discharging disorder onto the one who broke the boundary, with the mechanism fully sacralized as _divine law._ The scapegoat function isn't exposed and ended; it's codified, legalized, and made the permanent structure of society.

So the answer to your real question: **the Quran does not expose the mechanism — it perfects it.** Where the Gospel reveals the scapegoat as innocent and shuts the system down, the Sharia takes the protective machinery of archaic religion and installs it as the eternal, divinely-mandated order. It is, structurally, a _re-sacralization._ It's the Law without the prophetic crack — or rather, the Law with the crack deliberately sealed.

This is why Girard, in the few places he addresses Islam directly (mostly in _Battling to the End_ and late interviews), treats it as a return _toward_ the archaic sacred rather than a continuation of the biblical de-sacralizing movement — a powerful re-installation of the violence-managing system that the Gospel was working to dissolve. He's careful and he doesn't develop it much, and you should be careful too, because this is the hottest possible ground.

The locked formulation for your friend:

_"Girard says every archaic religion built a Law — prohibitions, rituals, sacrifices — to manage the violence that the founding murder once resolved. The Bible contains that Law and then spends a thousand years attacking it from inside, until the Gospel declares it fulfilled and shuts the sacrificial system down. The Sharia does the reverse: it takes the protective Law and makes it permanent, total, and divine. One tradition is dismantling the machine. The other is perfecting it."_

The escape route — and it's serious. Your friend will say: of course we have law, _every_ society has law, are you saying a society without prohibitions and penalties is more godly? A lawless world is not a redeemed world, it's chaos. And he has a point that you must concede: 

---
Girard does _not_ think the protective Law is wicked. It restrains violence; that's its whole purpose. The disagreement isn't "law bad." It's about _trajectory_ — whether the system is being opened toward the exposure of the victim, or sealed around the management of the victim. He'll also note, fairly, that historical Christendom _also_ re-sacralized — built its own scapegoating law, burned its own heretics, ran the Inquisition. Girard agrees completely. His claim was never that Christians behaved better; it's that the Gospel text contains the antidote _to_ Christendom's own violence, the standard by which Christendom condemns itself. The question to put back to your friend is precisely that: _does your text contain the resource to expose and end its own sacred violence — or does it sacralize that violence as the permanent will of God?_ That's the real fault line, and it's the one you can defend without sliding into "my religion is peaceful and yours isn't," which is a sentiment he'll crush and which would wound your mother for nothing.

Fight on trajectory and self-critique, not on body count. That's where the Girardian argument is actually locked.
