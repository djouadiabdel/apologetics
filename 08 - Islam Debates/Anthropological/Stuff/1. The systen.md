Mimetic desire → rivalry → the whole community in crisis → the crisis discharged onto a victim. That's the chain. Mimetic desire is the _origin_; the scapegoat is the _resolution_ the system reaches. So "purity comes from stopping the mimetic game" is the _deeper_ root, and "purity comes from refusing to scapegoat" is the _visible_ fruit. Both are Gospel, but they're two depths of the same thing.

More precisely: Gospel purity is not _primarily_ "stop desiring" — that's closer to Buddhism, and Girard is explicit that he is not preaching the extinction of desire. It's the **redirection of desire toward a model who doesn't compete with you.** Mimetic desire is inescapable — you will imitate someone. The question is whether your model is a rival (who has what you want, blocks you, and pulls you into conflict) or a model who gives rather than blocks. Christ as model is the non-rivalrous mediator: "imitate me" but the thing imitated is self-giving, so imitation produces no scarcity, no rivalry, no crisis, hence no need for a victim. _That's_ the cure at the root. Purity isn't the absence of desire; it's desire with the rivalry drained out, because the model is no longer a competitor.

So your sentence, corrected: _Gospel purity comes from changing what we imitate — from rivalrous models who pull us into conflict and toward a victim, to a self-giving model who breaks the rivalry at the source — which dissolves both the mimetic crisis and the scapegoating that resolves it._ Two layers: desire healed (root), scapegoating refused (fruit).

_Mimetic desire and rivalry (the diagnosis):_

- James 4:1–2 — "What causes quarrels? Your desires at war within you. You desire and do not have, so you kill." (The clearest mimetic-rivalry text in the NT.)
- Genesis 4 — Cain and Abel, the first rivalry, the first murder, the founding of the first city on it.
- Genesis 37 — Joseph, envied by his brothers, thrown in the pit. (Your Joseph/Oedipus key.)
- Exodus 20:17 — the tenth commandment against coveting; Girard reads this as the Bible naming mimetic desire directly, the only commandment about an interior state.

_The non-rivalrous model (the cure at the root):_

- John 13:34 / 15:12 — "Love as I have loved you." (Imitation, but of self-gift.)
- Philippians 2:5–8 — "Have this mind among yourselves… he emptied himself." (The model who descends instead of competing.)
- 1 Corinthians 11:1 — "Be imitators of me as I am of Christ." (Explicit mimetic chain, redirected.)
- Matthew 11:29 — "Learn from me, for I am gentle and lowly." (Model without rivalry.)

_The scapegoat exposed and refused (the cure as fruit):_

- John 8:1–11 — the woman caught in adultery; the mob dissolved. (The single cleanest scene.)
- John 11:47–53 — Caiaphas: "better one man die for the people." (The mechanism confessed by its operator.)
- Luke 23:34 — "Father, forgive them." (The victim refusing to return the accusation.)
- Acts 2:23, 3:14–15 — "you crucified… you killed the author of life." (The killers told the victim was innocent — your kill-shot.)
- Isaiah 52:13–53:12 — the Suffering Servant; the innocent victim the community is finally able to see.
- Leviticus 16 — the literal scapegoat ritual, named, the system the Gospel ends.
- Hebrews 9:11–10:14 — the final sacrifice that ends sacrifice.
- 1 Corinthians 1:18–25 — the cross as scandal; why it stays a scandal instead of being cleaned up.

That's your verse map. Read them in those three groups and you'll see the architecture: desire → rivalry → victim → exposure → end. The whole Girardian arc is checkable across those passages.
