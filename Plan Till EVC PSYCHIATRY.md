# What you are actually trying to build

You do **not** merely want to “learn some psychology before studying psychiatry.”

You want to approach psychiatry from the inside outward:

1. **What is the patient experiencing?**
    
2. **How do I describe it precisely as a doctor?**
    
3. **What psychiatric or medical processes could explain it?**
    
4. **What should I do clinically?**
    
5. **What does the clinical model fail to explain about the person?**
    

You are afraid—correctly—that if you begin only with diagnostic criteria, medication tables and exam questions, you may learn to recognise disorders without ever developing a deep understanding of:

- what consciousness is;
    
- what makes a thought feel like _my_ thought;
    
- how reality changes in psychosis;
    
- how time and possibility disappear in depression;
    
- how desire can oppose the will;
    
- how illness affects freedom and responsibility;
    
- what distinguishes a person from their symptoms;
    
- when psychiatry heals and when it merely controls;
    
- where scientific explanation ends and philosophical interpretation begins.
    

So your project is:

> **To form the mind that will later study clinical psychiatry—not to replace clinical psychiatry.**

You want to become a psychiatrist who can perform all the ordinary medical tasks—diagnosis, differential diagnosis, investigations, risk assessment and treatment—but who can also understand the human being those tasks concern.

That is a legitimate psychiatric education. The official French DES itself includes psychopathology, culture, development, diagnostic and functional assessment, interviewing, ethics, patient rights, consent, psychotherapies, rehabilitation and the organisation of care alongside neuroscience and psychopharmacology. ([Légifrance](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/loda/article_lc/LEGIARTI000047115341/2026-03-12?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Article Annexe II - II. Maquette 27 - Arrêté du 21 avril 2017 relatif ..."))

# Why this will help when you study clinically

Consider a patient who says:

> “Everywhere I go, people seem to be looking at me differently.”

A purely superficial approach asks:

> “Is that a delusion: yes or no?”

A properly formed psychiatrist asks:

- Is this ordinary self-consciousness?
    
- Social anxiety?
    
- Shame after a humiliating event?
    
- Trauma-related hypervigilance?
    
- An overvalued idea?
    
- Ideas of reference?
    
- A delusional perception?
    
- A culturally understandable fear?
    
- Is the patient doubtful, convinced or completely incorrigible?
    
- Did reality first begin feeling strange before the explanation appeared?
    
- Are there hallucinations, thought disorder, mood symptoms, substance use or neurological signs?
    

**Phenomenology helps you hear the differences.**

**Descriptive psychopathology gives those differences clinical names.**

**Medical psychiatry tells you the differential diagnosis, investigations, risks and treatment.**

**Philosophy prevents you from assuming that the diagnostic name has explained the entire person.**

This is the relationship we want:

> **Experience → semiology → diagnosis → formulation → action → reflection**

Not philosophy instead of medicine, and not medicine emptied of philosophy.

# How this eventually helps with the EVC

The philosophical reading will not directly answer every EVC question. The EVC will not ask you to explain Husserl, Girard or Chesterton.

Under the present 2026 format, the external EVC consists of one two-hour fundamental-knowledge paper and one two-hour practical-knowledge paper, equally weighted. That format must be checked again before your probable 2028 sitting because regulations may change. ([Le CNG](https://www.cng.sante.fr/session-evc-2026?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Session EVC 2026"))

Past official psychiatry papers asked concrete questions such as:

- dimensions of PTSD;
    
- evaluation of suicidal crisis;
    
- signs suggesting bipolar depression;
    
- definition of _barrage d’idéation_;
    
- criteria for OCD;
    
- opioid withdrawal;
    
- compulsory-care documents;
    
- lithium intoxication;
    
- neuroleptic malignant syndrome.
    

The practical examination used clinical cases requiring structured medical decisions. ([Le CNG](https://www.cng.sante.fr/sites/default/files/media/2022-06/2020_EVCF%20pt2.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com "MEDECINE GENERALE Epreuve de vérification des ..."))

Therefore:

- **Phenomenology will help you see and interview.**
    
- **Sims will help you name what you see.**
    
- **The French CNUP reference will help you organise diagnosis and management.**
    
- **Guidelines, law and pharmacology will tell you what to do.**
    
- **Annales will teach you how to express it under examination conditions.**
    

The deep curriculum gives you clinical intelligence. The later EVC phase gives that intelligence speed, completeness and French examination form.

# Your curriculum has three spines

You should always have one book open from each spine.

## Spine 1 — The lived experience

This asks:

> What is it like to inhabit depression, psychosis, mania, compulsion or depersonalisation?

Main texts:

- _The Maudsley Reader in Phenomenological Psychiatry_
    
- Matthew Ratcliffe, _Experiences of Depression_
    
- Louis Sass, _Madness and Modernism_
    
- Thomas Fuchs, _Ecology of the Brain_
    

## Spine 2 — Clinical psychopathology

This asks:

> What exactly am I observing, and what is the correct psychiatric term?

Main texts:

- Femi Oyebode, _Sims’ Symptoms in the Mind_
    
- the 2024 French _Référentiel de Psychiatrie et Addictologie_
    

## Spine 3 — The conceptual and ethical problem

This asks:

> What is a disorder, a person, a free choice, a diagnosis or a legitimate medical intervention?

Main texts:

- Gareth Owen, _Psychiatry and Human Nature_
    
- Derek Bolton, _What Is Mental Disorder?_
    
- Lisa Bortolotti, _Delusions and Other Irrational Beliefs_
    
- Mohammed Abouelleil Rashed, _Madness and the Demand for Recognition_
    

Girard, Chesterton, Michael Brown and Christian anthropology become **companions travelling through these three spines**, not substitutes for them.

### Girardian bridge

Girard asks where desire comes from and how another person becomes its mediator. But OCD teaches an essential correction:

> The presence of a thought or desire does not necessarily indicate endorsement, imitation or intention.

This prevents you from applying mimetic theory too crudely.

### Christian bridge

This becomes a rich area for distinctions between:

- temptation and consent;
    
- guilt and felt guilt;
    
- conscience and anxiety;
    
- moral responsibility and involuntary mental events.
    

### Clinical payoff

You will interview more safely because you will not confuse:

- intrusive violent thoughts with violent intent;
    
- sexual obsessions with desired fantasies;
    
- compulsive confession with ordinary religious practice;
    
- obsessional doubt with delusional conviction.

# Part XIV — Chesterton and the problem of sanity

Chesterton helps you question systems that are internally consistent but humanly incomplete.

## Read from _Orthodoxy_

- Chapter II: **The Maniac**
    
- Chapter III: **The Suicide of Thought**
    
- Chapter IV: **The Ethics of Elfland**
    
- Chapter VI: **The Paradoxes of Christianity**
    
- Chapter VII: **The Eternal Revolution**
    
- Chapter VIII: **The Romance of Orthodoxy** ([logoslibrary.org](https://www.logoslibrary.org/chesterton/orthodoxy/index.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Chesterton: Orthodoxy"))
    

## Psychiatric questions generated by Chesterton

- Is madness always a failure of logic, or can it be logic trapped inside a closed system?
    
- Can a theory explain everything by excluding anything that contradicts it?
    
- Is wonder psychologically important?
    
- Does material explanation exhaust personal reality?
    
- Can unlimited autonomy become psychologically destructive?
    
- What distinguishes sanity from mere social conformity?
    
- Can psychiatric theory become “mad” by reducing every human action to one mechanism?
    

Chesterton does not teach clinical psychiatry. He teaches intellectual vigilance against reductionism and systems that mistake narrowness for completeness.

# What success looks like

You have truly learned a psychiatric subject when you can do all of the following:

1. Describe the experience in ordinary human language.
    
2. Identify the exact psychopathological phenomena.
    
3. Produce a psychiatric and medical differential diagnosis.
    
4. Ask the necessary interview questions.
    
5. Assess severity, risk, insight and capacity.
    
6. Explain appropriate investigations and management.
    
7. Identify what the medical model explains well.
    
8. Identify what it leaves unresolved.
    
9. Relate it carefully to personhood, Girard, Chesterton or theology.
    
10. Explain one worthwhile question in a five-to-ten-minute video.
    

That is your real desire:

> **To become clinically exact without becoming intellectually narrow; philosophically deep without becoming medically vague; and Christian without using theology as a shortcut around the reality of psychiatric illness.**
