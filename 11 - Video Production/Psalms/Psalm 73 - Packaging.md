## Description

> There is a person you want something from that they cannot give you. Not a favour, something underneath that. You want them to look at you and settle the question of whether you are real, and you have probably noticed that when they do give you attention it lasts about four days and then you need it again.
>
> Psalm 73 is about that condition, and the man who wrote it names it in the second line.
>
> This psalm is usually read as theodicy, a complaint about the prosperity of the wicked. The Greek does not support that reading. At verse 3 the object of envy is not their goods but their peace, their untroubledness. At verse 13 the grievance turns out to be a wage dispute, in vain I justified my heart, purity run as a payment scheme. And at verse 22 the psalmist's own verdict on his earlier state, delivered after the sanctuary, is not that he was right but impatient. It is that he was livestock.
>
> Which sets up the real problem. The standard advice at this point is to desire God more, want him instead, redirect your heart. That is not actionable. You cannot want something by deciding to want it, which is exactly why wanting runs your life instead of the other way round.
>
> What the psalm does instead is verse 17. He tried to understand it and got nowhere, and then he went somewhere, and the seeing changed after the going. Not an argument. A location.
>
> Three tests on that in the second half, including why Psalm 88 ends on darkness is my closest friend and was kept in the book anyway, and what Psalm 51:17 says you are allowed to show up with on a day you have nothing.
>
> You are being formed into the shape of something right now, by whatever is in the slot, according to a law you did not consent to and cannot switch off. The question is never whether you are copying. It is only what.
>
> Scripture is NIV. Greek from the Septuagint, Swete and Rahlfs cross-checked. Psalm 73 in the Hebrew and the NIV is Psalm 72 in the Septuagint and the Orthodox psalters.
