# Assumed Knowledge — what the series never teaches and leans on anyway

**Written before checking the documents**, same discipline as `Exam.md`.

`Exam.md` asked whether the seasons answer what they promise. **This asks the
opposite question: what do they use without ever explaining?** Every item below is
something a script says, or depends on, while assuming the person saying it already
knows what it means.

## Why this file exists

Two failure modes, and they are different.

**On camera.** The pass condition is one take, cold. **A term you have used but
never had to define is exactly where a take dies**, because you reach for the
explanation and it is not there, and it is not there because the document never
needed it to be.

**In the comments.** Every one of these is a question a viewer can ask, and the
series' whole posture is *go and check*. **An unanswerable comment is a worse
result here than in an ordinary channel**, because this series is specifically
about not overclaiming.

## The rule for what belongs on this list

Not everything unexplained is a gap. **A term is a gap when the argument leans on
it.** `z = 3` needs the viewer to know what a standard deviation is, so that is a
gap. The date of Diodorus is decoration, so it is not.

>> **Test: if a viewer answered this wrongly, would they misunderstand a
>> conclusion?** If yes it is on the list. If no it is trivia.

## Girard, the man

**René Girard, 1923 to 2015. French.** Born in Avignon. Trained as an **archivist
and medieval historian** at the École des Chartes, moved to the United States in
1947, and spent his career there.

**He held chairs in French literature**, at Johns Hopkins, SUNY Buffalo, and
finally **Stanford**, where he was professor of French language, literature and
civilization. **Elected to the Académie française in 2005.**

>> **He was a literary critic by post, doing anthropology of religion by
>> inclination**, and that mismatch is the single most useful fact about his
>> reception. **He was never an anthropologist**, and anthropologists have
>> generally not adopted him.

The books, in order, with the English titles this series uses:

| French | English | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| *Mensonge romantique et vérité romanesque*, 1961 | *Deceit, Desire and the Novel* | mimetic desire, read out of novels |
| *La Violence et le sacré*, 1972 | *Violence and the Sacred* | the mechanism, sacrifice, archaic religion |
| *Des choses cachées…*, 1978 | *Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World* | the whole system, in dialogue form |
| *Le Bouc émissaire*, 1982 | ***The Scapegoat*** | **the four stereotypes. Chapter 2** |
| *Je vois Satan tomber comme l'éclair*, 1999 | ***I See Satan Fall Like Lightning*** | **the dissent claim. Pages 3 and 122** |

## The standing of the theory, said honestly

**Mimetic theory is not mainstream anthropology.** It has a serious following in
literary criticism, in theology, and among readers outside the academy, and a
dedicated society and journal. **It is not the working framework of the field
whose subject matter it claims to explain.**

**Say that plainly rather than implying consensus.** It also cuts the other way,
and the honest form of the defence is: an outsider's theory being unadopted is not
the same as its being refuted, and the project exists because the usual reason
given for not adopting it is that it cannot be tested.

>> **Naming individual critics is a research task nobody has done.** Episode 14
>> states five objections by content and **names no one**. Until somebody sources
>> them, **the objections are tier K as attributions** even though the arguments
>> are real. Do not invent a name on camera.

## What Girard is replying to

**Frazer**, *The Golden Bough*: sacrifice as magic and fertility, the dying and
returning king, religion as failed proto-science. **Girard keeps Frazer's
observation that the king is a victim and rejects the magical explanation.**

**Lévi-Strauss**, structuralism: a myth's meaning is in its **structure**, in
oppositions and transformations, not in any event behind it. **This is the direct
rival.** Girard's claim is that there is a real event behind the myth and the
structure is the scar left by it. **Lévi-Strauss thinks looking for the event is a
category mistake.**

**Freud**, *Totem and Taboo*: the primal horde kills the father. **Girard is
explicitly building on and against this.** The narcissism of small differences is
Freud's, and Girard's version is **structural rather than psychological**: the
conflict is produced by the collapse of difference, not by a drive. **Tier K, both
Freud references, until somebody opens the essays.**

**"Archaic religion"** means the sacrificial religions preceding and outside the
biblical tradition, taken as a class. **"The sacred"** in Girard is not a feeling
of holiness. It is the ambivalent power attributed to the victim afterwards:
**both the cause of the disaster and the source of the peace**, which is why
archaic gods are so often both.

## The statistics you are assumed to know

**Standard deviation.** The typical distance of a value from the average of its
group. If the fake results average −0.16 with a standard deviation of 2.95, then a
typical fake lands about three either side of zero. **`z` is just "how many of
those typical distances away am I."**

**Median rather than mean, and why every headline here is a median.** The mean is
dragged by extreme values; the median is not. **These distributions are skewed** —
a few texts score enormously and most score low — so a mean would report a number
no actual text is near. **It is the right choice and the project never defends
it.**

**One-tailed against two-tailed, and this is the real gap.** A two-tailed test
asks "is there a difference." A one-tailed test asks "is it bigger, in the
direction I predicted." **This project makes directional predictions throughout**
(Gospel above myth, tragedy above myth), so its tests are one-tailed.

>> **That matters and it is never said.** A one-tailed p-value is **half** its
>> two-tailed equivalent for the same data. Anybody recomputing these numbers
>> two-tailed gets different figures, and the pre-registrations do not state the
>> choice. **Say it out loud before somebody in the comments does.**

**Where 0.05 comes from.** Fisher, as a convenience, roughly a century ago. **It
has no principled basis**, it is not a property of the world, and the project uses
it because everyone does. **A result at 0.049 and one at 0.051 are the same
result**, which is the question already asked in Season 3 episode 9.

**Confidence intervals, and why there are none here.** An interval expresses the
precision of an **estimate**. Almost everything here is a **comparison against a
null** rather than an estimate of a quantity, so the natural summary is the null
distribution itself. **That is a defensible reason and the project never gives
it**, and the honest addition is that intervals on the tier medians would have
made the 0.07 mythography gap visibly meaningless years earlier.

**Multiple comparisons.** Fifty predictions at 0.05 means about two and a half
false passes expected **even if nothing is true anywhere**. The project applies no
correction. **Why it matters less here:** multiplicity inflates **false
positives**, and this project's problem is that almost everything **failed**. You
cannot have many false passes when you have few passes. **It would matter enormously
if the record were the other way round.**

**Effect size against p-value.** The p-value says how surprising the result is if
nothing is going on. **The effect size says how big it is.** A huge sample makes
tiny effects significant; a small sample leaves large effects insignificant.
**Season 3 episode 8's whole argument is an effect-size argument** — 0.5% inside a
17% wobble — made without ever using the term.

**Correlation, and what `r = −0.243` looks like.** A correlation runs from −1 to
+1. **0.243 is weak**: a faint tendency visible only in aggregate, invisible in any
individual text, and it explains about **6%** of the variation. **Report it as a
hint, never as a mechanism.**

## The texts and the tools

**ASV and KJV.** The **American Standard Version, 1901**, and the **King James
Version, 1611**. Both are **out of copyright**, which is the entire reason they are
the two translations in this project. **The translation control in Season 3
episode 5 compares two Bible translations and never names them on camera.**

**Why everything is a translation**, and the cost: no Greek is scored anywhere in
this project. **Every number is a measurement of English**, produced by translators
working between 1611 and 1921 with their own habits. Season 3 episode 5 is the only
check on this and it has a sample size of one.

**A messenger speech** is a convention of Greek tragedy: violence happens offstage
and a messenger comes on to describe it. **This is why the *Bacchae*'s killing is
600 words in a 17,180-word play** — the form requires it to be reported rather than
shown, and the instrument's dilution problem is partly a fact about the genre.

**A chorus** is the group that comments on the action, often the community's voice.
**For a Girardian reading that is not decoration**: it is the crowd, on stage,
saying what the crowd thinks.

**Git, a commit, and what it guarantees.** A commit is a dated snapshot of every
file. **It guarantees the order in which things were written down. It does not
guarantee the order in which things were known**, which is exactly the distinction
Season 2 episode 3 is built on.

**Operationalise** means turning a claim into a procedure that produces a number.
**It is the step where results are decided**, because the claim is settled in
prose and the procedure is what actually runs.
