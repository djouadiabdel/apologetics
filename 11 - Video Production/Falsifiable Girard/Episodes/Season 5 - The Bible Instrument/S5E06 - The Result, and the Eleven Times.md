## What came out

The prediction had five parts and four of them held.

**Direction holds.** Within each matched comparison the canon interlocks more than
the apocrypha. On the empty tomb it is 0.25 against 0.00. On the feeding of the
five thousand against the infancy miracles it is 0.67 against 0.00.

**Hard collisions hold.** The apocrypha collide harder, at 0.95 per thousand
words against the canon's 0.22.

Notice what that second number admits. **The canon is not clean.** Mark's ending,
where the women say nothing to anyone, is logged as a hard collision against the
other accounts, by the person hoping the canon would win. It is in the numerator
where it hurts.

That single logging decision is worth more than the paragraph around it, and here
is the test for whether it was honest. **The rescue was available.** Mark's ending
is textually disputed, the manuscript tradition is genuinely messy there, and a
coder who wanted to could have excluded the item on those grounds and written a
defensible sentence explaining why.

> **He did not, and the item sits in the canon's own collision count.** That is
> what it looks like when a rule written in advance binds somebody who would
> rather it did not, and it is the only reason any of the other numbers in this
> table are worth reading.

**Embellishment holds, and it is the strongest thing in the run.** One
embellishment in the canon, which is Matthew's raised saints coming out of the
tombs. Eight in the apocrypha. Adjusted for length that is **0.22 per thousand
against 2.54, about eleven times.**

**Independence holds.** All five canonical fits are between texts that do not
depend on each other, mostly John against the **Synoptics**.

That word needs unpacking, because the whole prediction rests on it. Matthew, Mark
and Luke are called the Synoptic gospels, from a Greek word meaning **seen
together**, because they can be laid out in three parallel columns and read
across. They share wording, order and whole episodes, and the standard explanation
is that **Matthew and Luke both used Mark.**

John is the odd one out. Different structure, different episodes, largely
different material, and no accepted account has him copying the other three.

> So a fit between two Synoptics is nearly worthless as evidence, because copying
> explains it. **A fit between John and a Synoptic cannot be explained that way.**
> All five canonical fits are of the second kind, so the interlock advantage is
> not an artifact of copying, which was the obvious deflating explanation and it
> is ruled out.

## The prediction that failed

**Magnitude fails on the canon's own side.** The floor was set at 0.60 before
scoring. The canon comes in at **0.50**, and only two of its four pericopes clear
the floor.

And the reason is worth sitting with, because it is not a technical failure. **The
passion narratives are collision-dense enough to drag the canon's own interlock
rate below the floor its defender set for it.** The empty tomb scores 0.25. That
is the single most theologically loaded pericope in the corpus and it is the worst
performer on the canonical side.

There are two readings of that and the episode should give you both rather than
pick.

**The uncomfortable one.** The instrument does worst exactly where the tradition
needs it most, and a defender would have to explain why the central claim is
supported by the shakiest pericope in the corpus.

**The interesting one.** Collision density is what you would expect from several
independent people describing a chaotic morning nobody understood at the time.
The accounts of a car crash disagree more than the accounts of a dinner party, and
the disagreement is not evidence that the crash was invented.

> **Both readings are available from the same number**, and nothing in this run
> chooses between them, which is why the episode reports the number and stops.

> The prediction that broke is the one flagged softest in advance, which is
> either good calibration or a sign that the soft flag was doing the work of a
> hedge. **Episode 7 is the author deciding which.**

The boy who withers a playmate kills him in one version and heals him in another.

Those are not interpretations. You do not need to know the hypothesis or trust the

## The clinical anchor

**Inter-observer variability, and which findings survive it.**

Two radiologists agree almost perfectly on whether there is a fracture. They agree
much less on whether a lung field shows early consolidation. **Same imaging, same
training, different amounts of judgement in the call.**

So a study reporting both should not report one confidence for both. The fracture
count is nearly a fact and the consolidation count is a measurement of the
observers as much as the patients, and a paper that averages them into a single
result has hidden its own weakest number inside its strongest.

## The character anchor

**A fishing story growing between two tellings.**

The fish gets bigger. That is the recognisable shape of a story being retold by
people invested in it, and everybody knows it on sight, which is exactly why it
works as a measure.

**Now notice what the measurement actually is.** Not whether the first telling was
true. Only whether the second telling grew, and by how much, against a baseline of
somebody retelling the same event without growth. The canon grows once in four and
a half thousand words. The apocrypha grow eight times in three thousand.

## What would count against this

1. **Thirty-nine items.** It is a very small run, and episode 8 measures exactly
   how fragile a run this size is.
2. **The 11× being about genre rather than truthfulness.** The infancy gospels are
   arguably a devotional genre where embellishment is the point, not a failure. If
   so, the measure is detecting genre and calling it legend.
3. **The empty tomb scoring worst.** The pericope the whole tradition cares most
   about is the one where the canon performs worst on its own instrument, and
   **that is either honest reporting or a sign the instrument mismeasures exactly
   where it matters.**
