## Collecting episode 12

Now the 51 items that survive. They are real clashes between two assertions, and
the question is no longer whether there is a problem. It is **what fixing it
costs.**

## The scale

Five levels, and the point of it is that "is it a contradiction" is the wrong
question and "what do you have to believe to make it go away" is the right one.

**Cost 0, dissolved.** The plain text shows no contradiction. Mentioning one of
two is not denying two.

**Cost 1, licensed.** A rescue exists, is directly cited, and is uncontested. A
lexeme genuinely has that range. A custom is genuinely attested.

**Cost 2, contested.** A real rescue exists but rests on a debated reading or a
reconstruction. A live difficulty, not a knockout.

**Cost 3, ad hoc.** The reconciliation needs an event no text states, or an
emendation of the text. Ehrman's phrase for this is **writing a new gospel**, and
it is the right phrase.

**Cost 4, hard.** No non-ad-hoc rescue on any channel. A genuine contradiction.

## The Ehrman guard, stated once and applied to everybody

> **A rescue counts as cost 1 or below only if it cites its licence:** a lexeme, a
> verse, a custom, a source. **If the only harmony available is invented events or
> emended text, it is cost 3, no matter how plausible it sounds.**

The last clause is the one that bites. Plausibility is not licence. A reconstruction
can be entirely reasonable, the sort of thing that probably did happen, and still
be cost 3, **because the question is not whether it is true but whether a text says
it.**

That separation is unfamiliar enough to be worth a second pass, because most
argument runs the two together.

Suppose two accounts of a journey disagree about where somebody stopped, and you
propose they stopped twice. **That may well be what happened.** People do stop
twice. The proposal is not far-fetched and a historian might accept it without
blinking.

**It is still cost 3**, because no text says they stopped twice, and the proposal
was generated by the need to resolve the disagreement rather than by any evidence.
The scale is not measuring whether your story is true. **It is measuring where
your story came from**, and a story generated by the problem it solves has no
independent standing however plausible it sounds.

And the guard runs both ways, which is the part that separates this from
apologetics with extra steps. It blocks the defender inventing a harmony **and it
blocks the coder dissolving an item because it feels fine.** Both are supplying
from outside what the text does not supply.

## Why the scale is better than a verdict

A binary verdict on a contradiction produces an argument that never ends, because
both sides can always say the word. The scale converts the argument into a
question with an answer: **name the channel your rescue runs on, and cite it.**

That reframing does something specific to the debate. It stops being about whether
the Bible has contradictions and starts being about the **price list**, and a price
list can be audited item by item by somebody who disagrees with you about
everything else.

And it changes what winning looks like, which is the deeper effect.

Under a binary, both sides are trying to establish a fact about the Bible, and
neither can, so the argument runs forever on the same examples. Under a scale,
**both sides are trying to establish a price for a specific item**, and prices can
be compared, aggregated, and revised one at a time.

> A defender who accepts the scale has conceded that some items cost something. A
> sceptic who accepts it has conceded that most cost very little. **Neither
> concession is available in the binary version**, which is why the binary
> version is the one that never moves.

## The device: one item at four prices

| proposed rescue for the same clash | channel cited | cost |
|---|---|---|
| the two texts describe different occasions, and a text says so | verse | **1** |
| the word has a second attested sense, here is the lexicon | lexeme | **1** |
| the word could mean something else | **nothing** | **3** |
| there was an intervening event nobody records | **nothing** | **3** |
| no rescue on any channel | | **4** |

**Rows three and four are the ones that sound fine out loud.** They are the ones
you hear in sermons and in debates, and they are priced at 3 because a sentence
that could be said about any two texts whatsoever has no evidential content.

## The clinical anchor

**Grading an adverse event rather than declaring one.**

Nobody asks whether the patient had a side effect. They grade it: mild, moderate,
severe, life-threatening, fatal. Five levels, defined in advance, applied by
somebody who did not choose the drug.

**The grading is what makes the data poolable.** A trial reporting "some adverse
events" tells you nothing, and a trial reporting grade 3 and above tells you what
the drug costs. And the discipline that makes it work is that the grade is fixed
by criteria rather than by how the reporter feels about the drug.

## The character anchor

**A repair quote.**

You do not ask the mechanic whether the car has a problem. You ask what it costs to
fix, and the answer sorts itself into a scale everybody understands: a bulb, a
service item, a part that needs ordering, an engine rebuild, **write it off.**

**And the useful conversation is always about the itemised list**, not about
whether the car is "fine". Somebody who says the car is fine and somebody who says
it is scrap can both look at the same quote and find they are arguing about one
line on it.

## What would count against this

1. **The scale rewarding ingenuity.** A clever defender can always find *some*
   channel, so a well-read apologist scores lower costs than a less-read one on
   identical texts. **The scale measures the state of the defence literature as
   much as the texts.**
2. **Cost 3 being the interesting category and the fuzziest.** The line between a
   reconstruction and a licensed reading is exactly where the argument lives.
3. **The guard being applied by one person.** Symmetric in principle, and applied
   by somebody with a view. **Episode 15 measures how much that mattered**, and
   the answer is: enough to fire a falsifier.
