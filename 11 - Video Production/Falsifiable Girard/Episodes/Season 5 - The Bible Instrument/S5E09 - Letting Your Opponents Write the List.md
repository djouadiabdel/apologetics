# Letting Your Opponents Write the List

## The stake

The contradiction list is built from hostile compilations, taken entire, so the
hard items cannot be skipped. **Letting your opponents write the list is the
strongest move available**, and this episode shows what it costs and what it
buys.

## Collecting episode 8

Two runs, eight months apart, failing the same way: direction without magnitude.
And a leave-one-out check showing the second verdict rests on four sentences.

Layer 1a asked whether the texts interlock. Layer 1b asks the question people
actually argue about. **When somebody alleges a contradiction, is it one, and what
does the rescue cost?**

## The first decision, and it settles the study's honesty

You need a list of alleged contradictions. There are two ways to get one.

**You can compile it yourself.** You know the texts, you know the hard places, and
you would produce a thoughtful list. And every reader who distrusts you would be
right to, because you also know which items you can answer.

**Or you can take the list from people who want it to hurt.**

The docket here is **671 raw allegations**, aggregated from three hostile
compilations: the Skeptic's Annotated Bible, Infidels.org, and EvilBible. Not a
sample of them. The aggregation, entire.

> **The docket is hostile-sourced and complete, so the coder cannot skip the hard
> ones.** They are on the list because somebody who wanted the New Testament to
> look bad put them there, and removing one requires a stated reason on the
> record.

## What that buys, and what it does not

It buys the one thing selection bias cannot survive: **the person being tested did
not choose the test.**

And it costs something real, which is why almost nobody does it.

A hostile list is full of bad entries. Items that misread the verse, items that
allege a contradiction where one side is a question, the same complaint four times
over. **You have to work all of them**, seriously, and write down the reason each
one fails, and that is a great deal of tedious labour producing nothing anybody
wants to read.

The temptation is to filter first: drop the obviously bad entries, keep the
serious ones, and work those properly. **That is fatal, and the reason is that you
would be the one deciding which are obviously bad**, which hands back exactly the
control that taking the list was meant to give away.

And it changes what the result is a statement about, in a way worth being precise
about. This is a **ceiling on the accusers' case**, never a floor on the truth.

If the strongest available compilation of objections yields two hard problems, you
have learned something about that compilation. **You have not learned that there
are only two problems in the New Testament**, because a contradiction nobody has
noticed is not on anybody's list, and no procedure here would find it.

That asymmetry is permanent and it is worth naming precisely, because it is the
kind of thing that gets lost the moment a number is quoted.

**A hostile docket bounds the accusers and says nothing about the texts.** It is
the difference between "your best shot failed" and "there was nothing to hit."
Those two sentences are often said as though they were one sentence, and only the
first is available here.

> The version that would say something about the texts is the denominator study
> in episode 17, which walks every cross-checkable pair whether or not anybody
> complained about it. **It has not been built**, and until it is, every number
> in this layer is a fact about three websites.

## The device: who picks the items, and what follows

| docket source | protects against | what the result means |
|---|---|---|
| the defender compiles it | nothing | worthless |
| a neutral party compiles it | most selection | a real estimate, unavailable in practice |
| **hostile compilers** | **the defender skipping hard items** | **a ceiling on their case** |

**Compare this to run 1**, which is the point of putting the two layers in one
season. Run 1's fit ratio failed for exactly the opposite reason: **there, the
coder picked the items.** Same person, same project, two months apart, and the
difference between a trustworthy number and an untrustworthy one is entirely who
wrote the list.

## The clinical anchor

**An incident reporting system, and what it can never measure.**

You can count everything that was filed. You can categorise it, trend it, and
present it to the board. And the denominator is not errors, it is **errors
somebody chose to write up**, which is a different quantity moving for different
reasons.

So a falling incident rate is compatible with improving care and equally
compatible with staff losing faith in the form. **The number is real and it is
about the reporting, not the harm**, and a service that forgets this congratulates
itself on a graph.

## The character anchor

**Letting the prosecution pick the charges.**

A defence that says "try me on whatever you like" is making a much stronger claim
than one that says "try me on this". Everybody watching understands the
difference immediately, without any legal training.

**And it has an exact limit that people forget.** Surviving the charges brought
says nothing about a crime nobody charged. The acquittal is real and it is bounded
by the indictment, which somebody else wrote.

## What would count against this

1. **The compilers being bad at their job.** These lists are aggregations of
   popular objections, not scholarly ones. **The best objections in the academic
   literature may not appear**, and the ceiling is a ceiling on the popular case.
2. **671 being inflated by duplication.** It is, heavily, and episode 12 measures
   exactly how much. That is a finding about the lists rather than a flaw in
   using them.
3. **Hostile sourcing not preventing hostile gating.** They pick the items. **The
   coder still decides which ones count**, and episode 15 is a falsifier firing on
   precisely that.
