# The Crimes, and Why They Are Never Small

## The stake

You have heard accusations like this one. When a group needs someone guilty, the
charge is never petty, because a petty crime could be handled by ordinary means.
**This episode shows you how to read the size of an accusation as information
about the accusers**, and once you see it you will see it in every scandal that
crosses your feed.

Episode 6 was the community saying **what is happening to us.** This episode is
the accusers saying **what he did**, and where the crisis bucket failed by being
too common, this one fails by being too rare.

## The pattern inside the accusations

Here is the observation that makes this more than a list of bad things.

**The accusation is never a small crime.** Nobody is scapegoated for petty theft,
or laziness, or being bad at their job. It is always a crime that **erases a
distinction the community lives by**, and Girard sorts them into four families.

| family | examples | the difference it erases |
|---|---|---|
| violence against the most protected | killing a father, a king, a child | kin against stranger, ruler against ruled |
| sexual transgression | incest, bestiality | family against outsider, human against animal |
| religious crime | sacrilege, blasphemy, profanation | sacred against common |
| contagion crimes | poisoning, sorcery, treason | spreads invisibly, erasing the line between one person and everyone |

## And now the argument, which is the whole episode

Look at what those four have in common. Not "they are serious." **Look at the
shape.**

Every one of them describes **a person who collapsed a distinction.**

And episode 6 told you what the crisis is: **distinctions collapsing.**

> **So the community is accusing one person of being the crisis.**
> 
> The victim is charged with **being the thing that is happening to everybody.**

That is the logic in a sentence, and it is why the crime families are not an
arbitrary list. They are the crisis, attributed to a person.

**And it explains something otherwise strange**: why the accusations in these
stories are so lurid and so specific. A community that says "he made us all
alike" has said nothing actionable. A community that says "he slept with his
mother" has said the same thing in a form you can execute someone for.

That is the step worth slowing down on, because it is where the theory earns its
keep. **The crisis is real and it is abstract**, and nobody can be put to death
for an abstraction. The accusation is the crisis translated into a charge, and a
charge has a defendant, and a defendant can be dealt with in an afternoon.

> **The lurid detail is not decoration and it is not sadism. It is the conversion
> step**, and a community that could not perform it would be stuck with a
> catastrophe it had no way to end.

## The clinical anchor

**A diagnosis of exclusion, arrived at from the wrong end.**

A patient is unwell and nothing fits. The picture is diffuse, the tests are
equivocal, and there is real pressure in the room to name something, because a
name is what lets the next step happen. **A named condition can be treated. An
unexplained deterioration cannot be**, and everyone in the corridor knows which of
those two the family will accept.

The label that gets attached is often specific out of all proportion to the
evidence for it, and the specificity is the point. **It converts a situation into
a problem**, and the conversion is what the room actually needed.

## The character anchors

**Light Yagami is accused, correctly, of exactly this shape.** Kira's crime is not
murder in the ordinary sense; the show is not interested in a body count. It is
that he **dissolves the difference between the judge and the criminal**, and
everyone who is frightened of him is frightened of that.

**Tom Ripley is the identity version.** What disturbs people is not the killing.
It is that he can **occupy another man's life and nobody can tell.** He is a crime
against distinctions in person.

## And the reason it is rare, which is a finding in its own right

Worth an extra minute, because it is a genuine discovery about **language** rather
than about theory.

Narrative texts **do not say "he committed parricide."** They say **"he slew his
father."**

The words `parricide`, `patricide` and `regicide` fire **zero times in roughly two
hundred thousand words** of Greek and Roman literature.

> **The theory names the crime with a label. The texts describe it with a verb.**

That gap is not a technicality. It is where the first version of the instrument
died, and repairing it is most of Season 2 episode 9.

## What would count against this

1. **Accusations in persecution texts that are ordinary crimes.** If victims were
   routinely charged with theft, cowardice or incompetence rather than
   difference-erasing crimes, the four families are a pattern imposed rather than
   found.
2. **The four families appearing at the same rate in texts with no persecution in
   them.** That is checkable and Season 3 checks a version of it.
3. **Communities that accuse the victim of nothing at all** and simply expel him.
   Girard needs the accusation to do work.

**A word about the play that is going to come up in every season**, because it is
named here for the first time and it carries more weight than any other text in
this project.

The ***Bacchae*** is a Greek tragedy by **Euripides**, written around 405 BC. The
god Dionysus arrives in the city of Thebes and the king, **Pentheus**, refuses to
recognise him and has him arrested. Dionysus drives the women of Thebes into a
frenzy out on the mountain, Pentheus goes to spy on them, and they tear him apart
with their hands. **The woman who leads the killing is Agave, his own mother**,
and she carries his head back into the city believing it is a lion's.

That tearing-apart has a Greek name, **`sparagmos`**, and it is the technical term
for the ritual dismemberment of a living victim by a crowd. **Keep the word. It
returns in Season 3**, where the instrument tries to locate that exact passage.

> Now keep number 3 in mind. **In Girard's own best example, the mob does not
> believe it is executing a criminal.** It believes it is killing a lion.
> **Episode 15 reads the play in full**, and the accusation stereotype is the
> weakest of the four in it.

Why Girard leans on it so hard: a mother killing her son, in public, unanimously,
while believing she is doing something else entirely. **All four stereotypes in
one scene**, which is why it becomes the exemplar the whole project is repeatedly
tested against, and why it failing four pre-registered tests later matters so
much.
