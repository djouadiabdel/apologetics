# Rivals Become Copies of Each Other

## The stake

The uncomfortable one. This episode says the rival you hate is the person you were
trying to become, and that the hatred is evidence of the aiming. You will resist
that sentence, which is correct: **the episode predicts your resistance and counts
it as data.

## Start by collecting episode 2, because this episode is its consequence

Episode 2 ended somewhere specific. **What is wanted is no longer the object. It
is the model's being.** Not what he has. What he is.

Now just follow that.

> **If what you want is to be him, then the closer you get to wanting it, the
> more you resemble him.** Not as a side effect. As the thing you were aiming at
> the whole time.

That is this entire episode in one line, and the episode exists because the
conclusion sounds wrong to everybody the first time they hear it.

## The character

**Naruto and Sasuke at the Valley of the End.**

Look at what the fight actually shows you, visually, before any dialogue. They
mirror each other's moves. They stand on **two statues** at either end of a
gorge. The frame is symmetrical. Each is the other's entire reason for being
there.

At the beginning of the series they are opposites and any viewer can say what the
difference is: one has nothing and wants to be seen, the other has everything and
wants to be left alone.

**By that fight, an audience member who did not know the show could not tell you
from the fight alone which one is supposed to be the hero.**

**Second anchor: Light Yagami and L.** They begin as pure opposites, the criminal
and the detective, and converge until each is doing the other's job. L breaks the
law and abducts and tortures. Light investigates the Kira case. **Each becomes
functionally indistinguishable from the thing he is opposing**, and the show is
explicit about it.

## The claim, with the step that usually gets skipped

Girard's word is **doubles**. Rivalry does not preserve the difference that
started it. **It destroys it.**

The lazy version is: A copies B, B copies back, they converge. But **why would B
copy back?** He was there first. He has the thing. What does he have to imitate?

**Episode 2 already answered this and it is worth spending the time.** The
feedback loop:

```
   disciple imitates  ->  model's valuation rises  ->  model becomes possessive
          ^                                                     |
          |_____________  disciple wants it more  <_____________|
```

Once that loop is running, **the model is watching the disciple to know how much
to want the thing.** He is taking his cue from the person who took a cue from
him. At that point the direction of imitation has stopped being a fact about
either of them.

> **The more intense the opposition, the more precisely each mirrors the other**,
> because each is now using the other as the template for how hard to want it.

## The one-sided case, which is a real objection and belongs in the episode

**Vegeta and Goku do not converge**, and if you have been paying attention that
should bother you.

Here is why, and it sharpens the claim rather than weakening it.

**Goku does not reciprocate.** He is not measuring himself against Vegeta. He is
not taking his cue from Vegeta's wanting. The loop above requires two participants
and Goku never joins it.

> **So mediation without reciprocity does not produce doubles.** It produces one
> obsessed person and one person who barely notices.

That is a genuine prediction and you should say it as one: **the theory predicts
convergence only where the rivalry runs both ways.** If you find two people
locked in mutual, escalating rivalry who become steadily **more** different over
time, that is a problem for Girard. If you find a one-sided obsession that never
converges, **that is what the theory expects.**

## The counter-intuitive bit, which is the whole point

Everybody assumes conflict comes from difference. Different values, different
groups, different beliefs, and then a clash.

**Girard says the opposite. Acute conflict comes from the collapse of
difference**, and people fight hardest across the smallest gap.

Freud has a version of this, the narcissism of small differences. **Mark that as
tier K on camera**, and since this is the first time the series says that, here is
what the two tiers mean. **You will hear them for the next seven seasons.**

| tier | what it means |
|---|---|
| **V** | source fetched, citation checked, **quote verified against the original** |
| **K** | the argument is real and the person is real, **the citation is unverified** |

> **Tier K is a lead, not a fact.** It is not a hedge and it is not modesty. It
> means somebody has told you a thing and nobody has opened the book, and the
> series says so out loud rather than letting a page number do work it has not
> earned.

The Freud phrase is real and this series has not gone and checked the essay it
comes from. Girard's version is structural rather than
psychological, and that is the difference worth stating.

**The clinical version, and it is exact.**

Atrial fibrillation is not the heart doing something varied. It is the heart
**losing the differentiation between the pacemaker and everything else.**
Normally one node fires and the rest follow, and that hierarchy is what produces
coordinated output.

In fibrillation every cell fires on its own. Maximum activity. **Complete
uniformity.** And the output is a useless quiver, and the patient dies of **too
much sameness, not too much difference.**

> That is the shape Girard means. Not chaos as variety. **Chaos as
> undifferentiation.**

## And when it goes far enough, the double becomes a monster

One more step, because it is the bridge to episode 5.

Girard has a term for the end state: the **monstrous double**. When two rivals
have become fully interchangeable, each experiences the other as something that
should not exist. Not a person with a different view. **A thing wearing your
face.**

That is why the language of these conflicts reaches for the inhuman. The enemy
becomes a beast, a demon, a plague. **Not because he has become more different,
but because he has become identical and the mind has no category for that.**

**Hold this.** In episode 5 an entire community will do the same thing to one
person.

## What this predicts about texts, and it is the first thing this project can test

If rivalry erases difference, then texts about rivalry should contain vocabulary
about **things losing their distinctness**. Twins. Doubles. Likeness. Sameness.

And beyond the pair, the same failure at whole-society scale: **plague**, which
kills without regard to rank, and **civil war**, which is a war where both sides
are the same people.

**That vocabulary is exactly what the `crisis` bucket in Season 2 is built out
of.** Plague, pestilence, contagion, famine, sterility, twin, double, likeness,
indistinguishable, discord, strife, faction, civil war.

> Episodes 1 and 2 listed things that would falsify them, and then admitted
> **nothing in this project tests any of it.** Episode 3 is different. **This is
> the first claim in the season that the instrument actually reaches**, and the
> counting starts in Season 2.

## What would count against this

1. **Mutual, escalating rivals who become steadily more different.** The core
   prediction reversed.
2. **Rivalry that cools as similarity increases.** Girard says proximity is
   accelerant.
3. **Texts about intense rivalry with no undifferentiation vocabulary in them at
   all**, consistently. That one is checkable and Season 3 checks it.

## Handing off

Two people have now become indistinguishable, and each experiences the other as
something monstrous.

**Episode 4 asks what happens when that is not two people but everybody.**
