# The Marks, and the Victim Who is Too High

[[S1E07 - The Crimes, and Why They Are Never Small|← The Crimes, and Why They Are Never Small]] · [[S1E09 - The Violence, and the Unanimity Hidden Inside it|The Violence, and the Unanimity Hidden Inside it →]]

## The stake

Why him and not someone else? This episode answers a question nobody asks out
loud, and it explains a pattern you have already met: the popular one, the
talented one, the outsider who was somehow always going to be the one blamed.
**After this episode the choice of victim stops looking random to you, in texts
and in group chats alike.

## Collecting episode 7

Episode 7 ended with the accusers saying **what he did.** This episode is the
accusers saying **why it was him**, and it answers the obvious question nobody
asks.

## Why him

If the mechanism really is a community discharging its own tension, and the victim
is not actually the cause, the choice looks arbitrary. **But it is not random
either**, and Girard's answer is precise.

> Victims are chosen for **a sign that puts them slightly outside the group,
> without giving them the power to retaliate.**

Both halves do work. **Outside enough** that turning on them does not feel like
turning on ourselves. **Weak enough** that nobody comes for revenge afterwards,
because revenge would restart the crisis and ending the crisis was the whole
point.

**Notice that this is a two-condition test**, which is unusual in Girard and worth
flagging as a gift. Most of the theory is stated in a form that accommodates
whatever you find. **This one can be run on a candidate and fail**, and the next
section runs it.

## Two directions, and the second is the one people miss

**Too low.** The stranger, the foreigner, the cripple, the lame, the blind, the
deformed, the beggar, the orphan. Everyone expects this list.

**Too high.** The king. The queen. The tyrant.

## Why "too high" works, because the episode has to earn this

Just asserting that the king is a victim mark is not enough. **Run the two
criteria on him.**

**Is he outside the group?** Completely. A king has no peers by definition. He is
the one person in the society who does not have an equal, and every ritual around
him exists to say so.

**Can he retaliate?** This is the counter-intuitive half. He looks like the most
powerful person there. But **he has no faction of his own kind**, because there is
no other king. When a community turns on him, **nobody is obliged to avenge him**,
because he stands in no relation of equals to anybody.

> A powerful man with peers is dangerous to attack. **A powerful man with no
> peers is the safest target in the room**, and this is precisely inverted from
> how it looks.

**And there is a further argument Girard makes**, which is worth having because it
explains why kingship keeps showing up in these stories at all: in many traditions
the king is a **victim whose execution has been deferred.** The rituals that set
him apart, the taboos, the special food, the special clothing, the periodic
humiliations, look like the treatment of a sacred victim postponed.

**Mark that as tier K.** The comparative material on sacred kingship is real and
this series has not gone and checked the sources. **Say so rather than implying
you have.**

## The device: the two criteria, run as a grid

**Put the two questions on one axis and the candidates on the other**, and the
"too high" category stops being a paradox.

| candidate | outside the group? | able to retaliate? | a victim mark? |
|---|---|---|---|
| an ordinary member | no | yes | **no** |
| the stranger, the cripple, the beggar | **yes** | no | **yes** |
| a powerful man **with peers** | no | **yes** | **no** |
| **the king** | **yes**, he has no equals | **no**, no faction of his own kind | **yes** |

**Read the third and fourth rows together.** They are both powerful. Only one of
them is a victim mark, and the difference is not power. **It is whether anybody
is obliged to avenge him.**

## The character anchors

**Too low: Zuko's scar.** Visible, permanent, on the face, and given to him by his
own father inside his own family.

**Too high: Macbeth.** A king who becomes the thing the kingdom must expel to be
well again, and the play describes the country as **sick** and the cure as
removing him.

**And the modern one, which lands hardest: BoJack.** Marked by being famous, which
is the too-high position exactly. The story is about a public turning on him with
grievances that are **completely real** and a process that is **completely
mechanical**, at the same time, and it refuses to let you pick one reading.

> That is the most useful thing about BoJack here. **The mechanism does not
> require the victim to be innocent.** It only requires that the turning is doing
> something for the people turning.

## The number, and it is the one that comes back hardest

`marks` fires at **2.90 per thousand words**, and it is **44% the single word
`king`.**

Put it in the sequence you now have. Crisis 60% `death`. Crimes 12% `murder`.
Marks 44% `king`. **The bucket carrying Girard's most distinctive claim is the
second most dominated**, and dominated by a word that is not a description of a
person at all. It is a job title.

So an instrument built to detect victim-marking will in practice very largely
detect **whether a text is about royalty.** And the highest `marks` score in the
whole corpus belongs to Sophocles' *Oedipus the King*, in which the title
character is addressed as king on nearly every page.

## Do not resolve this on camera

**Two readings, both live, both coming out of the same number.**

**The objection.** The bucket is broken. It picks up a genre signal, not a victim
mark. Any text set in a palace scores high and it means nothing.

**Girard's reading.** The king **is** a marked victim, for the reasons three
paragraphs up, and a text full of kings really is full of potential victims.

> Say that this episode **cannot settle it.** What settles it is an experiment in
> **Season 4** where the word is simply deleted and everything re-run, and **the
> answer is not the one this episode makes you expect.**

## The clinical anchor

**The patient nobody is calling about.**

No family in the corridor, no relative who works in health, no one who knows which
words to use. **Nothing has been decided against them.** No individual decision on
that ward is unfair.

And they are, quietly, the one whose discharge gets pushed, whose complaint gets
recorded as agitation, whose plan changes without a conversation. **Slightly
outside, and nobody to answer to afterwards**, which is Girard's two conditions in
a corridor rather than a myth.

## What would count against this

1. **Victims in these stories who are ordinary, unmarked members of the
   community**, chosen with no distinguishing feature at all. The marks stereotype
   needs marks.
2. **The "too high" category being an artifact.** If deleting `king` destroys the
   instrument's results, the category was carrying the bucket rather than
   describing the world. **Season 4 episode 17 runs exactly this test.**
3. **The two-condition test never actually being run on anything else.** It was
   run on the king, in this episode, by Girard. **Nobody has taken a list of
   attested victims and checked what proportion satisfies both conditions**, which
   is the version of this that would be evidence rather than illustration.
