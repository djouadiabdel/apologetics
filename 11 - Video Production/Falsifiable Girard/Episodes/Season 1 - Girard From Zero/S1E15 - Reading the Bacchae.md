## The story, for someone who has never read it

**Start with the grievance, because it is the crisis and everyone skips it.**

Dionysus was born in Thebes. His mother Semele died when Zeus appeared to her in
his true form. And Semele's own sisters, **including Agave**, say she made the
god up, slept with somebody ordinary, and was killed for the lie.

So the family is split about what happened, and **the god arrives to settle a
dispute about his own paternity.** That is where the play opens: not with a
monster at the gates but with **a family that cannot agree on a fact.**

Dionysus drives the women of Thebes out onto the mountain, Agave among them.

**Pentheus**, the young king, Agave's son, treats him as a foreign agitator
corrupting the city's women and has him arrested. The prison does not hold.

And then Dionysus offers Pentheus what he wants: **to see.** To go up the
mountain and watch. And to get close, Pentheus puts on women's clothes.

The women see him. **They do not see a man.** They see a lion. And they tear him
apart with their hands, **his mother leading**, and she carries his head down into
the city on a stick, believing it is a hunting trophy, and shows it proudly to
her father, who has to walk her, slowly, to what she is holding.

## The word for it, and you will hear it for six seasons

**Sparagmos.** The tearing apart of a victim by a crowd, with hands rather than
weapons.

It matters that Greek has a word for this at all. **A culture does not coin a
noun for something that happens once**, and Girard's whole case rests on the
mechanism being ordinary enough to leave vocabulary behind.

> **Season 2 episode 9 is the day somebody nearly put that vocabulary in the
> wrong bucket**, and the reason it would have been cheating is the distinction
> from episode 7: the tearing is what the mob **does**, not what the victim is
> **accused of**.

## What to read aloud

**The messenger's speech**, and then **the recognition scene** where Cadmus takes
Agave apart piece by piece until she sees.

Do not summarise either. Read them.

## Now do the four by hand, and be honest about the hard one

| stereotype | present | where |
|---|---|---|
| **crisis** | **yes, and it is everywhere** | a family that cannot agree what happened. A mother who cannot recognise her son. A king dressed as a woman. A man seen as an animal. A god taken for a foreigner |
| **crimes** | **weak** | see below |
| **marks** | **yes** | he is the **king**, the too-high mark from episode 8 |
| **violence** | **yes, in its purest form** | the entire female population of the city, with their hands, unanimous |

> **Do the crimes row slowly, because it is the honest problem.**

Pentheus is not accused of parricide, or incest, or poisoning, or sorcery. You
can argue impiety, and it is a real reading, and it is **much thinner than the
other three.**

**But the deeper problem is this: the mob does not believe it is executing a
criminal at all. It believes it is killing a lion.** There is no accusation in
the killing, because there is no recognition in it.

> **In Girard's own best example, the accusation stereotype is the weakest of the
> four.** That is a real observation about the text, made by reading it, and it
> belongs on camera before any statistics arrive.

## And now the thing this play does to episode 12's universal negative

Episode 12 said the differentia is **"no one ever questions the guilt."** A
universal negative. One counterexample kills it.

**So look for dissent in the *Bacchae*. It is there.**

**Cadmus and Tiresias** both try to talk Pentheus into accepting the god. They
argue with him directly, on stage, at length. That is a voice against the
prevailing verdict.

**And Cadmus, at the end**, walks Agave out of her delusion, which is the most
explicit unmasking of a lynching in Greek tragedy.

**So does the play falsify Girard?**

> **Look at where the dissent sits.** Cadmus and Tiresias speak **before** the
> killing. Cadmus speaks **after** it. **Nobody speaks during.** At the moment of
> collective violence, on the mountain, the unanimity is total.

That is either a rescue or a fudge, and **you should say it is not obvious which.**
If dissent counts only when it happens at the exact moment of the killing, the
claim is much narrower than it sounded. If dissent counts whenever it appears, this
play has it and the universal negative is in trouble.

And be exact about why this is uncomfortable rather than merely open. **The
narrowing arrives after the counterexample**, which is the structure of every
rescued theory in history. A claim that said "no dissenting voice is ever heard"
meets a play with two dissenting voices in it, and the reply is that those do not
count because of where they stand in the text.

**The reply happens to be right**, and it is right for a reason internal to
Girard's mechanism: unanimity is what the mechanism requires at the moment it
operates, so dissent before and after is exactly what the theory would predict. But
notice that the argument would have been available whatever the play contained,
which is the property the whole series is watching out for.

> **Season 4 builds an instrument to ask exactly this question, whether the dissent
> language sits near the violence language, and the *Bacchae* comes out at
> p = 0.414.** Plant that here, because this episode is where the question is born,
> and it detonates in Season 4 episode 16, where the answer arrives.

## The character anchor

**King Kong.**

The crowd sees a monster. The film shows you an animal, frightened, in a city it
did not choose to enter, and the audience is never allowed to side fully with the
people doing the killing.

**The mob on the mountain is not lying about what it sees.** Agave genuinely
believes she is holding a lion. That is the part to hold on to, because it means
the accusation stereotype can be almost absent while the mechanism runs at full
strength: **you do not need to believe the victim is guilty. You need only to
believe he is not one of you.**

## The arithmetic to do on screen

The play is **17,180 words.** The killing is one messenger speech, roughly **600
words.**

Divide it. **The lynching is about 3.5% of the play.**

> Any measurement that averages across the whole text is **diluting the event by
> a factor of nearly thirty.** Understanding that single division is
> understanding half of Season 3.

## What would count against this reading

1. **The four being read into the play rather than out of it.** This is objection
   1 from episode 14, arriving immediately, on the first text. **The defence is
   that the tiers were fixed before scoring and the play still failed**, which is
   a defence about procedure rather than about reading.
2. **Cadmus and Tiresias counting as the dissent Girard says never happens.** If
   they count, the universal negative is dead on the first text anybody checks.
   **The narrowing to "during the killing" is what saves it**, and that narrowing
   is examined rather than assumed above.
3. **The accusation stereotype being genuinely absent rather than weak.** If the
   mob believes it is killing an animal, there is no accusation at all, and one of
   the four is missing from Girard's flagship case. **Season 3 episode 1 scores
   `crimes` at 0.00 on Pentheus and the reasons are arithmetic**, so this stays
   open.
