# When Everyone Wants the Same Thing

[[S1E03 - Rivals Become Copies of Each Other|← Rivals Become Copies of Each Other]] · [[S1E05 - All Against One|All Against One →]]

---
## The stake

Two people copying each other is a rivalry. A whole crowd copying each other is
the air you grew up breathing. This episode is about the wants you have never
examined because everyone around you held them first: the degree, the city, the
life plan. **Ten minutes from now, some of your certainties will have a model
standing behind them.

## Collecting the last three episodes, because this one is where they combine

**Episode 1:** desire is copied from a model, and **desire is contagious.**
**Episode 2:** the model becomes the rival, and the loop between them has no
thermostat. **Episode 3:** rivals converge until each experiences the other as a
**monstrous double**.

All of that was about **two people.** This episode is what happens when it is
everybody, and the step from two to everybody is not automatic. **It needs a
mechanism, and episode 1 already supplied it.**

## The mechanism nobody states, and it is one sentence

> **A rivalry is itself a model.**

Two people fighting intensely over something is the most powerful possible signal
that the thing is worth having. Anyone watching learns, from the fight, that
there is something here worth fighting for.

So the bystander starts wanting it. Which makes a third party. Which is an even
stronger signal. And so on.

**That is how one rivalry becomes a crisis**, and it uses nothing new: it is
episode 1's contagion running on episode 2's loop.

## The character

**Squid Game, and specifically the night in the dormitory.**

The games are the visible plot. **The dormitory is the mechanism.**

Once it is clear that everyone's survival requires everyone else's death, the
ordinary rules of the room stop working. Alliances form on nothing and dissolve
on nothing. The strong protect the weak for an hour and are hunting them by
morning. Somebody is always about to be turned on, and **which somebody is not
decided by anything you could call a reason.**

And notice: **the organisers do nothing that night.** They do not intervene, do
not provoke, do not need to. They built a situation where everyone wants the same
thing and there is not enough of it, and then they went to bed. **The crisis runs
itself.**

**Second anchor: Lord of the Flies**, where the collapse is explicitly about
**distinctions**. The conch is a difference-making device. Whoever holds it
speaks; that is the whole rule, and the entire social order is one object saying
who is different from whom right now.

Watch what happens to it. It is not argued away. It is ignored, then contested,
then it **shatters**, and when it shatters the last difference goes with it.
After that there is no chief and no assembly. Only bodies.

## The claim

Girard calls this the **sacrificial crisis**, and the important word is not
*sacrificial*. It is **crisis of distinctions**.

> **What is under threat is not peace. It is the system of differences that made
> peace possible.** Who is senior, who is kin, what is sacred, what is clean.
> **Peace is the output. Distinctions are the machine, and the machine is what
> fails.**

## Why this is not just "society breaks down"

The flattened version is: things get bad, people fight. That predicts nothing.

Girard's version is more specific and therefore more falsifiable. **The failure
has a direction.** People do not become more different and clash. They become
**less** different, and the clash is a symptom of the sameness.

**The clinical version, and episode 3 already handed you half of it.**

Atrial fibrillation was the anchor there for two rivals losing the difference
between them. **Scale it up now, because the thing that changes is the whole
point of this episode.**

In episode 3 what mattered was the pair. One node fires, the rest follow, and
when that hierarchy fails the two become interchangeable. **A crisis is the same
failure running through the entire organ at once.** Every cell fires on its own
initiative, and the output is a useless quiver.

**Here is the part that belongs to this episode and not to episode 3.** The
fibrillating heart is not doing less work than a healthy one. It is doing more.
Maximum activity, complete uniformity, and **the patient dies of too much
sameness** produced by maximum effort.

> **And not one cell in that heart is doing anything wrong.** Every one of them
> is firing exactly as a cardiac cell fires. Hold onto that, because episode 5
> is about what a society does when it needs somebody to be at fault and the
> failure is genuinely nobody's.

## The three signs a text is describing one

These come straight from the theory, and they are why the instrument in Season 2
looks the way it does. **These three become a word-list.**

| sign | why it belongs | what it looks like in a text |
|---|---|---|
| **plague or famine** | kills without regard to rank, flattening the hierarchy from outside | Thebes at the opening of *Oedipus* |
| **twins and doubles** | the difference between two specific people has failed | rival brothers, mirrored fighters |
| **civil strife** | war in which both sides are the same people | Thebes again, the brothers on the walls |

**Now say how strange that grouping is**, because this is the moment on camera.

The first is a **disease**. The second is a **fact about a family**. The third is
a **political event**. A doctor, a genealogist and a historian would file them in
three different books.

> Girard's claim is that they are **the same thing at three scales**, and that a
> text in crisis will reach for all three **without knowing why it is doing so.**

That is a real claim and it could be false. A literature could describe plagues
with no doubling and no civil strife anywhere near them.

## The number to plant, and the honest thing to say about it

The `crisis` bucket will turn out to fire at **2.07 hits per thousand words**
across 76 Greek and Roman texts.

And **60% of those hits are one word. "Death."**

Say the number, then stop.

> A bucket that is sixty percent the word *death* may be detecting a **genre**
> rather than a mechanism. Greek tragedy is about people dying. **This is the
> first crack in the instrument and it appears before a single test is run.**

**And here is the honest thing, which is better than a payoff.**

The same objection hits a second bucket. `marks` is 44% the word `king`, and that
one **does** get tested: Season 2 episode 13 shows a hospitality scene with an
empty `crimes` bucket outscoring every tragedy but one on `marks`, and
Season 4 episode 17 runs the experiment that settles it.

> **For eight rounds nothing in this project ran the equivalent test on
> `crisis`.** The 60%-death problem was noticed in July, written down, and never
> touched again.

That is not a cliffhanger. It is a **gap**, and pointing at your own untested
assumption is worth more than a resolved one.

**It has since been run, and Season 4 episode 18 is the round that runs it.**
Hold two things until then, because the pair is the point.

Deleting `king` from `marks` turned out to be **inert**. Nothing the project rests
on moved. So the obvious lesson is that a dominant word is an alarming number and
not a problem.

**Deleting `death` from `crisis` removes a third of the metric**, and the check
the project uses to validate that metric **reports no change at all.**

> Same instrument, same kind of edit, opposite outcomes. **Work out now what
> could make those two results compatible**, because the answer is a fact about
> the metric rather than about either word, and it is the best thing in Season 4.

## What would count against this

1. **Crises in literature that arrive with distinctions intact and simply escalate
   from real, stable differences.** Girard needs the differences to fail first.
2. **The three signs failing to co-occur.** If plague, doubling and civil strife
   turn out to be statistically unrelated across a large corpus, the grouping is
   arbitrary. **Season 3 measures the correlations between the buckets and the
   answer is genuinely interesting.**
3. **A community in crisis that resolves it some other way** and stays resolved.
   That is episode 5's problem.

## Handing off

The crisis has no internal brake. Everyone against everyone does not settle down
on its own, and the feedback loop from episode 2 only points one direction.

**So how does it ever stop?** Episode 5 is Girard's answer, and it is the most
famous thing he ever wrote and **the weakest joint in his whole system.**
