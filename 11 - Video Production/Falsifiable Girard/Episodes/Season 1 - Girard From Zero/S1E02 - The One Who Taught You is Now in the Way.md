# The One Who Taught You is Now in the Way

[[S1E01 - You Want it Because He Wants it|← You Want it Because He Wants it]] · [[S1E03 - Rivals Become Copies of Each Other|Rivals Become Copies of Each Other →]]

## The stake

You already have someone in the room with you. Same field, same year, same prize.
This episode explains why the closer you get to them the worse it gets, and why
admiration for someone far away never cost you a night of sleep. **If you have
ever wondered why your respect turned into rivalry without a single thing changing
but distance, this is the episode that names it.

**This is the load-bearing episode of the season.** Everything above it is setup
and everything below it is consequence.

And it only works because of the last section of episode 1. **This episode is
about internal mediation.** Models who are out of reach never do any of what
follows.

## Collecting episode 1

Episode 1 gave you the triangle. You do not want the object, you want it because a
model wants it, and the model is doing the same thing to somebody else.

**And it left the triangle stable**, which it is not. This episode is what happens
when the model and the subject reach for the same object at the same time, and the
answer is that the relationship inverts without anybody deciding to invert it.

## The character, told accurately

**Griffith and Guts**, and get the sequence right, because the lazy version
misses the whole point.

Guts does not choose Griffith. He is beaten by him and conscripted. He then
becomes the best soldier in Griffith's army, and for years his life is organised
entirely around another man's ambition.

Then Guts overhears Griffith defining what a friend is. **A friend, Griffith
says, is someone who has a dream of his own and does not depend on another
man's.**

So Guts leaves. To have a dream of his own. **Exactly as instructed.**

And Griffith, watching the man he trained walk away to become an equal, comes
apart within a single night. He goes to the princess, is caught, is imprisoned
and tortured for a year, and everything after that follows from it.

> **Read that sequence again.** Griffith's own stated instruction, obeyed
> precisely, is the thing that destroys Griffith. Nobody betrayed anybody. Nobody
> lied. **The teaching worked, and the teaching working is the catastrophe.**

**Second anchor, gentler and cleaner: Ippo and Miyata.** Miyata shows Ippo what a
boxer is. Ippo's entire drive is organised around a promise to fight Miyata one
day. So the person who gave him the goal **is** the goal, and the story cannot
resolve that by anyone being nicer or more generous.

## The claim

The model shows you what to want. Wanting the same thing puts you in the model's
way. So **the model becomes the rival automatically, by the same mechanism that
made him the model.**

Girard's term is the **double bind**. The model is saying two things at once and
means both:

- **Imitate me.** This is what is worth wanting.
- **Do not take it.** It is mine.

**The follower cannot obey both.** Obeying the first is disobeying the second.

And Berserk stages it literally: *have a dream of your own* and *do not leave
me*, said by the same man, and the second is never said out loud because he does
not know he means it.

## The feedback loop, which is the engine and is usually left out

Here is the part that makes this a mechanism rather than an observation, and most
summaries skip it.

**Why does the model mind being imitated?** He should be flattered. Often he is,
at first.

But watch what imitation does to the model. **The disciple's wanting is
evidence.** If somebody else wants what I have, that confirms it was worth
wanting. My own valuation goes up. I hold it tighter.

And the disciple sees the model holding it tighter, which is a stronger signal
that the object matters, so the disciple wants it more, so the model tightens
again.

```
   disciple imitates  ->  model's valuation rises  ->  model becomes possessive
          ^                                                     |
          |_____________  disciple wants it more  <_____________|
```

> **Nobody adds anything to this system from outside. It powers itself, and it
> only goes one direction: up.**

That is why Girard's rivalries do not settle down. There is no thermostat in the
loop.

## Why this is the hinge of the entire season

Write this sentence down:

> **Rivalry does not require anyone to be at fault. It is produced by successful
> teaching.**

That is what makes the theory worth anything. Any account of conflict that needs
a villain explains less, because it cannot explain the conflicts where nobody is
a villain **and everyone involved can see nobody is.**

**Zuko and Ozai is the version where there obviously IS a villain and the
structure still explains more than the villain does.** Zuko's problem is not that
his father is cruel, though he is. It is that he has been taught to want his
father's approval, and **the only person who can grant it is the only person
standing in the way of him becoming someone who does not need it.**

Notice that Ozai could become kind tomorrow and Zuko's problem would not resolve.
That is the test of whether you have a structural claim or a moral one.

## The object stops mattering

When the model becomes the rival, **the thing they are fighting over thins out
until it is a token.**

Watch what Vegeta is actually training for by the end. Watch what Guts and
Griffith are actually in conflict about. The stated object keeps being replaced
and the intensity does not drop, which is the signature.

Girard's name for the far end of this is **metaphysical desire**: what is wanted
is no longer any object at all but **the model's being.** Not what he has. What
he is.

And that explains something the object-level reading cannot: **why victory does
not help.** If you wanted the object, winning it ends the matter. If you wanted
his being, winning the object leaves you holding a thing while he still exists,
and the wanting is unchanged.

> Salieri gets everything a composer of his era could want: the post, the
> income, the Emperor's ear. **It does nothing whatever for him**, and the film is
> two hours of explaining why.

**Tyler Durden is the clean structural case**, because the narrator's model and
the narrator's rival turn out to be literally the same person, and the film is
about how long that can stay invisible from the inside.

## The clinical anchor

**The registrar and the consultant.**

The person who teaches you what competent practice looks like is the same person
who decides whether you have reached it. **You cannot learn the standard from
anybody else and you cannot be signed off by anybody else**, so admiration and
obstruction arrive through one door.

And notice that nobody has behaved badly for this to bite. The consultant is doing
his job. **The structure produces the resentment**, which is why it shows up in
people who like their supervisor and cannot explain why they are angry.

## What would count against this

**Three things would break it**, and the audience should have them:

1. **A rivalry that cools as the two get closer and more alike.** Girard predicts
   the opposite. If proximity reliably calmed rivals, the mechanism is wrong.
2. **A model who is imitated intensively and becomes genuinely more generous with
   the object over time**, with no strain. The feedback loop above says his
   valuation should rise.
3. **Winning ending it.** If people who obtain the contested object routinely
   report the matter closed, metaphysical desire is a fiction.

> And say the honest thing again, as in episode 1: **nothing in this project
> tests any of the three.** The instrument starts at episode 6.
