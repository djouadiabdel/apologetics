# What Girard Says the Gospels Do

[[S1E12 - Myth is the Story the Crowd Tells Itself|← Myth is the Story the Crowd Tells Itself]] · [[S1E14 - Who Says He is Wrong|Who Says He is Wrong →]]

## The stake

The strangest claim in the season, and the one Girard staked everything on: that
the Gospels are not myth but the same events told from the victim's side. You do
not have to believe it. **You do have to notice that it is checkable**, because
the difference between the two story versions is concrete enough to test, and the
project tests it later.

## Collecting episode 12, and reusing its diagram

Episode 12 gave you two versions of the same events.

**What the myth says:**

```
he committed the crime  ->  so we expelled him  ->  so the plague lifted
```

**What Girard reconstructs underneath it:**

```
the crisis  ->  we needed somebody  ->  we expelled him  ->  the crisis ended
                                                                    |
                                     so he must have caused it  <----+
```

The second one is a **reconstruction.** Girard has to argue for it, because no
myth says it.

> **Girard's claim about the Gospels is that they narrate the second diagram
> directly.** Not as a reconstruction. As the story.

That is the entire episode, and everything else is consequence.

## The claim, and it is smaller and stranger than people expect

Girard is **not** saying the Gospels describe a different kind of killing.

He is saying they describe **the same kind of killing**. Same mechanism, same
crowd, same arbitrary selection, same manufactured guilt.

What differs is where the camera stands. They narrate it from
**the position the myth has to suppress**: the victim's, and the position of the
few who do not join in.

*I See Satan Fall Like Lightning*, page 122:

> "it is a rebellious minority, a small group of dissidents that separates from
> the collective violence of the crowd and **destroys its unanimity**. This
> minority group is the community of the first witnesses to the Resurrection,
> that is, the apostles and those who gather around them. **This dissident
> minority has no equivalent in the myths.**"

## The character anchor, and it is the same one

**The Usual Suspects again, deliberately, from episode 12, with the last ninety
seconds moved to the front.**

Same events. Same participants. Same crime. But the telling begins from the
knowledge that the crowd's account is a construction, and **once you have that,
the entire story reads differently without a single fact changing.**

That is the structural claim, and it is worth making the audience feel it before
any theology arrives:

> **The difference between myth and Gospel, on this account, is not what
> happened. It is who is holding the camera.**

## The structural consequence, and it is the thing most people get backwards

**If Girard is right, the Gospels should score HIGH on all four stereotypes. Not
low.**

Run the list from episodes 6 to 9 against the Passion and check:

| stereotype | present in the Passion? |
|---|---|
| **crisis** | a city under occupation, a festival crowd, a religious authority in conflict with a popular teacher |
| **crimes** | **blasphemy**, which is Girard's third crime family exactly |
| **marks** | a provincial, a carpenter's son, **and simultaneously a claimed king**. Both directions from episode 8 |
| **violence** | a crowd, calling out together, and a public execution |

**All four. Emphatically.**

> **So the four-bucket instrument built in Season 2 can never tell a Gospel from
> a myth, and it was never going to.** That is **the design, not a defect**, and
> the project wrote it down in July before it became inconvenient.

Say that on camera, because it is the kind of limitation people usually discover
late and then present as a nuance.

## So where is the difference

Girard says exactly where, and it is the one place: **the unanimity breaks.**

Somebody says he has done nothing. Somebody washes his hands. Somebody follows at
a distance. The crowd is reported as **divided** rather than as one voice.

And in myth, on Girard's account, **that never happens anywhere.**

> Which is why the discriminating instrument, when it is finally built in Season
> 3, is not about violence at all. It is a word-list for **crowds coming apart.**

## The ordinal series, and it is the most tested claim in the project

Girard treats tragedy as **partway** toward revelation. Tragedy knows something is
wrong. It does not know what.

So the prediction is not a binary. **It is an ordering:**

```
myth   <   tragedy   <   Gospel        in dissent, in broken unanimity
```

**Plant this and do not explain it further. It detonates in Season 3 episode 18.**

> This series is the **single most tested claim in the entire project.** It was
> attacked **four times with four different instruments** and survived **none** of
> them.

Notice why an ordering is a better thing to predict than a difference. **A binary
claim has one way to be right and one way to be wrong.** An ordering of three tiers
has six possible arrangements and Girard is committed to exactly one of them, so
five of the six outcomes are losses. The theory is being made to take on more risk
than it has to, deliberately, and that is the whole point of the exercise.

And the reason it failed is not the reason anyone expected. **It did not fail
because the Gospels came out low.** It failed because **tragedy and myth turned out
to be indistinguishable from each other**, which nobody predicted and nobody has
explained.

**That detonates in Season 3 episode 18**, where a round's own sensitivity analysis
shows the **middle of the series does not exist.**

## The objection you must raise yourself, in this episode

There is an obvious one and it should come from you, not from a comment.

**A Christian testing whether the Gospels are special is not a neutral party.**

That is not a smear. It is simply true, and pretending otherwise is worse than
admitting it. The person who built this instrument wanted a particular answer.

**The project's response is not a denial.** It is a set of procedures a hostile
reader can check **without trusting anybody**: predictions committed before
scoring, falsifiers with numbers fixed in advance, sensitivity analyses declared
before the data existed, and a public record of every occasion the procedure
caught the person running it.

> Say it plainly here: **you are not asking anyone to trust you. You are asking
> them to check.** Season 7 is entirely about those procedures and the eight
> times they fired.

## The clinical anchor

**The near-miss reported by the person who made the error.**

Same event as the incident report in episode 12. Same facts, same timeline, same
outcome. **Told from the position the other document structurally cannot occupy.**

And notice what makes it valuable, because it is not accuracy. The incident report
may well be more accurate. **What the near-miss has is a narrator who knows the
mechanism operated**, and that is the entire difference Girard is pointing at, in a
form you can hold in your hand.

## What would count against this

1. **A myth with a sustained dissenting voice**, which kills the universal
   negative from episode 12 and takes this episode with it.
2. **The Gospels showing no more dissent language than myth.** The whole
   distinction is empirical and it can simply fail to appear.
3. **Tragedy failing to sit in the middle.** Girard needs the gradient. **This is
   the one that actually happens.**
