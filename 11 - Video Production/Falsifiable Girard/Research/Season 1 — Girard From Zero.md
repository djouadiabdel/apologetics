@@ THE SKELETON — READ THIS BEFORE ANYTHING ELSE

Not what happens. What the moving parts are, and which ones carry weight.

## The four moving parts

Girard's whole system is four claims stacked, and each one depends on the one
under it. If the bottom one is wrong the rest collapses, so the order matters.

| # | the part | what it claims | if it is false |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Mimetic desire** | you want things because someone else wants them, not because of what they are | nothing above survives |
| 2 | **The model becomes the rival** | the person who taught you to want it is now standing in front of it | conflict needs a different explanation |
| 3 | **The crisis** | when enough people want the same thing, the differences that kept order collapse | there is nothing to resolve |
| 4 | **The scapegoat mechanism** | the crisis ends when everyone turns on one victim, and the peace afterwards makes the victim look guilty | myth needs a different explanation |

## Which part is load-bearing

**Part 2 is the hinge and almost nobody notices it.**

Part 1 on its own is a familiar idea. Advertising has run on it for a century.
Part 3 and 4 are the famous parts, the ones people quote.

But part 2 is where the theory earns anything. **Desire that is copied turns the
teacher into the obstacle automatically**, without anyone choosing it, without
anyone being a villain. That is the sentence carrying the weight, and if you only
remember one thing from this season it is that one.

Everything the instrument in Season 2 tries to detect is downstream of part 2.

## What each episode is doing structurally

| ep | what it is | function |
|---|---|---|
| 1-3 | the mechanism, built one piece at a time | the foundation |
| 4-5 | the mechanism running to its end | the payoff |
| 6-8 | the four stereotypes | **the part that becomes measurable** |
| 9-10 | the claim about myth, and the claim about the Gospels | **the part that gets tested and mostly fails** |
| 11 | **who says he is wrong, and why** | so the series is not a fan club |
| 12-15 | the texts themselves, read | so the corpus is not an abstraction |

Episodes 6 to 8 are the ones Season 2 turns into code. Episodes 9 and 10 are the
ones Seasons 3 and 4 attack. Episodes 1 to 5 are never tested by anything in this
project, and that limitation is stated in episode 5 rather than hidden.

## The one rule for this whole season

**Every concept gets a character you already know.** Not Stendhal's Julien Sorel,
not Proust's Mme Verdurin, which is how the books explain it and how nobody
learns it. If a concept has no character, the concept is not ready to film.

@@ EPISODE 1 — YOU WANT IT BECAUSE HE WANTS IT

## The first thirty seconds

>% There is something you want right now that you did not want three years ago.
>% You can say why you want it, and the reason is good, and it is your reason.
>% Now try to remember the first time you saw it, and who was holding it.
>% Most people cannot. That is not a memory problem.
>% Watch what happens when you look.

## The stake

Before any theory: this episode is about the wanting itself, and you are doing it
right now. Somewhere in your life there is a thing you want that you never wanted
before a specific person wanted it first, and you have experienced that wanting as
entirely your own. **By the end of these ten minutes you will be able to find it.**
That is the whole promise of the episode.

## The character

**Vegeta.**

And be careful with him, because the lazy version of this example is wrong and an
audience will catch it.

**The lazy version:** Vegeta only wants what Goku wants. That is false. Vegeta had
goals before Goku mattered to him. He wanted immortality. He wanted to be the
strongest Saiyan. He had a whole identity built on being royalty.

**What actually happens is more interesting.** A low-class Saiyan turns out to be
stronger than the prince. And from that moment Vegeta's existing ambition is
**re-routed.** He does not acquire a new goal. Every goal he already had gets
re-expressed as a comparison with one specific person.

>> **That is the more accurate claim and the more useful one: the model does not
>> hand you a desire. The model gives your desire a scoreboard.**

You can prove it from the story. Every time Vegeta reaches a level he previously
called the summit, he is not satisfied for a single episode, because Goku reached
it too. **The target moves because the target was never a number.** It was a
person.

**Second anchor: Salieri in *Amadeus*.** He does not want music. He wants to be
the one who has it. When he sees that Mozart's manuscript has no corrections, he
does not hear beauty. He hears a verdict on himself.

Hold Salieri, because he comes back at the end of this episode and he is doing
something Vegeta is not.

## The claim, stated plainly

Girard's first move is that desire has **three** parts, not two.

The ordinary picture has two: a **subject** who wants, and an **object** wanted.

Girard says that for almost everything humans fight over there is a third part: a
**model**, sometimes called a mediator. The model wants the object, or seems to,
or simply has it. And the subject learns to want it from the model.

```
                    MODEL
                   /     \
        imitates  /       \  wants
                 /         \
            SUBJECT ------- OBJECT
                  the visible line
```

The line from subject to object is the one everybody sees. **The other two do the
work.**

**Keep the triangle on screen to the end of the episode**, because in episode 2 it deforms, and the
whole hinge of the season is what happens to the top vertex.

## What this does NOT cover, and the theory is worthless without this

Here is the objection that arrives in the first thirty seconds, and the episode
must answer it or the whole thing is over-broad.

**You want water because you are thirsty.** Nobody modelled that. A newborn wants
milk with no social input whatever. If mimetic desire covers everything, it
explains nothing.

**Girard's answer is a distinction, and you should state it hard.**

| | driven by | example | can two people share it peacefully? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **appetite / need** | the body | thirst, hunger, sleep, warmth | **yes**, if there is enough |
| **desire** | a model | status, a career, a person, recognition | **no**, and the reason is the next section |

>> **The theory is about the second column only.** Somebody who says "Girard
>> thinks I only eat because others eat" has been handed the theory badly. **Say
>> the limit out loud in episode 1**, because the credibility of everything after
>> it depends on the audience knowing you know where it stops.

## Why this is not just "people are envious"

Envy is a feeling somebody has. Girard is not describing a feeling. He is
describing a **structure that produces the feeling**, and it runs whether or not
anyone involved is a bad person.

**The clinical version**, which may land faster. Nobody chooses to have referred
pain. Gallbladder disease presents in the right shoulder. The patient is not
confused and is not lying. The wiring routes it there.

Mimetic desire is a claim of that kind: **the wanting is routed through someone
else, and the person doing the wanting experiences it as their own, directly,
with no sense of a detour.**

Vegeta does not think "I am copying Goku." He experiences pure, first-person,
burning ambition.

>> The structure is **invisible from inside it**, and that is not an inconvenient
>> side effect. The theory requires it, because a mechanism everyone could see
>> operating would stop working.

**Mark that as a tier-K claim on camera.** Girard says this repeatedly across
several books and this series has not gone and verified the page. **Say "I have
not checked the page for this one"** rather than implying you have. That habit
costs you nothing here and it is the habit the entire series is about.

## The distinction that decides everything after this

**This is the load-bearing section of episode 1**, and it is the one most
summaries of Girard leave out, which is why most summaries make episode 2 sound
false.

Not every model becomes a rival. Girard says which ones do, and it depends on one
thing: **can you and the model actually collide?**

**External mediation.** The model is out of reach. A dead legend. A fictional
hero. A star you will never meet. A kid who trains like his favourite fighter is
imitating a model, thoroughly, and **no rivalry is possible**, because the model
is not competing for anything the kid can touch. This kind is stable. It can last
a lifetime. It is mostly benign and it is what we usually call admiration.

**Internal mediation.** The model is **in your world**. Same room, same field,
same year, same prize. Now the imitation puts you on a collision course, because
what he wants is finite and you are now reaching for it.

**And now go back to Salieri, because this is why that story is a tragedy rather
than a career.**

>> If Mozart had lived two hundred years before Salieri, Salieri could have
>> worshipped him for a lifetime and been happy. **The problem is not Mozart's
>> genius. The problem is that Mozart is in the next room, at the same court,
>> competing for the same commissions.** Same admiration, moved close, becomes
>> poison.

**Vegeta is internal mediation.** Goku is right there, same species, same fights,
same scoreboard.

>> **Episode 2 is entirely about internal mediation.** When you hear "the model
>> becomes the rival," it does not mean every model. It means the ones close
>> enough to be reached.

## Two consequences that are not obvious

**Desire is contagious.** If wanting is learned from a model, it spreads, and one
person wanting a thing makes it more wantable. **Notice that this is a real
prediction and it could be false.** If wanting came from the object's properties,
crowd interest would tell you nothing about whether you should want it. Anyone
who has watched an auction, a bidding war on a house, or a queue forming outside
a shop has watched the prediction hold.

**Desire has nothing to reach.** If the object was only ever a signal of the
model's attention, then getting it does not end anything. Vegeta beats the level
and the level moves. **And this one is testable in your own life**, which is what
question 1 is for.

## What would count against all this

The series is about falsifiability, so episode 1 has to model the question rather
than just asserting a theory.

**What would break mimetic desire?** Three candidates, and say them:

1. A desire that is **intense, sustained and socially costly**, held by someone
   with no model available, in a domain nobody around them values.
2. Getting the object producing **lasting satisfaction**, routinely, rather than a
   moved target.
3. Rivalry that **does not intensify** as the two parties get closer and more
   similar. Girard predicts it should get worse, not better.

>> **And here is the honest part. Nothing in this project tests any of the
>> three.** The instrument built in Season 2 starts at the four stereotypes, which
>> are episodes 6 to 9. **Everything before that is a premise the instrument
>> assumes rather than tests**, and a critic will say so if you do not.

> **And here is the promise that pays for the wait.** Before this season ends you
> will watch the instrument touch four real texts, and in the last episode you
> will watch one of them break it: the least persecution-shaped story in Greek
> literature will produce the most persecution-shaped score in the corpus. **You
> will see the failure with your own eyes, and you will see what saves the
> instrument.** That is the deal: no test until episode 18, and a real one when
> it comes.

## Handing off

Episode 2 takes the diagram and breaks it. **Watch the top vertex.** The model
does not stay where it is drawn, and nobody moves it deliberately.

@@ EPISODE 2 — THE ONE WHO TAUGHT YOU IS NOW IN THE WAY

## The first thirty seconds

>% A man tells his best soldier what a friend is. A friend, he says, is
>% someone with a dream of his own, who does not live inside another man's.
>% The soldier hears it and leaves, to go and have one. That night the man
>% who said it comes apart completely. Nobody lied. Nobody betrayed anybody.
>% The instruction was obeyed exactly.

## The stake

You already have someone in the room with you. Same field, same year, same prize.
This episode explains why the closer you get to them the worse it gets, and why
admiration for someone far away never cost you a night of sleep. **If you have
ever wondered why your respect turned into rivalry without a single thing changing
but distance, this is the episode that names it.

**This is the load-bearing episode of the season.** Everything above it is setup
and everything below it is consequence.

And it only works because of the last section of episode 1. **This episode is
about internal mediation.** Models who are out of reach never do any of what
follows.

## Collecting episode 1

Episode 1 gave you the triangle. You do not want the object, you want it because a
model wants it, and the model is doing the same thing to somebody else.

**And it left the triangle stable**, which it is not. This episode is what happens
when the model and the subject reach for the same object at the same time, and the
answer is that the relationship inverts without anybody deciding to invert it.

## The character, told accurately

**Griffith and Guts**, and get the sequence right, because the lazy version
misses the whole point.

Guts does not choose Griffith. He is beaten by him and conscripted. He then
becomes the best soldier in Griffith's army, and for years his life is organised
entirely around another man's ambition.

Then Guts overhears Griffith defining what a friend is. **A friend, Griffith
says, is someone who has a dream of his own and does not depend on another
man's.**

So Guts leaves. To have a dream of his own. **Exactly as instructed.**

And Griffith, watching the man he trained walk away to become an equal, comes
apart within a single night. He goes to the princess, is caught, is imprisoned
and tortured for a year, and everything after that follows from it.

>> **Read that sequence again.** Griffith's own stated instruction, obeyed
>> precisely, is the thing that destroys Griffith. Nobody betrayed anybody. Nobody
>> lied. **The teaching worked, and the teaching working is the catastrophe.**

**Second anchor, gentler and cleaner: Ippo and Miyata.** Miyata shows Ippo what a
boxer is. Ippo's entire drive is organised around a promise to fight Miyata one
day. So the person who gave him the goal **is** the goal, and the story cannot
resolve that by anyone being nicer or more generous.

## The claim

The model shows you what to want. Wanting the same thing puts you in the model's
way. So **the model becomes the rival automatically, by the same mechanism that
made him the model.**

Girard's term is the **double bind**. The model is saying two things at once and
means both:

- **Imitate me.** This is what is worth wanting.
- **Do not take it.** It is mine.

**The follower cannot obey both.** Obeying the first is disobeying the second.

And Berserk stages it literally: *have a dream of your own* and *do not leave
me*, said by the same man, and the second is never said out loud because he does
not know he means it.

## The feedback loop, which is the engine and is usually left out

Here is the part that makes this a mechanism rather than an observation, and most
summaries skip it.

**Why does the model mind being imitated?** He should be flattered. Often he is,
at first.

But watch what imitation does to the model. **The disciple's wanting is
evidence.** If somebody else wants what I have, that confirms it was worth
wanting. My own valuation goes up. I hold it tighter.

And the disciple sees the model holding it tighter, which is a stronger signal
that the object matters, so the disciple wants it more, so the model tightens
again.

```
   disciple imitates  ->  model's valuation rises  ->  model becomes possessive
          ^                                                     |
          |_____________  disciple wants it more  <_____________|
```

>> **Nobody adds anything to this system from outside. It powers itself, and it
>> only goes one direction: up.**

That is why Girard's rivalries do not settle down. There is no thermostat in the
loop.

## Why this is the hinge of the entire season

Write this sentence down:

>> **Rivalry does not require anyone to be at fault. It is produced by successful
>> teaching.**

That is what makes the theory worth anything. Any account of conflict that needs
a villain explains less, because it cannot explain the conflicts where nobody is
a villain **and everyone involved can see nobody is.**

**Zuko and Ozai is the version where there obviously IS a villain and the
structure still explains more than the villain does.** Zuko's problem is not that
his father is cruel, though he is. It is that he has been taught to want his
father's approval, and **the only person who can grant it is the only person
standing in the way of him becoming someone who does not need it.**

Notice that Ozai could become kind tomorrow and Zuko's problem would not resolve.
That is the test of whether you have a structural claim or a moral one.

## The object stops mattering

When the model becomes the rival, **the thing they are fighting over thins out
until it is a token.**

Watch what Vegeta is actually training for by the end. Watch what Guts and
Griffith are actually in conflict about. The stated object keeps being replaced
and the intensity does not drop, which is the signature.

Girard's name for the far end of this is **metaphysical desire**: what is wanted
is no longer any object at all but **the model's being.** Not what he has. What
he is.

And that explains something the object-level reading cannot: **why victory does
not help.** If you wanted the object, winning it ends the matter. If you wanted
his being, winning the object leaves you holding a thing while he still exists,
and the wanting is unchanged.

>> Salieri gets everything a composer of his era could want: the post, the
>> income, the Emperor's ear. **It does nothing whatever for him**, and the film is
>> two hours of explaining why.

**Tyler Durden is the clean structural case**, because the narrator's model and
the narrator's rival turn out to be literally the same person, and the film is
about how long that can stay invisible from the inside.

## The clinical anchor

**The registrar and the consultant.**

The person who teaches you what competent practice looks like is the same person
who decides whether you have reached it. **You cannot learn the standard from
anybody else and you cannot be signed off by anybody else**, so admiration and
obstruction arrive through one door.

And notice that nobody has behaved badly for this to bite. The consultant is doing
his job. **The structure produces the resentment**, which is why it shows up in
people who like their supervisor and cannot explain why they are angry.

## What would count against this

**Three things would break it**, and the audience should have them:

1. **A rivalry that cools as the two get closer and more alike.** Girard predicts
   the opposite. If proximity reliably calmed rivals, the mechanism is wrong.
2. **A model who is imitated intensively and becomes genuinely more generous with
   the object over time**, with no strain. The feedback loop above says his
   valuation should rise.
3. **Winning ending it.** If people who obtain the contested object routinely
   report the matter closed, metaphysical desire is a fiction.

>> And say the honest thing again, as in episode 1: **nothing in this project
>> tests any of the three.** The instrument starts at episode 6.

## Handing off

The diagram from episode 1 has now deformed. The model has moved onto the line
between the subject and the object.

**Episode 3 asks what happens to the two people themselves** when that
arrangement runs for a while, and the answer is the opposite of what everyone
expects.

@@ EPISODE 3 — RIVALS BECOME COPIES OF EACH OTHER

## The first thirty seconds

>% Two boys start a story as opposites. One has nothing and wants to be
>% seen. One has everything and wants to be left alone. Anybody could tell
>% you the difference. Then they fight, on two statues, at either end of a
>% gorge, mirroring each other's moves. And a viewer coming in cold cannot
>% tell you which one is the hero.

## The stake

The uncomfortable one. This episode says the rival you hate is the person you were
trying to become, and that the hatred is evidence of the aiming. You will resist
that sentence, which is correct: **the episode predicts your resistance and counts
it as data.

## Start by collecting episode 2, because this episode is its consequence

Episode 2 ended somewhere specific. **What is wanted is no longer the object. It
is the model's being.** Not what he has. What he is.

Now just follow that.

>> **If what you want is to be him, then the closer you get to wanting it, the
>> more you resemble him.** Not as a side effect. As the thing you were aiming at
>> the whole time.

That is this entire episode in one line, and the episode exists because the
conclusion sounds wrong to everybody the first time they hear it.

## The character

**Naruto and Sasuke at the Valley of the End.**

Look at what the fight actually shows you, visually, before any dialogue. They
mirror each other's moves. They stand on **two statues** at either end of a
gorge. The frame is symmetrical. Each is the other's entire reason for being
there.

At the beginning of the series they are opposites and any viewer can say what the
difference is: one has nothing and wants to be seen, the other has everything and
wants to be left alone.

**By that fight, an audience member who did not know the show could not tell you
from the fight alone which one is supposed to be the hero.**

**Second anchor: Light Yagami and L.** They begin as pure opposites, the criminal
and the detective, and converge until each is doing the other's job. L breaks the
law and abducts and tortures. Light investigates the Kira case. **Each becomes
functionally indistinguishable from the thing he is opposing**, and the show is
explicit about it.

## The claim, with the step that usually gets skipped

Girard's word is **doubles**. Rivalry does not preserve the difference that
started it. **It destroys it.**

The lazy version is: A copies B, B copies back, they converge. But **why would B
copy back?** He was there first. He has the thing. What does he have to imitate?

**Episode 2 already answered this and it is worth spending the time.** The
feedback loop:

```
   disciple imitates  ->  model's valuation rises  ->  model becomes possessive
          ^                                                     |
          |_____________  disciple wants it more  <_____________|
```

Once that loop is running, **the model is watching the disciple to know how much
to want the thing.** He is taking his cue from the person who took a cue from
him. At that point the direction of imitation has stopped being a fact about
either of them.

>> **The more intense the opposition, the more precisely each mirrors the other**,
>> because each is now using the other as the template for how hard to want it.

## The one-sided case, which is a real objection and belongs in the episode

**Vegeta and Goku do not converge**, and if you have been paying attention that
should bother you.

Here is why, and it sharpens the claim rather than weakening it.

**Goku does not reciprocate.** He is not measuring himself against Vegeta. He is
not taking his cue from Vegeta's wanting. The loop above requires two participants
and Goku never joins it.

>> **So mediation without reciprocity does not produce doubles.** It produces one
>> obsessed person and one person who barely notices.

That is a genuine prediction and you should say it as one: **the theory predicts
convergence only where the rivalry runs both ways.** If you find two people
locked in mutual, escalating rivalry who become steadily **more** different over
time, that is a problem for Girard. If you find a one-sided obsession that never
converges, **that is what the theory expects.**

## The counter-intuitive bit, which is the whole point

Everybody assumes conflict comes from difference. Different values, different
groups, different beliefs, and then a clash.

**Girard says the opposite. Acute conflict comes from the collapse of
difference**, and people fight hardest across the smallest gap.

Freud has a version of this, the narcissism of small differences. **Mark that as
tier K on camera**, and since this is the first time the series says that, here is
what the two tiers mean. **You will hear them for the next seven seasons.**

| tier | what it means |
|---|---|
| **V** | source fetched, citation checked, **quote verified against the original** |
| **K** | the argument is real and the person is real, **the citation is unverified** |

>> **Tier K is a lead, not a fact.** It is not a hedge and it is not modesty. It
>> means somebody has told you a thing and nobody has opened the book, and the
>> series says so out loud rather than letting a page number do work it has not
>> earned.

The Freud phrase is real and this series has not gone and checked the essay it
comes from. Girard's version is structural rather than
psychological, and that is the difference worth stating.

**The clinical version, and it is exact.**

Atrial fibrillation is not the heart doing something varied. It is the heart
**losing the differentiation between the pacemaker and everything else.**
Normally one node fires and the rest follow, and that hierarchy is what produces
coordinated output.

In fibrillation every cell fires on its own. Maximum activity. **Complete
uniformity.** And the output is a useless quiver, and the patient dies of **too
much sameness, not too much difference.**

>> That is the shape Girard means. Not chaos as variety. **Chaos as
>> undifferentiation.**

## And when it goes far enough, the double becomes a monster

One more step, because it is the bridge to episode 5.

Girard has a term for the end state: the **monstrous double**. When two rivals
have become fully interchangeable, each experiences the other as something that
should not exist. Not a person with a different view. **A thing wearing your
face.**

That is why the language of these conflicts reaches for the inhuman. The enemy
becomes a beast, a demon, a plague. **Not because he has become more different,
but because he has become identical and the mind has no category for that.**

**Hold this.** In episode 5 an entire community will do the same thing to one
person.

## What this predicts about texts, and it is the first thing this project can test

If rivalry erases difference, then texts about rivalry should contain vocabulary
about **things losing their distinctness**. Twins. Doubles. Likeness. Sameness.

And beyond the pair, the same failure at whole-society scale: **plague**, which
kills without regard to rank, and **civil war**, which is a war where both sides
are the same people.

**That vocabulary is exactly what the `crisis` bucket in Season 2 is built out
of.** Plague, pestilence, contagion, famine, sterility, twin, double, likeness,

@@ EPISODE 4 — WHEN EVERYONE WANTS THE SAME THING

## The first thirty seconds

>% There is a night in Squid Game where the guards do nothing. They do not
>% intervene, they do not provoke, they go to bed. In the dormitory,
>% alliances form on nothing and break on nothing, and the strong are
>% hunting the weak by morning. Nobody organised it. They built a room where
>% everyone wants the same thing, and left.

## The stake

Two people copying each other is a rivalry. A whole crowd copying each other is
the air you grew up breathing. This episode is about the wants you have never
examined because everyone around you held them first: the degree, the city, the
life plan. **Ten minutes from now, some of your certainties will have a model
standing behind them.

## Collecting the last three episodes, because this one is where they combine

**Episode 1:** desire is copied from a model, and **desire is contagious.**
**Episode 2:** the model becomes the rival, and the loop between them has no
thermostat. **Episode 3:** rivals converge until each experiences the other as a
**monstrous double**.

All of that was about **two people.** This episode is what happens when it is
everybody, and the step from two to everybody is not automatic. **It needs a
mechanism, and episode 1 already supplied it.**

## The mechanism nobody states, and it is one sentence

>> **A rivalry is itself a model.**

Two people fighting intensely over something is the most powerful possible signal
that the thing is worth having. Anyone watching learns, from the fight, that
there is something here worth fighting for.

So the bystander starts wanting it. Which makes a third party. Which is an even
stronger signal. And so on.

**That is how one rivalry becomes a crisis**, and it uses nothing new: it is
episode 1's contagion running on episode 2's loop.

## The character

**Squid Game, and specifically the night in the dormitory.**

The games are the visible plot. **The dormitory is the mechanism.**

Once it is clear that everyone's survival requires everyone else's death, the
ordinary rules of the room stop working. Alliances form on nothing and dissolve
on nothing. The strong protect the weak for an hour and are hunting them by
morning. Somebody is always about to be turned on, and **which somebody is not
decided by anything you could call a reason.**

And notice: **the organisers do nothing that night.** They do not intervene, do
not provoke, do not need to. They built a situation where everyone wants the same
thing and there is not enough of it, and then they went to bed. **The crisis runs
itself.**

**Second anchor: Lord of the Flies**, where the collapse is explicitly about
**distinctions**. The conch is a difference-making device. Whoever holds it
speaks; that is the whole rule, and the entire social order is one object saying
who is different from whom right now.

Watch what happens to it. It is not argued away. It is ignored, then contested,
then it **shatters**, and when it shatters the last difference goes with it.
After that there is no chief and no assembly. Only bodies.

## The claim

Girard calls this the **sacrificial crisis**, and the important word is not
*sacrificial*. It is **crisis of distinctions**.

>> **What is under threat is not peace. It is the system of differences that made
>> peace possible.** Who is senior, who is kin, what is sacred, what is clean.
>> **Peace is the output. Distinctions are the machine, and the machine is what
>> fails.**

## Why this is not just "society breaks down"

The flattened version is: things get bad, people fight. That predicts nothing.

Girard's version is more specific and therefore more falsifiable. **The failure
has a direction.** People do not become more different and clash. They become
**less** different, and the clash is a symptom of the sameness.

**The clinical version, and episode 3 already handed you half of it.**

Atrial fibrillation was the anchor there for two rivals losing the difference
between them. **Scale it up now, because the thing that changes is the whole
point of this episode.**

In episode 3 what mattered was the pair. One node fires, the rest follow, and
when that hierarchy fails the two become interchangeable. **A crisis is the same
failure running through the entire organ at once.** Every cell fires on its own
initiative, and the output is a useless quiver.

**Here is the part that belongs to this episode and not to episode 3.** The
fibrillating heart is not doing less work than a healthy one. It is doing more.
Maximum activity, complete uniformity, and **the patient dies of too much
sameness** produced by maximum effort.

>> **And not one cell in that heart is doing anything wrong.** Every one of them
>> is firing exactly as a cardiac cell fires. Hold onto that, because episode 5
>> is about what a society does when it needs somebody to be at fault and the
>> failure is genuinely nobody's.

## The three signs a text is describing one

These come straight from the theory, and they are why the instrument in Season 2
looks the way it does. **These three become a word-list.**

| sign | why it belongs | what it looks like in a text |
|---|---|---|
| **plague or famine** | kills without regard to rank, flattening the hierarchy from outside | Thebes at the opening of *Oedipus* |
| **twins and doubles** | the difference between two specific people has failed | rival brothers, mirrored fighters |
| **civil strife** | war in which both sides are the same people | Thebes again, the brothers on the walls |

**Now say how strange that grouping is**, because this is the moment on camera.

The first is a **disease**. The second is a **fact about a family**. The third is
a **political event**. A doctor, a genealogist and a historian would file them in
three different books.

>> Girard's claim is that they are **the same thing at three scales**, and that a
>> text in crisis will reach for all three **without knowing why it is doing so.**

That is a real claim and it could be false. A literature could describe plagues
with no doubling and no civil strife anywhere near them.

The `crisis` bucket will turn out to fire at **2.07 hits per thousand words**
across 76 Greek and Roman texts.

And **60% of those hits are one word. "Death."**

Say the number, then stop.

>> A bucket that is sixty percent the word *death* may be detecting a **genre**
>> rather than a mechanism. Greek tragedy is about people dying. **This is the
>> first crack in the instrument and it appears before a single test is run.**

**And here is the honest thing, which is better than a payoff.**

The same objection hits a second bucket. `marks` is 44% the word `king`, and that

>> **For eight rounds nothing in this project ran the equivalent test on
>> `crisis`.** The 60%-death problem was noticed in July, written down, and never
>> touched again.

That is not a cliffhanger. It is a **gap**, and pointing at your own untested
assumption is worth more than a resolved one.

**It has since been run, and Season 4 episode 18 is the round that runs it.**
Hold two things until then, because the pair is the point.

Deleting `king` from `marks` turned out to be **inert**. Nothing the project rests

**Deleting `death` from `crisis` removes a third of the metric**, and the check
the project uses to validate that metric **reports no change at all.**

>> Same instrument, same kind of edit, opposite outcomes. **Work out now what
>> could make those two results compatible**, because the answer is a fact about
>> the metric rather than about either word, and it is the best thing in Season 4.

## What would count against this

2. **The three signs failing to co-occur.** If plague, doubling and civil strife

3. **A community in crisis that resolves it some other way** and stays resolved.

@@ EPISODE 5 — ALL AGAINST ONE

## The first thirty seconds

>% A room full of people is under pressure. One person in it gets marked,
>% and the mark stays on his face for the rest of his life. He is put out,
>% and the room goes back to working. Ask anyone who was there and they will
>% not say mob. They will say justice. And they are not lying.

## The stake

This is the mechanism the whole project tests, and you have lived it at small
scale: a group under pressure finding one person whose expulsion restores the
peace. The family, the class group, the workplace. **This episode gives you the
shape of that moment, and the reason the peace it buys is real.

## Collecting four episodes, because this one is the payoff

**Episode 3** ended on the **monstrous double**: two rivals who have become
interchangeable, each experiencing the other as a thing wearing his face, and
reaching for inhuman language to describe him.

**Episode 4** ended on a crisis with **no internal brake**. Everyone against
everyone, a feedback loop that only points one way, and no mechanism inside the
system for stopping.

**This episode does one thing: it takes the monstrous double and gives it to an
entire community, pointed at one person.**

## The character

**Zuko's banishment.**

A room full of people. One person is marked, and the mark is on his face for the
rest of the series. The room's tension resolves onto him, and the room goes back
to functioning.

And here is the part to watch. **Nobody in that room afterwards describes it as a
mob.** They describe it as **justice**, and they are not lying.

**The harder and better anchor: any school you were ever in.** The mechanism does
not require a monster. It requires a room, a tension, and one person who can be
selected without anyone retaliating on their behalf.

## The claim, which is the famous one

The crisis of episode 4 has no internal brake. What ends it, on Girard's account,
is a **switch**: all against everyone becomes **all against one.**

The community selects a victim. Not for a reason that would survive inspection.
**For availability.** The violence that was flying in every direction goes in one
direction, is discharged, and stops.

## Stop here, because this is the weakest joint in the whole theory

**Do not smooth this over on camera.**

Everything up to this point has a mechanism you can watch operating. Copied
desire produces rivalry. Rivalry produces doubles. Doubles multiplied produce a
crisis. Each step follows from the one before and you can see why.

**And then: "and then everyone turns on one person."**

*Why one?* Why does all-against-all become all-against-one rather than continuing,
or burning itself out, or fracturing into factions that stabilise?

Girard's answer is that **the mob's imitation converges the way everything else
in the system converges.** A crowd copying each other's hostility will land on
the same target for the same reason a crowd copying each other's desire lands on
the same object. **The hostility is mimetic too.**

That is coherent. It uses no new machinery. **It is also the one step in the
sequence with no independent evidence behind it.**

>> **A critic who wants to break Girard should attack here, not at the
>> stereotypes.** The stereotypes are testable and this project spends eight
>> rounds testing them. **The switch is not testable by anything in this series**,
>> and it is the load-bearing joint between the psychology and the anthropology.

Say that out loud. It costs nothing, and it is what an honest teacher does.

## And then the part that makes this a theory about texts at all

This is the step people miss, and without it none of the rest of the series makes
sense.

**The peace afterwards is real.** The community genuinely feels better. The
violence has stopped. Whatever was tearing the place apart is no longer tearing
it apart.

So now run the community's own reasoning, from inside, with no bad faith
anywhere:

- before: chaos
- we removed him
- after: peace

>> **He must have been the cause.** Look what happened when he was taken away.

**The mechanism produces its own justification, and it produces it after the
fact.** That is the sentence carrying this episode.

Nobody lied. Nobody conspired. The community observed a real correlation and drew
the only inference available to people standing where they were standing.

## Why the victim ends up sacred, which sounds strange and follows exactly

He caused the crisis, on their reading. **And he ended it.**

So he is simultaneously the worst thing that ever happened to this community and
the source of its order. That is not a contradiction they have to resolve. **It
is what a god looks like in the oldest layer of myth**: dangerous, set apart, and
the reason things work.

**Zuko again, and it is exact.** Banished as a disgrace, and the same story makes
him the one who restores the balance. **The story does not experience that as
odd.**

## The device: the mechanism as four states

**Stay on this table.** Everything in episodes 6 to 9 is a way of
detecting one of these four rows in a text.

| | the community | the victim |
|---|---|---|
| **1 crisis** | all against all, distinctions gone | not yet chosen |
| **2 selection** | needs one, not any one | acquires the marks retrospectively |
| **3 violence** | all against one, unanimous | killed or expelled |
| **4 peace** | distinctions return | becomes sacred, both curse and cure |

>> **Row 4 is why nobody inside can see it.** The peace is real, it arrives on
>> schedule, and it is the evidence the community uses to conclude that the victim
>> was guilty. **The mechanism produces its own proof.**

## The clinical anchor

**A treatment given at the moment a self-limiting illness turns the corner.**

The patient recovers. The recovery is real. The timing is real. And the causal
story writes itself, and it will be repeated confidently for years, and there is
nothing dishonest anywhere in the chain.

>> **That is what makes the mechanism hard to see from inside: the evidence for
>> the wrong explanation is genuine.**

## Where this becomes countable

The four stereotypes in episodes 6 to 9 are Girard's list of **the traces this
mechanism leaves in a text**, and that list is the entire basis of the instrument.

**And here is the limit, said in this episode rather than later.**

Everything in episodes 1 to 5 is a claim about what happened in **real
communities, in prehistory, before writing.** Nothing in this project tests any
of it. No instrument can reach a mechanism that operated before there were texts.

>> What the instrument tests is far narrower: whether the **residue** Girard says
>> the mechanism leaves is detectable in texts, in the pattern he says it should
>> be. **That is a smaller question, and it is the only one a corpus and a
>> computer can ask.**

## What would count against this

1. **Crises that resolve without a victim**, routinely, in the historical and
   ethnographic record. Girard needs the victim to be the normal exit.
2. **Victims selected for properties that genuinely predict the crisis**, rather
   than for availability. If the selection turns out to be rational, the theory
   loses its point.
3. **Communities that do not sacralise the victim afterwards.** The double
   attribution, dangerous and saving, is the theory's fingerprint.

**None of the three is tested here.** Say it again.

@@ EPISODE 6 — THE CRISIS, AND THE WORD THAT SWALLOWS IT

## The first thirty seconds

>% Somebody writes down fifteen words that mean a world coming apart.
>% Plague, famine, barren, twins, likeness. Then he counts them across
>% seventy-six old books, and the bucket fires two times in every thousand
>% words. Now break the number open. Sixty per cent of it is one word. The
>% word is death. Watch what that does to everything built on top of it.

## The stake

The season changes gear here, from philosophy to evidence. From this episode on,
nothing rests on Girard sounding right. **Everything becomes a list you can
check**, and the promise of the rest of the season is that you can run every
check yourself.

## Collecting episode 5

Episode 5 ended with a mechanism that **operated before writing** and that
**nobody inside it could see.** Which leaves an obvious problem: if it is
invisible from the inside and prehistoric, how would anyone ever study it?

**Girard's answer is that it leaves residue.** The people who ran the mechanism
went on to tell stories about what happened, and the stories carry marks.

>> From here the season stops doing philosophy and starts making **a list**,
>> because a list is the only thing you can turn into code.

Source: Girard, *The Scapegoat*, chapter 2.

## The frame, and one sentence makes it testable

Girard says a text written **by the persecutors**, who do not know they are
persecutors, carries four marks. He calls them the stereotypes of persecution.

**And the thing to say slowly: they are not four themes.**

A theme is something a text is about. **These are four positions in one event**,
seen from different places, by people with different amounts of information.

| # | stereotype | whose view it is | one line |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **crisis** | the community's | everything that made us different has stopped working |
| 2 | **crimes** | the accusers' | here is what he did |
| 3 | **marks** | the accusers' | here is why it was him and not someone else |
| 4 | **violence** | nobody's, it is what happens | all of us, on one of him |

**Four episodes, one each**, because each of the four has its own vocabulary, its
own dominant word, its own way of going wrong, and its own number that comes back
later to do damage. Taking them in pairs hides exactly the thing worth seeing,
which is that **they fail differently.**

## Stereotype 1, and the part episode 4 did not do

**Episode 4 gave you the crisis as a phenomenon.** Plague, famine, sterility,
twins, doubles, civil strife. A community describing a world in which
distinctions have stopped working. Do not re-teach it.

**This episode is the other half, and it is the half nobody does.** You have a
phenomenon. Now turn it into something a machine can count, which means writing
down the actual words.

Here is the list, as it was written, before any text existed:

> plague · pestilence · famine · drought · blight · barren · sterile · sickness ·
> disease · **death** · twin · twins · double · doubles · likeness

Look at how the list is built, because the construction is doing two different
jobs at once. Some of those words name **the disaster**. Some of them name
**the loss of difference**, which is what the disaster means in the theory. Twins
and doubles and likeness are not calamities at all. They are in the bucket because
Girard says undifferentiation is the crisis, and twins are undifferentiation with
a face.

>> **So the bucket already contains a theoretical commitment**, and it is a good
>> one, and nobody scoring a text will ever see it. **This is what an
>> operationalization is**: a claim about the world, compressed into a list, and
>> then invisible for the rest of the project.

## The number, and it is the worst one in the instrument

`crisis` fires at **2.07 per thousand words.** Middle of the pack.

And it is **60% the single word `death`.**

**Sixty percent.** That is the highest domination of any of the four buckets, and
it is worth sitting with before you hear the other three, because it sets the
standard against which they will look healthy.

## Why that number is a problem and not a curiosity

Ask what `death` tells you about a text.

**Almost nothing**, because it is one of the most common words in narrative
literature of any kind. Epics are full of deaths. Tragedies are full of deaths.
Genealogies are full of deaths, in the flattest possible sense, because that is
what a genealogy is. Hymns mention death. Philosophy mentions death.

So a bucket built to detect **a community losing its distinctions** is, in
practice, six-tenths a detector for **somebody having died**, which is a different
claim and a much duller one.

>> **The crisis bucket is the one place in the instrument where the theory is
>> loudest and the measurement is weakest.** Episode 4 spent ten minutes on the
>> most interesting idea in Girard, and this is what it looks like once it has been
>> made countable.

## The character anchor

**Agent Smith.**

He starts as one agent among several, a distinct thing with a role. Then he copies
himself onto everybody, and by the end the street is full of Smiths, and the point
is not that Smith is strong. **The point is that difference has stopped
existing**, which is the crisis stereotype in one image.

And now the joke the instrument played on itself. **The bucket built to detect
undifferentiation lost its own differentiation.** Fifteen words went in, and one
of them replicated across the bucket until it was 60% of everything the bucket
reports. `plague` and `sterile` and `twins` are still in there, doing almost
nothing, the way the last non-Smith is still technically on the street.

## The clinical anchor

**Fever.**

It is a real sign, not noise and not an artifact, and a patient with a fever
genuinely has something going on. **And it barely narrows anything**,
because it is present in most of what walks through the door, so the differential
you had before you took the temperature is roughly the differential you have
after.

Fever is a symptom that is common enough to be almost uninformative, and that is
exactly `death` at 60%. **A word can be a correct member of a category and still
destroy the category's usefulness by being everywhere.**

## What would count against this

1. **`death` genuinely being the crisis marker in these texts.** A serious
   objection, and the strongest one available. In a plague narrative the deaths
   **are** the crisis, so the word is not a contaminant, it is the thing itself.
   **Nobody has read a sample of `death` hits in context to find out**, which is a
   day's work and would settle it.
2. **The bucket working anyway.** Domination is not the same as failure. Delete
   `death`, re-run everything, and see whether any result moves. **Never done**,
   for any bucket, in any round.
3. **60% being normal.** If every word-list in every field is dominated by its
   commonest member, this is a fact about language rather than a defect here.
   Plausible, and it would make the number less alarming without making the bucket
   any more useful.

That is the community's view of what is happening. **Episode 7 is the accusers'
view of who did it**, and it contains the rarest bucket in the instrument and the
one that kills the project's first prediction.

3. Write the crisis word-list you would have written, before seeing the other

@@ EPISODE 7 — THE CRIMES, AND WHY THEY ARE NEVER SMALL

## The first thirty seconds

>% Nobody is ever run out of town for petty theft. Not for laziness, not for
>% being bad at the job. The charge is always enormous. He killed his
>% father. He slept with his mother. He poisoned the wells. That is not an
>% accident of taste, and it is not sadism. There is a reason the charge has
>% to be that size.

## The stake

You have heard accusations like this one. When a group needs someone guilty, the
charge is never petty, because a petty crime could be handled by ordinary means.
**This episode shows you how to read the size of an accusation as information
about the accusers**, and once you see it you will see it in every scandal that
crosses your feed.

## Collecting episode 6

Episode 6 was the community saying **what is happening to us.** This episode is
the accusers saying **what he did**, and where the crisis bucket failed by being
too common, this one fails by being too rare.

## The distinction that everything later depends on

Say this now, and it gets said again in episode 9, because a later episode is
about the day it nearly got broken.

>> **Stereotype 2 is what the victim is ACCUSED of. Stereotype 4 is what the crowd
>> DOES.** Different buckets. **Confusing them wrecks the instrument.**

Hold it. **Season 2 episode 9** is the episode where somebody, reasonably, with
entirely good intentions, almost merges them to fix a failing prediction, and the
reason it would have been cheating is not statistical.

## The pattern inside the accusations

Here is the observation that makes this more than a list of bad things.

**The accusation is never a small crime.** Nobody is scapegoated for petty theft,
or laziness, or being bad at their job. It is always a crime that **erases a
distinction the community lives by**, and Girard sorts them into four families.

| family | examples | the difference it erases |
|---|---|---|
| violence against the most protected | killing a father, a king, a child | kin against stranger, ruler against ruled |
| sexual transgression | incest, bestiality | family against outsider, human against animal |
| religious crime | sacrilege, blasphemy, profanation | sacred against common |
| contagion crimes | poisoning, sorcery, treason | spreads invisibly, erasing the line between one person and everyone |

## And now the argument, which is the whole episode

Look at what those four have in common. Not "they are serious." **Look at the
shape.**

Every one of them describes **a person who collapsed a distinction.**

And episode 6 told you what the crisis is: **distinctions collapsing.**

>> **So the community is accusing one person of being the crisis.**
>>
>> The victim is charged with **being the thing that is happening to everybody.**

That is the logic in a sentence, and it is why the crime families are not an
arbitrary list. They are the crisis, attributed to a person.

**And it explains something otherwise strange**: why the accusations in these
stories are so lurid and so specific. A community that says "he made us all
alike" has said nothing actionable. A community that says "he slept with his
mother" has said the same thing in a form you can execute someone for.

That is the step worth slowing down on, because it is where the theory earns its
keep. **The crisis is real and it is abstract**, and nobody can be put to death
for an abstraction. The accusation is the crisis translated into a charge, and a
charge has a defendant, and a defendant can be dealt with in an afternoon.

>> **The lurid detail is not decoration and it is not sadism. It is the conversion
>> step**, and a community that could not perform it would be stuck with a
>> catastrophe it had no way to end.

## The clinical anchor

**A diagnosis of exclusion, arrived at from the wrong end.**

A patient is unwell and nothing fits. The picture is diffuse, the tests are
equivocal, and there is real pressure in the room to name something, because a
name is what lets the next step happen. **A named condition can be treated. An
unexplained deterioration cannot be**, and everyone in the corridor knows which of
those two the family will accept.

The label that gets attached is often specific out of all proportion to the
evidence for it, and the specificity is the point. **It converts a situation into
a problem**, and the conversion is what the room actually needed.

## The character anchors

**A word about the play that is going to come up in every season**, because it is

@@ EPISODE 8 — THE MARKS, AND THE VICTIM WHO IS TOO HIGH

## The first thirty seconds

>% Who gets picked. Everybody expects the list to run one way: the stranger,
>% the beggar, the lame, the orphan. It does. But the same list has the king
>% on it, and the queen, and the tyrant. The most powerful man in the room
>% is one of the safest people there to destroy. Once you see why, you
>% cannot unsee it.

## The stake

Why him and not someone else? This episode answers a question nobody asks out
loud, and it explains a pattern you have already met: the popular one, the
talented one, the outsider who was somehow always going to be the one blamed.
**After this episode the choice of victim stops looking random to you, in texts
and in group chats alike.

## Collecting episode 7

Episode 7 ended with the accusers saying **what he did.** This episode is the
accusers saying **why it was him**, and it answers the obvious question nobody
asks.

## Why him

If the mechanism really is a community discharging its own tension, and the victim
is not actually the cause, the choice looks arbitrary. **But it is not random
either**, and Girard's answer is precise.

>> Victims are chosen for **a sign that puts them slightly outside the group,
>> without giving them the power to retaliate.**

Both halves do work. **Outside enough** that turning on them does not feel like
turning on ourselves. **Weak enough** that nobody comes for revenge afterwards,
because revenge would restart the crisis and ending the crisis was the whole
point.

**Notice that this is a two-condition test**, which is unusual in Girard and worth
flagging as a gift. Most of the theory is stated in a form that accommodates
whatever you find. **This one can be run on a candidate and fail**, and the next
section runs it.

## Two directions, and the second is the one people miss

**Too low.** The stranger, the foreigner, the cripple, the lame, the blind, the
deformed, the beggar, the orphan. Everyone expects this list.

**Too high.** The king. The queen. The tyrant.

## Why "too high" works, because the episode has to earn this

Just asserting that the king is a victim mark is not enough. **Run the two
criteria on him.**

**Is he outside the group?** Completely. A king has no peers by definition. He is
the one person in the society who does not have an equal, and every ritual around
him exists to say so.

**Can he retaliate?** This is the counter-intuitive half. He looks like the most
powerful person there. But **he has no faction of his own kind**, because there is
no other king. When a community turns on him, **nobody is obliged to avenge him**,
because he stands in no relation of equals to anybody.

>> A powerful man with peers is dangerous to attack. **A powerful man with no
>> peers is the safest target in the room**, and this is precisely inverted from
>> how it looks.

**And there is a further argument Girard makes**, which is worth having because it
explains why kingship keeps showing up in these stories at all: in many traditions
the king is a **victim whose execution has been deferred.** The rituals that set
him apart, the taboos, the special food, the special clothing, the periodic
humiliations, look like the treatment of a sacred victim postponed.

**Mark that as tier K.** The comparative material on sacred kingship is real and
this series has not gone and checked the sources. **Say so rather than implying
you have.**

## The device: the two criteria, run as a grid

**Put the two questions on one axis and the candidates on the other**, and the
"too high" category stops being a paradox.

| candidate | outside the group? | able to retaliate? | a victim mark? |
|---|---|---|---|
| an ordinary member | no | yes | **no** |
| the stranger, the cripple, the beggar | **yes** | no | **yes** |
| a powerful man **with peers** | no | **yes** | **no** |
| **the king** | **yes**, he has no equals | **no**, no faction of his own kind | **yes** |

**Read the third and fourth rows together.** They are both powerful. Only one of
them is a victim mark, and the difference is not power. **It is whether anybody
is obliged to avenge him.**

## The character anchors

**Too low: Zuko's scar.** Visible, permanent, on the face, and given to him by his
own father inside his own family.

**Too high: Macbeth.** A king who becomes the thing the kingdom must expel to be
well again, and the play describes the country as **sick** and the cure as
removing him.

**And the modern one, which lands hardest: BoJack.** Marked by being famous, which
is the too-high position exactly. The story is about a public turning on him with
grievances that are **completely real** and a process that is **completely
mechanical**, at the same time, and it refuses to let you pick one reading.

>> That is the most useful thing about BoJack here. **The mechanism does not
>> require the victim to be innocent.** It only requires that the turning is doing
>> something for the people turning.

## The number, and it is the one that comes back hardest

`marks` fires at **2.90 per thousand words**, and it is **44% the single word
`king`.**

Put it in the sequence you now have. Crisis 60% `death`. Crimes 12% `murder`.
Marks 44% `king`. **The bucket carrying Girard's most distinctive claim is the
second most dominated**, and dominated by a word that is not a description of a
person at all. It is a job title.

So an instrument built to detect victim-marking will in practice very largely
detect **whether a text is about royalty.** And the highest `marks` score in the
whole corpus belongs to Sophocles' *Oedipus the King*, in which the title
character is addressed as king on nearly every page.

## Do not resolve this on camera

**Two readings, both live, both coming out of the same number.**

**The objection.** The bucket is broken. It picks up a genre signal, not a victim
mark. Any text set in a palace scores high and it means nothing.

**Girard's reading.** The king **is** a marked victim, for the reasons three
paragraphs up, and a text full of kings really is full of potential victims.

>> Say that this episode **cannot settle it.** What settles it is an experiment in
>> **Season 4** where the word is simply deleted and everything re-run, and **the
>> answer is not the one this episode makes you expect.**

## The clinical anchor

**The patient nobody is calling about.**

No family in the corridor, no relative who works in health, no one who knows which
words to use. **Nothing has been decided against them.** No individual decision on
that ward is unfair.

And they are, quietly, the one whose discharge gets pushed, whose complaint gets
recorded as agitation, whose plan changes without a conversation. **Slightly
outside, and nobody to answer to afterwards**, which is Girard's two conditions in
a corridor rather than a myth.

## What would count against this

1. **Victims in these stories who are ordinary, unmarked members of the
   community**, chosen with no distinguishing feature at all. The marks stereotype
   needs marks.
2. **The "too high" category being an artifact.** If deleting `king` destroys the
   instrument's results, the category was carrying the bucket rather than
   describing the world. **Season 4 episode 17 runs exactly this test.**
3. **The two-condition test never actually being run on anything else.** It was
   run on the king, in this episode, by Girard. **Nobody has taken a list of
   attested victims and checked what proportion satisfies both conditions**, which
   is the version of this that would be evidence rather than illustration.

@@ EPISODE 9 — THE VIOLENCE, AND THE UNANIMITY HIDDEN INSIDE IT

## The first thirty seconds

>% Four phrases go into a word list. With one voice. One accord. As one man.

>% under violence. That one filing decision is made in an afternoon by
>% somebody being reasonable, and it decides the result of a study that will
>% not run for another year.

## The stake

The last stereotype is the crowd itself, and it carries the tell you can test
anywhere: nobody dissents. This episode shows you what unanimity looks like from
the inside, which is warmth. **You have been inside that warmth. The episode
gives you the tool for noticing it next time.

## Collecting episode 8

Three stereotypes down, and all three have been about **one person**: what is
happening to the community, what he is accused of, why he was chosen.

**This one is about everybody else.**

And collect the distinction from episode 7, because this is the bucket it protects
against: **stereotype 2 is what the victim is accused of. Stereotype 4 is what the
crowd does.** Season 2 episode 9 is the day that nearly got broken.

## Three components, and the third is the series

**The crowd itself.** Crowd, mob, throng, multitude, rabble.

**What it does.** Stones, expels, banishes, drives out, hunts, tears apart.

**Its unanimity.** With one voice. One accord. As one man. The whole city.

## And the third component is the rest of the series

**The unanimity is not a detail. It is the load-bearing part**, and here is why
that is a design decision worth explaining on camera.

When you look at the word-list in Season 2, you will find phrases in the
**violence** bucket that are not violence words at all: `with one voice`,
`one accord`, `as one man`, `the whole city`.

>> **Those are unanimity words, and they are in the violence bucket because for
>> Girard the unanimity is what makes the violence work.** Violence without
>> unanimity is just a fight. Violence with unanimity is a mechanism.

And Girard's entire distinction between myth and Gospel, the claim that Seasons 3
and 4 spend eleven episodes failing to measure, is **only about whether that
unanimity holds.** Not whether there is violence. Not whether the victim is
innocent. **Whether anybody breaks ranks.**

Which means this bucket is doing two jobs under one name, and **it will be split
apart in Season 3** into a fifth bucket built entirely out of the crowd coming
apart. Everything the project eventually finds, and it is not much, lives in that
split.

And be clear about what kind of mistake the merger is, because it is not a
mislabelling. **The two things have completely different frequencies.** Violence
words are everywhere in Greek literature; unanimity phrases are rare and turn up
only when a text is describing a group acting as one. Put a rare, decisive signal
into the same bucket as a common, uninformative one and **the total is the common
one**, to within a rounding error, forever.

>> Nobody chose that. It follows from addition, and it was locked in the day
>> somebody decided which bucket `with one voice` belonged to.

## The character anchor

**"I am Spartacus."**

Everybody knows the scene and almost nobody notices what it is. A crowd is ordered
to identify one man. **The mechanism requires exactly one thing from them, which
is that they point**, and instead every man claims to be him.

Nothing about that scene is a rescue. It does not save Spartacus and the men know
it will not. **What it destroys is the unanimity**, and the reason it is one of
the most famous moments in cinema is that an audience feels, without being told,
that something enormous has been broken.

>> **Hold that scene for four seasons.** It is the negative image of everything
>> this instrument is built to find, and the whole of Season 3's dissent lexicon is
>> an attempt to detect it in Greek.

## The clinical anchor

**Quorum sensing.**

Bacteria that individually do nothing at all. Each one releases a signal molecule,
each one measures how much of that signal is around it, and **below a threshold
every single cell stays inert.** Cross the threshold and they all switch on
together, and the population starts behaving as one organism.

The individual bacterium has not changed. **What changed is that it detected
agreement**, and agreement is the trigger rather than the accompaniment.

That is Girard's claim about crowds, in a system with no minds in it: the
destructive behaviour is not the sum of individual intentions, **it is switched on
by unanimity itself.**

## The four numbers to have cold

Four episodes, four buckets. **Here is the whole instrument on one slide**, and
you have already met three of the rows.

| bucket | rate per 1000 | dominated by | that word's share |
|---|---|---|---|
| violence | 4.76 | `blood` | 21% |
| **marks** | 2.90 | **`king`** | **44%** |
| crisis | 2.07 | `death` | 60% |
| crimes | 0.95 | `murder` | 12% |

**Violence is the commonest bucket and the second least dominated.** Which makes
it, on the two crude measures available, the healthiest of the four: plenty of
hits, no single word running away with it.

>> And that is the trap. **The healthiest bucket measures the thing Girard cares
>> least about.** He is not claiming that persecution texts are violent. Everything
>> in Greek literature is violent. **He is claiming something about the unanimity**,
>> and the unanimity is a handful of phrases hiding inside the one bucket nobody
>> would think to look in.

## The translation problem, planted here because it lands in Season 3

**The unanimity signal is phrases, and phrases are the first thing a translator
changes.**

`With one voice`, `one accord`, `as one man`, `the whole city`. Every one of those
is an idiom, and a translator rendering the same Greek as "everyone shouted" has
preserved the meaning perfectly and **destroyed the measurement completely.**

Compare that with the rest of the instrument. `Blood`, `king`, `death`, `murder`
are single common nouns, and any competent translator will use them because there
is nothing else to use. **The buckets built out of ordinary words are robust to
translation. The one carrying Girard's actual claim is not.**

>> So the most theoretically important signal in the instrument is also the most
>> fragile one, and **it is fragile in a way no amount of statistics can repair**,
>> because the information was lost before the corpus existed.

**Season 3 episode 5 measures this**, once, on one paired play, and finds two
translators differing by 17.2% on total rate. Nobody has ever measured it on the
unanimity phrases specifically, which is where it would bite hardest.

## What would count against this

1. **Unanimity language turning out to be equally common in texts with no
   collective violence in them.** Then it is a rhetorical register, not a
   fingerprint. **Season 3 tests exactly this and the answer is not clean.**
2. **The unanimity phrases being too rare to measure.** They are phrases rather
   than words, in a corpus of translations, and a translator who writes "everyone
   shouted" instead of "with one voice" has erased the signal without changing the
   meaning. **This is the objection the project takes least seriously and it may
   be the strongest.**
3. **Putting unanimity in the violence bucket being simply wrong.** It merges a
   frequent, uninformative thing with a rare, decisive one, and the rare one
   disappears into the total. **Season 3 splits them out and everything the project
   finds comes from the split**, which is an argument that they should never have
   been together.

@@ EPISODE 10 — WHY ALL FOUR TOGETHER IS THE WHOLE ARGUMENT

## The first thirty seconds

>% Violent stories contain violence. Stories about kings contain the word
>% king. Tragedies contain death. Four word lists, and every one of them is
>% a tautology on its own. Run all four across seventy-two Greek and Roman
>% books and all four fire in nine out of ten. So what is left. One word is
>% left, and the word is together.

## The stake

No new content in this episode, only a word: *together*. But that word is the
difference between a theory and a vocabulary, and it tells you exactly where the

Episode 9 finished the list. **This episode is about one word in it: *together*.**

## Collecting episodes 6 to 9

Four episodes, four stereotypes, four numbers. Crisis at 2.07 per thousand and 60%
one word. Crimes at 0.95, the rarest and the best built. Marks at 2.90 and 44%

**Say what kind of average those four are, because a viewer who goes and checks

figures are**, and the pooled version weights the long texts more heavily, which

**Violent stories contain violence.** Not a finding. A tautology.

**Stories about kings contain the word king.** Also not a finding.

**Tragedies contain death.** Tragedies are about people dying.

**Myths contain accusations.** Myths are about people being accused.

>> Any one of the four, found alone, in a text of the relevant genre, proves
>> **nothing at all.** You could build a word-list for almost any theory and find
>> it in Greek literature, because Greek literature is enormous and about extreme
>> things.

## The device: the ladder, and only one rung holds weight

**Three rungs, and only the top one holds weight.**

| level | the claim | what the corpus says | worth testing? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | each vocabulary appears in these texts | trivially true | no |
| **2** | all four appear in the same text | **true in 90% of 72 texts** | **almost worthless** |
| **3** | all four converge on **one passage** | this is the whole project | **yes** |

## Three levels of claim, and only one of them is worth testing

This is the spine of the episode. **Climb it one level at a time and do not skip a rung.**

**Level 1. Each vocabulary appears in these texts.**
Trivially true. Nobody needs an instrument.

**Level 2. All four appear in the same text.**
This sounds much stronger. **It is almost as trivial, and here is the number
that proves it.**

When the instrument is finally built and run on 72 Greek and Roman texts, the
score for the weakest of the four buckets is **above zero in 90% of them.**

>> **All four stereotypes are present in nine out of ten texts in the corpus.**
>> Including the ones that are not about persecution at all. **Level 2 is nearly
>> worthless as evidence**, and knowing that now is what stops you being impressed
>> by it later.

**Level 3. All four converge on the same passage, more than chance would put
them there.**

**That is the claim**, and it is what "four faces of one event" means if it means
anything. One process, one place, one moment: the crisis, the accusation, the
marks, the killing.

>> **The difference between level 2 and level 3 is the difference between a word
>> list and a theory.** A word list says these terms show up in these books. A
>> theory says they show up **in the same place, because one thing caused all
>> four.**

## And here is what the season is really telling you

The project measured level 3 **four separate times, with four different
instruments, and failed every time.**

Not because the Gospels came out wrong. Not because the corpus was bad. **Because
the convergence was not there to find, or the instruments could not see it, and
after four attempts nobody can say which.**

Say that here. **It is not a spoiler, it is the reason to keep watching**, and it
is more honest than pretending the series has a triumphant ending.

## The clinical anchor, and it is not a metaphor

A raised white cell count means nothing on its own. Fifty causes.

A fever means nothing on its own. A hundred causes.

A raised lactate means nothing on its own.

**Sepsis is the claim that they arrive together, because one process produces all
of them.** That is what a syndrome is. It is a stronger claim than a symptom and
correspondingly harder to demonstrate.

**And you already know the objections to a syndrome**, which is why this anchor
is worth the time:

- *your three findings are common in sick patients generally*
- *you have defined a cluster that would appear by chance in any intensive care

- *your criteria were built by looking at patients who already had the diagnosis*

>> **Every one of those has an exact counterpart in this series.** The first is

>> existed, which is Season 2 episode 2.

**A relay team.**

Nobody thinks the fastest individual leg tells you about the team. The claim

go all the way round.

>>

>> theory needs. **And** it means they do not co-occur strongly, which is what the

the books and checked the page numbers. **What came back is a lesson in how much
of what everyone believes about a thinker was never checked against the page**,
and one of the corrections changes the argument itself.

**This episode exists because somebody finally opened the books.** Every page
number quoted in this series came out of the project's own files and had never
been checked against a copy. When they were checked, three of four needed a
correction, and one of them turned out to be about the argument rather than the
page.

## Collecting episode 10

Episode 10 built a ladder. Level 1, each vocabulary appears. Level 2, all four
appear in the same text, which is nearly worthless because **all four fire in 90%
of 72 Greek and Roman texts.** Level 3, all four converge on one passage, more
than chance would put them there.

**And it said level 3 is the claim.** This episode asks whether Girard says that,
and the answer is on a page nobody in eight rounds had read.

## And now the thing that was found by opening the book

**This is tier V. Go and check it, the passage is four pages into chapter 3 of
*The Scapegoat*.**

Girard states his own decision rule for identifying a persecution text, and it is
worth reading in full because nobody in this project had read it until the
citations were verified:

>> "Each time an oral or written testament mentions an act of violence that is
>> directly or indirectly collective we question whether it includes the
>> description of a social and cultural crisis... The juxtaposition of more than
>> one stereotype within a single document indicates persecution. **Not all the
>> stereotypes must be present: three are enough and often even two.**"

**Read the last sentence again and put it on the ladder.**

That is a **level 2** rule. Presence in a document. And it is not even the strict
version of level 2, because it does not ask for four. **It asks for two.**

>> **Girard's own procedure sits on the rung this episode has just shown is nearly
>> worthless**, and it sits one rung lower than the version that was measured.

Now apply the project's own number to it. All four buckets fire in **90% of 72
Greek and Roman texts**, including texts with no persecution in them anywhere. If
all four are present in nine texts out of ten, then **"three are enough and often
even two" classifies almost the whole of Greek literature as a persecution
document.**

**That is not an objection somebody invented. It is Girard's stated rule meeting
this project's measurement, and the measurement was made without anybody noticing
it bore on the rule.**

## The device: put the ladder next to the source

**Same three rungs, one extra column**, and the column is what the citation check
bought.

| level | what it asks | who requires it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | the vocabulary appears | nobody |
| **2** | **two or three of four, anywhere in the document** | **Girard, *Scapegoat* ch. 3** |
| 2 strict | all four, anywhere in the document | true in 90% of texts |
| **3** | all four **on one passage** | **this project, and nobody else** |

>> **Read the second and fourth rows.** They are two rungs apart, and the project
>> spent eight rounds on the top one believing it was on Girard's.

## What follows, and be careful, because two things do not follow

**It does not follow that Girard is wrong**, because a rule of thumb for reading a
medieval chronicle is not the same as a claim about what the mechanism produces.
He may well hold that the event generates all four and that a surviving text
preserves only some. **Say that out loud, because it is the strongest defence and
it is probably correct.**

**And it does not follow that this project was wasting its time.** The opposite.

>> **Level 3 is this project's invention.** Girard did not ask for co-location on a
>> passage. **The instrument has been testing something harder than its source
>> requires**, for eight rounds, and failing at it.

Which reframes every failure ahead of you.

At the end of Season 3 the tempting sentence is "Girard's claim did not show up."
**The honest one is longer and says two things.** The strengthened version of his
claim did not show up. **And the version he actually stated is refuted by this
project's own 90% figure, in the other direction.**

Hold both. **Neither is comfortable and both are checkable.**

## The move that settles it, and it takes one sentence

Here is the question that makes the whole thing tractable, and it is the question
to ask of any detection rule anybody ever hands you.

>> **Run it against the case it is supposed to distinguish.**

Girard's rule says two or three stereotypes in a document indicate persecution. So
run it on **the Passion**, which is the standard name for the last part of the
gospel story: the arrest of Jesus, his trial before the Jewish authorities and
then Pilate, the crowd calling for his execution, and the crucifixion. From the
Latin for suffering, and nothing to do with the modern sense of the word.

**That episode is the one text Girard's whole theory has to treat differently**,
so it is the right thing to point a persecution detector at.

**Crisis**: a city under occupation, a festival crowd, an authority in conflict
with a popular teacher. **Crimes**: blasphemy, which is Girard's third crime family
exactly. **Marks**: a provincial and a carpenter's son who is simultaneously a
claimed king, both directions at once. **Violence**: a crowd, calling out together,
and a public execution.

**Four of four. Emphatically.** Episode 12 works through it properly, and it is not
a grudging pass.

>> So the rule fires on the Gospels **as hard as it fires on anything**, and by its
>> own terms it must, because Girard says the Gospels describe the same event. **A
>> rule that classifies both sides of your distinction as positive is not a
>> distinction.**

**And Girard knows this.** That is the part that turns a gotcha into a finding. He
never claims the four stereotypes separate myth from Gospel. He puts the whole
difference somewhere else, in one sentence about a dissenting voice, and this
series reads that sentence in episode 12.

>> **The four stereotypes were never the discriminating instrument. They are the
>> detector.** They tell you a persecution is being described. They cannot tell you
>> who is describing it or whether the describer knows what he is doing.

So the finding is not that Girard contradicts himself. **It is that the first three
rounds built an ever better detector and tested it as though it were a
discriminator.**

The theory says it cannot be one. **And the sentence saying so was on the shelf
the whole time.**

**Round 4 is the round that finally builds the discriminator.** Everything before
it was sharpening the wrong tool, and the person sharpening it could have known
that from chapter 3 of *The Scapegoat* and page 3 of *I See Satan*.

## The character anchor

**A recipe you have cooked from memory for years.**

You know it. You have made it forty times, you have taught it to somebody else,
and you could write it out on request. **Then you find the card**, and it says
180 degrees where you have always used 200, and it does not mention the step you
have always thought was the whole trick.

The dish you have been making is not bad. It may genuinely be better. **But it is
yours**, and you have spent years attributing it to somebody else, sincerely,
without ever once putting the card next to the pan.

**That is what happened here**, and notice the specific thing that made it
possible: **the recipe was quoted from memory of a summary**, and everybody in the
chain was quoting the same summary.

## The clinical anchor

**Citing a guideline you have never opened.**

Everybody has done it. You know what the guideline says because you learned it
from a senior, who learned it from a talk, which was based on the guideline. **The
chain is usually accurate and it is never checkable at any single link**, and the
gap between "what the guideline says" and "what everybody knows it says" widens by
one small step per retelling.

The uncomfortable part is that this is normally harmless. **It stops being
harmless the moment you build an instrument out of it**, because an instrument
does not know it is repeating a summary and will happily produce four significant
figures on top of one.

## What would count against this

1. **The chapter 3 sentence being a rule of thumb rather than a claim.** Argued

   precisely the reader's task in that sentence**, which is deciding from a

2. **Girard saying the opposite somewhere else.** Very likely, across a fifty-year
   body of work, and **nobody has looked.** One verified sentence is one verified

3. **The 90% figure being an artifact.** It comes from this project's lexicon, and

>> **Objection 2 is the honest ceiling on this episode.** It found one sentence.
>> Finding one sentence is not scholarship, it is the first ten minutes of
>> scholarship, and the series should say so while reporting it.

@@ EPISODE 12 — MYTH IS THE STORY THE CROWD TELLS ITSELF

## The first thirty seconds

>% You watch a story for two hours. It is detailed, it holds together, every
>% part of it fits. In the last ninety seconds you find out it was assembled
>% by the one person who gained from you believing it. Now the problem. From
>% inside the telling, a made-up account and a true one look exactly the
>% same. So how would you ever check.

## The stake

You have read myths your whole life as stories about gods and monsters. This
episode reads one as a press release written by the people who won. **Once the
reversal is shown to you, you will not be able to unsee it, and it works on
modern stories too**, which is what the incident report in this episode is for.

## Collecting episode 5, and the whole episode falls out of it

Episode 5 established two things that sit uncomfortably together.

**One:** the mechanism is invisible from inside it. It only works because the
community cannot see what it is doing.

**Two:** the community afterwards tells stories about what happened.

>> **So a myth is what that invisibility looks like written down.**

That is the organizing sentence of this episode. Not "myths are lies." Not "myths
are primitive." **Myth is an accurate report by people who could not see the one
thing that mattered.**

## The character

**The Usual Suspects.**

The story you are told for two hours is coherent, detailed, internally consistent,
and **assembled by the person with the most to gain from you believing it.** It
is only visible as a construction in the last ninety seconds, when you see where
the details came from.

That is the shape of the claim, and the reason the film works is the reason the
claim is hard to test: **from inside the telling, a constructed account and a
true one look identical.**

**Second anchor: the Death Note ending, from the point of view of ordinary
people.** Somebody dangerous is gone. Order returns. The account that circulates
afterwards is not a lie anyone told. **It is the only account available to people
who were not in the room.**

## The teaching device: run the causation both ways

**Walk both arrows**, because they convert the claim from a mood into something
you can apply to any story in ten seconds.

**What the myth says happened:**

```
he committed the crime  ->  so we expelled him  ->  so the plague lifted
```

**What Girard says happened:**

```
the crisis  ->  we needed somebody  ->  we expelled him  ->  the crisis ended
                                                                    |
                                     so he must have caused it  <----+
```

**Same events. Same order in time. Opposite direction of causation.**

And notice the crucial detail: **the accusation arrives last** in Girard's
version, even though it appears first in the telling. The community is not lying
about the sequence. It is **explaining a real correlation with the only
explanation available to it.**

>> That is the whole of Girard's reading of myth in one diagram, and you can hand
>> the audience the diagram and let them run it on any story they like.

## What that predicts about the shape of myths

A claim about what a text **omits** is a claim you can go and check.

| what the myth contains | what it leaves out |
|---|---|
| the crisis, in vivid detail | that the crisis was ordinary rivalry, spreading |
| the crime, as established fact | any doubt at all about whether he did it |
| the marks, as the explanation | that the marks were selected afterwards |
| the violence, as necessary | that the violence was arbitrary |
| the victim becoming a god | that the community killed him |

**And the omission Girard cares most about: nobody in a myth ever suggests the
victim might be innocent.**

## The universal negative, which is the sharpest thing he ever wrote

*I See Satan Fall Like Lightning*, page 3. **Read it aloud twice.**

>> "not merely the innocence of the victims versus their guilt, but the fact
>> that, in mythology, **no one ever questions this guilt**. In the Gospels, the
>> revealing account of scapegoating emanates not from the unanimous crowd but
>> from a dissenting few. Initially, Jesus' disciples almost surrender to the
>> mimetic power of the many, but on the third day, thanks to the Resurrection,
>> they secede from the deluded mob and proclaim the innocence of their Lord.
>> **In mythology no dissenting voice is ever heard.**"

**Now point at the load-bearing half**, because it is routinely misread and the
misreading wasted four rounds of this project.

**The differentia is NOT "the victims are innocent."** Girard rules that out
himself, **in the same sentence**: *not merely the innocence of the victims versus
their guilt.*

**The differentia is "no one ever questions."**

>> It is a claim about **the crowd's verdict**, not about **the victim's
>> character.** Those are two different things and everything in Seasons 3 and 4
>> turns on the difference.

## Why this shape is a gift to anyone trying to test it

>> A **universal negative** is the most falsifiable shape a claim can take. *This
>> never happens, anywhere, in any of them.* **One genuine counterexample kills
>> it.**

Compare that with almost everything else in Girard, which is a claim about
tendencies and readings and cannot be cornered. **This one has an edge you can
walk up to.**

That is why Season 3 builds an entire instrument aimed at this single sentence,
and why the failure of that instrument is worth four episodes.

## The number to plant, and it is the best self-catch in the project

When the project finally built a word-list for that sentence, the list split into
two halves.

**One half about the victim being innocent:** innocent, guiltless, no fault,
falsely accused, done nothing.

**One half about the crowd being divided:** division, some said, others said, not
all, spare him.

**Across four rounds of testing, the innocence half carried nothing at all and
the division half carried everything.**

>> **And Girard's own sentence, quoted above, says innocence is not the
>> differentia.** The instrument was built mostly out of innocence vocabulary
>> anyway.

**That detonates in Season 7 episode 4**, and it is the strongest self-catch in
the whole project: **the word-list contradicted the sentence it was built from,
and nobody noticed for four rounds.**

## The clinical anchor

**The incident report written by the team involved.**

It is coherent. It is complete. Every fact in it is true, the timeline is right,
and it explains how the outcome followed from the circumstances. **It is also
written by the people whose account it exonerates**, and it is the document that
enters the record.

Nobody lied. **The account is simply told from the only position available to the
people telling it**, which is the position that cannot see itself acting.

## What would count against this

1. **A myth containing a sustained dissenting voice.** One is enough. The claim is
   universal.
2. **Myths that describe the violence as arbitrary**, in their own voice, without
   a later author's irony. Girard needs the account to be from inside.
3. **The causal reversal failing to fit.** If myths routinely establish the crime
   independently of the crisis, with evidence, the diagram is wrong.

>> Number 1 is the one Season 3 goes after, and **what happens is stranger than
>> either passing or failing.**

## Handing off

**Episode 13 is the other half of the claim**, and it is the one that made Girard
famous and got him disliked in equal measure: **what the Gospels do differently.**

@@ EPISODE 13 — WHAT GIRARD SAYS THE GOSPELS DO

## The first thirty seconds

>% Take the story the crowd tells and do not change a single event. Same
>% mechanism, same crowd, same picked-out man, same manufactured guilt. Move
>% one thing only. Move where the camera stands, to the people who did not
>% join in. That is the whole claim, and the strange part is that it is
>% checkable. Somebody either breaks ranks or nobody does.

## The stake

The strangest claim in the season, and the one Girard staked everything on: that
the Gospels are not myth but the same events told from the victim's side. You do
not have to believe it. **You do have to notice that it is checkable**, because
the difference between the two story versions is concrete enough to test, and the
project tests it later.

## Collecting episode 12, and reusing its diagram

Episode 12 gave you two versions of the same events.

**What the myth says:**

```
he committed the crime  ->  so we expelled him  ->  so the plague lifted
```

**What Girard reconstructs underneath it:**

```
the crisis  ->  we needed somebody  ->  we expelled him  ->  the crisis ended
                                                                    |
                                     so he must have caused it  <----+
```

The second one is a **reconstruction.** Girard has to argue for it, because no
myth says it.

>> **Girard's claim about the Gospels is that they narrate the second diagram
>> directly.** Not as a reconstruction. As the story.

That is the entire episode, and everything else is consequence.

## The claim, and it is smaller and stranger than people expect

Girard is **not** saying the Gospels describe a different kind of killing.

He is saying they describe **the same kind of killing**. Same mechanism, same
crowd, same arbitrary selection, same manufactured guilt.

What differs is where the camera stands. They narrate it from
**the position the myth has to suppress**: the victim's, and the position of the
few who do not join in.

*I See Satan Fall Like Lightning*, page 122:

>> "it is a rebellious minority, a small group of dissidents that separates from
>> the collective violence of the crowd and **destroys its unanimity**. This
>> minority group is the community of the first witnesses to the Resurrection,
>> that is, the apostles and those who gather around them. **This dissident
>> minority has no equivalent in the myths.**"

## The character anchor, and it is the same one

**The Usual Suspects again, deliberately, from episode 12, with the last ninety
seconds moved to the front.**

Same events. Same participants. Same crime. But the telling begins from the
knowledge that the crowd's account is a construction, and **once you have that,
the entire story reads differently without a single fact changing.**

That is the structural claim, and it is worth making the audience feel it before
any theology arrives:

>> **The difference between myth and Gospel, on this account, is not what
>> happened. It is who is holding the camera.**

## The structural consequence, and it is the thing most people get backwards

**If Girard is right, the Gospels should score HIGH on all four stereotypes. Not
low.**

Run the list from episodes 6 to 9 against the Passion and check:

| stereotype | present in the Passion? |
|---|---|
| **crisis** | a city under occupation, a festival crowd, a religious authority in conflict with a popular teacher |
| **crimes** | **blasphemy**, which is Girard's third crime family exactly |
| **marks** | a provincial, a carpenter's son, **and simultaneously a claimed king**. Both directions from episode 8 |
| **violence** | a crowd, calling out together, and a public execution |

**All four. Emphatically.**

>> **So the four-bucket instrument built in Season 2 can never tell a Gospel from
>> a myth, and it was never going to.** That is **the design, not a defect**, and
>> the project wrote it down in July before it became inconvenient.

Say that on camera, because it is the kind of limitation people usually discover
late and then present as a nuance.

## So where is the difference

Girard says exactly where, and it is the one place: **the unanimity breaks.**

Somebody says he has done nothing. Somebody washes his hands. Somebody follows at
a distance. The crowd is reported as **divided** rather than as one voice.

And in myth, on Girard's account, **that never happens anywhere.**

>> Which is why the discriminating instrument, when it is finally built in Season
>> 3, is not about violence at all. It is a word-list for **crowds coming apart.**

## The ordinal series, and it is the most tested claim in the project

Girard treats tragedy as **partway** toward revelation. Tragedy knows something is
wrong. It does not know what.

So the prediction is not a binary. **It is an ordering:**

```
myth   <   tragedy   <   Gospel        in dissent, in broken unanimity
```

**Plant this and do not explain it further. It detonates in Season 3 episode 18.**

>> This series is the **single most tested claim in the entire project.** It was
>> attacked **four times with four different instruments** and survived **none** of
>> them.

Notice why an ordering is a better thing to predict than a difference. **A binary
claim has one way to be right and one way to be wrong.** An ordering of three tiers
has six possible arrangements and Girard is committed to exactly one of them, so
five of the six outcomes are losses. The theory is being made to take on more risk
than it has to, deliberately, and that is the whole point of the exercise.

And the reason it failed is not the reason anyone expected. **It did not fail
because the Gospels came out low.** It failed because **tragedy and myth turned out
to be indistinguishable from each other**, which nobody predicted and nobody has
explained.

**That detonates in Season 3 episode 18**, where a round's own sensitivity analysis
shows the **middle of the series does not exist.**

## The objection you must raise yourself, in this episode

There is an obvious one and it should come from you, not from a comment.

**A Christian testing whether the Gospels are special is not a neutral party.**

That is not a smear. It is simply true, and pretending otherwise is worse than
admitting it. The person who built this instrument wanted a particular answer.

**The project's response is not a denial.** It is a set of procedures a hostile
reader can check **without trusting anybody**: predictions committed before
scoring, falsifiers with numbers fixed in advance, sensitivity analyses declared
before the data existed, and a public record of every occasion the procedure
caught the person running it.

>> Say it plainly here: **you are not asking anyone to trust you. You are asking
>> them to check.** Season 7 is entirely about those procedures and the eight
>> times they fired.

## The clinical anchor

**The near-miss reported by the person who made the error.**

Same event as the incident report in episode 12. Same facts, same timeline, same
outcome. **Told from the position the other document structurally cannot occupy.**

And notice what makes it valuable, because it is not accuracy. The incident report
may well be more accurate. **What the near-miss has is a narrator who knows the
mechanism operated**, and that is the entire difference Girard is pointing at, in a
form you can hold in your hand.

## What would count against this

1. **A myth with a sustained dissenting voice**, which kills the universal
   negative from episode 12 and takes this episode with it.
2. **The Gospels showing no more dissent language than myth.** The whole
   distinction is empirical and it can simply fail to appear.
3. **Tragedy failing to sit in the middle.** Girard needs the gradient. **This is
   the one that actually happens.**

Thirteen episodes of a theory with no opposition is not teaching, it is

**Episode 14 is who says he is wrong**, and it comes before the readings on

@@ EPISODE 14 — WHO SAYS HE IS WRONG

## The first thirty seconds

>% Thirteen episodes of one man's theory, taught by somebody who finds it
>% convincing, with not one objection anywhere in them. That is not
>% teaching. A doctor who thinks of one diagnosis and then goes looking for
>% evidence has not diagnosed anything. He has picked a favourite and
>% collected agreement. So the list of everything else it could be goes up
>% now, before the texts.

## The stake

Thirteen episodes without an objection is recruitment, and this episode exists to
break that. **You are being handed the other side's best case before the readings
start**, so that when you go into the texts you are reading with both cases in
your head instead of one.

## Collecting thirteen episodes, and noticing what is missing from them

You have now had thirteen episodes of one man's account of human violence, presented
by someone who finds it persuasive, with no opposition anywhere in it.

>> **Thirteen episodes of a theory with no opposition is not teaching. It is
>> recruitment**, and the audience should be told that this episode exists for
>> that reason.

It comes **before** the readings on purpose. Meet the objections, then go and
look at the texts, so that you are reading with both cases in your head.

## The clinical anchor, and it is the whole episode

**A differential diagnosis.**

You do not confirm a diagnosis. You **list the alternatives and rule them out**,
and the list goes on the board before the treatment starts. A clinician who
considers one diagnosis and finds evidence for it has not diagnosed anything.
They have pattern-matched and then collected confirmations.

>> **Everything in episodes 1 to 13 is evidence FOR one hypothesis.** Not one
>> minute of it was spent on what else could produce the same pattern. **This
>> episode is the differential.**

**The character version: House at the whiteboard.** The list goes up first. The
whole ritual of that show is that naming the alternatives is the work, and the
answer is worthless if the list was never written.

## A note on citation, and it is the series' own rule turned inward

This project tiers its own sources. **V** means fetched and checked. **K** means
the argument and the person are real but the citation is unverified, so it is a
lead and not a fact.

**Apply that here.** The families of objection below are real and standard.
Wherever a specific name and page is needed, **mark it K and go and check before
it goes on camera.**

>> **Do not attribute a precise claim to a named scholar from memory.** The
>> project caught itself doing exactly that twice and was wrong both times.

## Objection 1 — the readings are selective

The commonest and the hardest.

**The charge:** Girard picks the myths that fit. Greek myth is enormous. Choose
forty stories out of thousands and you can demonstrate anything, and nothing in
the method tells you which stories were rejected or why.

**Why it is serious:** it is unanswerable from inside the reading. Producing
another good reading is another selected example. You cannot escape a selection
problem by selecting more.

>> **This is the objection the entire project exists to answer**, and it is why
>> the text tiers were written down and committed before any scoring. Say so here,
>> and then let Seasons 3 and 4 show how badly it went.

## Objection 2 — the founding murder has no evidence

**The charge:** the account requires that real communities really did kill real
victims and really did found their institutions on it, in prehistory. There is no
archaeological or ethnographic record of this happening.

**The strongest form**, and state it at its strongest: it is not that the evidence
is thin. It is that the claim is **structured so evidence is impossible**, because
the mechanism only works while nobody can see it, so any record would necessarily
be a record of the concealment.

>> Notice that this is the same shape as the unfalsifiability charge, and notice
>> where it lands: **squarely on the part of Girard that nothing in this project
>> tests.** Episodes 1 to 5 are exactly that part. **Be honest about it here.**

## Objection 3 — the account of sacrifice is contested by people who study sacrifice

**The charge:** scholars of actual sacrificial systems, including biblical and
rabbinic sacrifice, argue that Girard's picture of sacrifice as disguised
scapegoating does not match how those systems describe themselves or how they
worked. Sacrifice in the Hebrew Bible is largely a system of gift, thanksgiving
and purification, and the scapegoat rite of Leviticus 16 is **one rite among
many** rather than the key to all of them.

**Mark this K.** The names to check are the scholars of ancient Judaism who have
written directly against Girard. **Fetch them, quote them accurately, or drop the
episode.**

## Objection 4 — the theological one, and it cuts from inside

**The charge, from Christians:** if the Cross is primarily **the unveiling of a
mechanism**, then Girard has turned salvation into a sociological demonstration.
The Cross reveals something rather than doing something.

## Why this text first

It is Girard's single most important example, the one he returns to across

| test | what it asked | result |

## The story, for someone who has never read it

**Start with the grievance, because it is the crisis and everyone skips it.**

Dionysus was born in Thebes. His mother Semele died when Zeus appeared to her in
his true form. And Semele's own sisters, **including Agave**, say she made the
god up, slept with somebody ordinary, and was killed for the lie.

So the family is split about what happened, and **the god arrives to settle a
dispute about his own paternity.** That is where the play opens: not with a
monster at the gates but with **a family that cannot agree on a fact.**

Dionysus drives the women of Thebes out onto the mountain, Agave among them.

**Pentheus**, the young king, Agave's son, treats him as a foreign agitator
corrupting the city's women and has him arrested. The prison does not hold.

And then Dionysus offers Pentheus what he wants: **to see.** To go up the
mountain and watch. And to get close, Pentheus puts on women's clothes.

The women see him. **They do not see a man.** They see a lion. And they tear him
apart with their hands, **his mother leading**, and she carries his head down into
the city on a stick, believing it is a hunting trophy, and shows it proudly to
her father, who has to walk her, slowly, to what she is holding.

## The word for it, and you will hear it for six seasons

**Sparagmos.** The tearing apart of a victim by a crowd, with hands rather than
weapons.

It matters that Greek has a word for this at all. **A culture does not coin a
noun for something that happens once**, and Girard's whole case rests on the
mechanism being ordinary enough to leave vocabulary behind.

>> **Season 2 episode 9 is the day somebody nearly put that vocabulary in the
>> wrong bucket**, and the reason it would have been cheating is the distinction
>> from episode 7: the tearing is what the mob **does**, not what the victim is
>> **accused of**.

## What to read aloud

**The messenger's speech**, and then **the recognition scene** where Cadmus takes
Agave apart piece by piece until she sees.

Do not summarise either. Read them.

## Now do the four by hand, and be honest about the hard one

| stereotype | present | where |
|---|---|---|
| **crisis** | **yes, and it is everywhere** | a family that cannot agree what happened. A mother who cannot recognise her son. A king dressed as a woman. A man seen as an animal. A god taken for a foreigner |
| **crimes** | **weak** | see below |
| **marks** | **yes** | he is the **king**, the too-high mark from episode 8 |
| **violence** | **yes, in its purest form** | the entire female population of the city, with their hands, unanimous |

>> **Do the crimes row slowly, because it is the honest problem.**

Pentheus is not accused of parricide, or incest, or poisoning, or sorcery. You
can argue impiety, and it is a real reading, and it is **much thinner than the
other three.**

**But the deeper problem is this: the mob does not believe it is executing a
criminal at all. It believes it is killing a lion.** There is no accusation in
the killing, because there is no recognition in it.

>> **In Girard's own best example, the accusation stereotype is the weakest of the
>> four.** That is a real observation about the text, made by reading it, and it
>> belongs on camera before any statistics arrive.

## And now the thing this play does to episode 12's universal negative

Episode 12 said the differentia is **"no one ever questions the guilt."** A
universal negative. One counterexample kills it.

**So look for dissent in the *Bacchae*. It is there.**

**Cadmus and Tiresias** both try to talk Pentheus into accepting the god. They
argue with him directly, on stage, at length. That is a voice against the
prevailing verdict.

**And Cadmus, at the end**, walks Agave out of her delusion, which is the most
explicit unmasking of a lynching in Greek tragedy.

**So does the play falsify Girard?**

>> **Look at where the dissent sits.** Cadmus and Tiresias speak **before** the
>> killing. Cadmus speaks **after** it. **Nobody speaks during.** At the moment of
>> collective violence, on the mountain, the unanimity is total.

That is either a rescue or a fudge, and **you should say it is not obvious which.**
If dissent counts only when it happens at the exact moment of the killing, the
claim is much narrower than it sounded. If dissent counts whenever it appears, this
play has it and the universal negative is in trouble.

And be exact about why this is uncomfortable rather than merely open. **The
narrowing arrives after the counterexample**, which is the structure of every
rescued theory in history. A claim that said "no dissenting voice is ever heard"
meets a play with two dissenting voices in it, and the reply is that those do not
count because of where they stand in the text.

**The reply happens to be right**, and it is right for a reason internal to
Girard's mechanism: unanimity is what the mechanism requires at the moment it
operates, so dissent before and after is exactly what the theory would predict. But
notice that the argument would have been available whatever the play contained,
which is the property the whole series is watching out for.

>> **Season 4 builds an instrument to ask exactly this question, whether the dissent
>> language sits near the violence language, and the *Bacchae* comes out at
>> p = 0.414.** Plant that here, because this episode is where the question is born,
>> and it detonates in Season 4 episode 16, where the answer arrives.

## The character anchor

**King Kong.**

The crowd sees a monster. The film shows you an animal, frightened, in a city it
did not choose to enter, and the audience is never allowed to side fully with the
people doing the killing.

**The mob on the mountain is not lying about what it sees.** Agave genuinely
believes she is holding a lion. That is the part to hold on to, because it means
the accusation stereotype can be almost absent while the mechanism runs at full
strength: **you do not need to believe the victim is guilty. You need only to
believe he is not one of you.**

## The arithmetic to do on screen

The play is **17,180 words.** The killing is one messenger speech, roughly **600
words.**

Divide it. **The lynching is about 3.5% of the play.**

>> Any measurement that averages across the whole text is **diluting the event by
>> a factor of nearly thirty.** Understanding that single division is
>> understanding half of Season 3.

## What would count against this reading

1. **The four being read into the play rather than out of it.** This is objection

2. **Cadmus and Tiresias counting as the dissent Girard says never happens.** If

>% crime caused it, and curses him publicly, and questions everybody. The
>% investigation arrives at the king. This is a detective story where the

## The stake

The cleanest fit in existence, and the cleanness is the problem. **This episode
is about the moment a perfect example should make you more suspicious, not
less**, because the texts that fit too well are the ones that were chosen for
fitting.

## Collecting episode 15

The *Bacchae* is Girard's best example and it came apart in our hands: the
accusation stereotype was the weakest of the four, and the dissent turned out to
be in the wrong place.

**This play is the opposite problem.** It is the cleanest fit in existence, and
the cleanness is what should worry you.

## The story, for someone who has never read it

**Storr's translation, and the opening line to read cold is "My children, latest
born to Cadmus old."**

Thebes is dying. Crops failing, cattle dying, women miscarrying. **The city is
under plague and the play opens inside it**, with a delegation begging the king
to do something.

The king is **Oedipus**, who saved the city once before by solving the Sphinx's
riddle, and who is therefore exactly the man to solve this.

The oracle says: the plague is a pollution. The old king Laius was murdered and
the murderer is still here. **Find him and expel him and the city will live.**

So Oedipus opens an investigation. He curses the murderer publicly. He questions
the prophet Tiresias, who tells him to stop. He questions his wife's brother. He
questions a herdsman.

**And the investigation arrives at himself.** He killed Laius, who was his father.
He married Jocasta, who is his mother. Jocasta hangs herself. Oedipus blinds
himself with her brooches and is driven out.

## The structural feature nobody points at, and it is episode 3

**This is a detective story in which the detective is the criminal.**

Episode 3 was about rivals converging until you cannot tell them apart. Here the
collapse is not between two people. **It is between two roles inside one person.**
The investigator and the culprit, the healer and the pollution, the man looking
and the thing being looked for.

>> **The play is a machine for collapsing a distinction**, and it runs for fifteen
>> hundred lines doing exactly that. Which is a strange thing to find at the
>> centre of a theory about the collapse of distinctions.

**Tiresias is in this play too**, and he does the same job he does in the
*Bacchae*: he tells a king a true thing the king cannot hear, and he is abused for
it. Worth pointing out across the two episodes.

## The four, laid out by Sophocles himself

| stereotype | and the text does it out loud |
|---|---|
| **crisis** | Thebes **under plague**. Crops, cattle, childbirth. **The first scene**, before any plot |
| **crimes** | **parricide and incest.** Two of Girard's four crime families, named, both difference-erasing in exactly his sense |
| **marks** | a **foreigner** who turns out to be native. **Lame** from infancy, his feet pierced. And the **king** |
| **violence** | **expulsion.** He is driven out, and the city recovers |

**Read the opening scene aloud.** A city on its knees under plague, begging its
king for a cure, **and the cure turns out to be his own expulsion.**

## The marks stereotype, all three directions at once

Worth dwelling on. Oedipus is simultaneously:

- **too low**: exposed as an infant, crippled, raised by strangers, a man with no
  known parents
- **too high**: the king, saviour of the city, married to the queen
- **the outsider**: a Corinthian, or so everyone including him believes

>> **He is every category of victim mark in the theory, in one man, at once.**
>> Girard did not have to reach for this. Sophocles handed it to him.

## Now run episode 12's causal diagram on it, because the play is strange here

Episode 12's device: myth tells it forwards, Girard reconstructs it backwards.

**And this play stages the reconstruction on purpose.** We watch the community
**assemble** the accusation, live, scene by scene, over the course of an
afternoon.

So which is it?

**Reading one, for Girard.** Look at them building it. The crisis came first, the
need for a culprit came second, and the crime was assembled to fit. The play shows
you the machinery.

**Reading two, against.** No. They are not assembling it, they are **discovering**
it, with witnesses and evidence and a herdsman who was there. **The investigation
is competent and the conclusion is correct.**

>> **Say both and settle neither.** The play is the strongest evidence for the
>> theory and the strongest evidence that the theory is reading a detective story
>> as a lynching.

## The character anchor

**Zuko's Agni Kai with his father**, from the accusation side rather than the scar
side.

Everybody in that room can give you a lawful account of what happened. A boy spoke
out of turn in a war meeting, a challenge was issued under the rules, the duel was
fought, the outcome was the outcome. **Every procedural step is correct.** Nobody
present is lying about the sequence.

And what actually happened is a father needing a son to be the problem.

**That is the two readings of *Oedipus*, in one scene.** You cannot settle it by
checking whether the procedure was followed, because the procedure was followed.

## The clinical anchor

**A case conference that reaches the correct diagnosis and would have reached a
diagnosis regardless.**

The workup is real. The tests were indicated, the findings are genuine, the
conclusion fits. **And the meeting was convened because something had to be
decided by the end of it**, and everybody in the room knew that when they walked
in.

Being right does not tell you which of those two produced the answer, and the
notes will only record the first.

## The two numbers

It scores **21.23**, second highest of 72 texts.

And it holds the **highest `marks` score in the whole corpus, 9.58.**

Why? Because `marks` is **44% the word "king"**, and the title character is
addressed as king on nearly every page.

>> **The text that best fits the theory is also the single strongest piece of
>> evidence for the objection that the instrument is only detecting royalty.**

**Sit in that and do not resolve it.** Season 4 runs the experiment that settles
it, and **the result is not the one this episode makes you expect.**

## The thing about Sophocles nobody says

Here is a real problem for the theory, better coming from you than from a comment.

**Oedipus is guilty. He did it.** He killed his father and married his mother.
**The accusation is true.**

Girard's mechanism runs on victims selected arbitrarily, whose guilt is
manufactured by the peace that follows. And his cleanest textual fit is a case
where the charge is accurate and the investigation is sound.

>> **What does a theory built on arbitrary selection do with a victim who actually
>> did it?**

There is an answer available: the theory is about **the crowd's process**, not the
victim's record, and a real culprit can be scapegoated too. **Say that, and then say
that this project never tests it and the question stays open.**

And it is worth seeing how much that answer gives away, because it is a good answer
with a large bill attached. **If guilt is irrelevant to the mechanism, then
innocence cannot be the thing that distinguishes a Gospel from a myth**, and the
distinction has to live entirely in how the crowd behaves rather than in what the
victim did.

That is not a problem invented here. It is Girard's own position, stated in his own
sentence, and it is the sentence episode 12 read twice. **Hold on to it**, because
the instrument built in Season 3 fills its word-lists mostly with innocence
vocabulary anyway, and **Season 7 episode 4 is where that contradiction finally
does visible damage.**

@@ EPISODE 17 — READING APOLLODORUS

## The first thirty seconds

>% Take something you love and go and read its plot summary. In episode
>% nine, this one kills that one, and the sister finds out and leaves. Same
>% events, same order, same people, and none of the thing you actually
>% watched it for. Somebody did that to the whole of Greek myth in the
>% second century, and nobody meant it as an experiment.

## The stake

Myth with the storytelling stripped out. If the stereotypes survive that
stripping, they were never a style, they were a structure. **This episode is the
closest thing the season has to an experiment**, and its result is genuinely
ambiguous, which is the most honest thing about it.

## Collecting episodes 15 and 16

Two masterpieces. Two texts where every word is doing work, where the poetry and
the structure and the irony are inseparable from the content.

**This episode reads the opposite of that**, and the opposite turns out to be a
natural experiment nobody set up on purpose.

## What it is

The *Bibliotheca*. A systematic prose summary of **the whole of Greek myth**,
compiled around the first or second century AD. **Frazer's translation**, which is
the one `get_apollodorus.py` pulled off Theoi and the one every word count in this
episode was taken from.

Not a story. **A catalogue of stories.**

Everything is in it: the theogony, the houses of Argos and Thebes and Athens, the
labours, the war, the returns. All of it, at speed, one after another, with no
description, no scene-setting, no dialogue worth the name, no digression, and no
opinion.

## Read a page aloud and let the audience hear the speed

A character is born, marries, kills somebody, is transformed, and dies **in four
lines.** Then the next one. Then the next.

>> **Every sentence is an event.** There is no connective tissue, because
>> connective tissue is what a summary removes.

## The character anchor

**A Wikipedia plot summary.**

Everybody has read one. In episode nine, X kills Y; Z discovers the betrayal and
leaves. Same events, same order, same characters, **and none of the thing you
actually watched it for.**

Now use it as a test, because that is what this episode is going to do with
Apollodorus. **Take something you love and read its plot summary.** Whatever
survives the summary was in the events. Whatever evaporates was in the telling, and
you have just separated the two with no theory and no instrument.

**The *Bibliotheca* is Greek myth's plot summary**, written eighteen centuries
before anybody thought of doing that on purpose.

## And here is why this text is worth an episode

**Apollodorus is what myth looks like with the storytelling taken out.**

Same events. Same characters. Same crimes and killings and expulsions. **The plot
without the telling.**

Which sets up a question the corpus can actually answer:

>> **Are Girard's four stereotypes in the EVENTS, or in the TELLING?**
>>
>> If they are in the events, they should survive the removal of the poetry. **If
>> they are an artifact of tragic rhetoric, they should vanish here.**

Nobody designed that experiment. It is sitting in the corpus by accident, which is
worth saying out loud, because a natural experiment is the most valuable thing a
corpus can contain and **you only ever get one by noticing it.** Nobody could have
funded this. Nobody could have commissioned a poet to write a version of Greek myth
with the poetry removed. **Somebody in the first century did it for reasons of his
own, and the control group has been sitting on a shelf ever since.**

## The answer, and it cuts two ways

Mythography scores **13.95.** Tragedy scores **13.88.**

**A dead heat.** Strip out the poetry, the meter, the irony, the choral odes and
the characterisation, and the stereotype vocabulary is **exactly as dense.**

**Reading one, and this is the one the project took.** It is a **confound.** A
rate per thousand words rewards plot per word, and Apollodorus is nothing but
plot per word, so it scores like a tragedy without being one. Here is the
measurement that supports it, taken much later with a **neutral** event-word list
containing nothing Girardian:

| genre | neutral event-words per 1000 |
|---|---|
| **MYTHOGRAPHY** | **28.65** |
| EPIC | 22.68 |
| OVID | 21.73 |
| TRAGEDY | 21.45 |
| CONTROL | 20.77 |

**It is the densest narrative prose in the corpus by a wide margin**, and that is
a real finding.

**Reading two, which the project never made, and it is worth saying on camera.**

The stereotypes **survived the removal of the telling.** If they were a feature of
tragic rhetoric, a mood produced by choral lament and heightened diction, they
should have thinned out in a bare summary and they did not.

>> **That is arguably evidence FOR Girard**, and nowhere in eight rounds does
>> anybody say so. The tie was recorded as a problem. **It can also be read as the
>> stereotypes being structural rather than stylistic**, which is exactly what the
>> theory claims.

Say both. **Do not pick.** And note out loud that the project only ever wrote down
the unflattering reading, which is its own kind of bias and worth noticing in a
series about bias.

**And notice which direction that bias runs**, because it is not the direction
anybody warns you about. The whole apparatus of pre-registration exists to stop a
believer flattering his own theory. Here the believer had a result that favoured
him sitting in front of him, and filed it as a confound, and never wrote the other
sentence down.

>> That is not a virtue. **A researcher who systematically records one reading of
>> his ambiguous results is biased whichever way he leans**, and being biased
>> against yourself is still a way of not looking.

## The clinical anchor

**A discharge summary against the full notes.**

Every diagnosis is in the summary. Every drug, every procedure, every date.
**What is not in it is the reasoning.** The two days somebody was unsure, the
differential that was considered and dropped, the conversation that changed the
plan.

Read a hundred summaries and you will know what happened to a hundred patients and
almost nothing about how medicine is practised. **The events survive the
compression. The thinking does not**, and that is the experiment sitting in this
corpus by accident.

## And the twist that stops either reading being comfortable

Across all 73 texts, the correlation between event density and the Girard score is
**negative.** r = −0.243, p = 0.040.

**So density does not generally inflate the score. It deflates it.** Epic is dense
and scores low.

>> The confound is real **for mythography specifically** and **unproven as a
>> general mechanism.** Say plainly that the explanation does not exist. **This is
>> an open problem in the project, not a solved one**, and pretending otherwise
>> would be the exact thing this series is about.

## What would count against this reading

1. **The tie being too small to interpret in either direction.** It is 0.07, and
   **Season 3 episode 8 shows it sits inside a 17% noise floor.** Both readings
   offered above may be explanations of nothing, and that episode is where this one
   gets taken apart.
2. **Density explaining it entirely.** Measured, real, and it would make the
   survival of the stereotypes an artifact rather than evidence.
3. **The corpus imbalance.** Mythography is 8 files against epic's 36, for reasons
   Season 2 episode 4 shows have nothing to do with antiquity. **Nobody has
   reweighted and re-run.**

## Read book 7 aloud, because nobody will believe it otherwise

**It is a dinner party.**

A shipwrecked stranger arrives at a palace. He is received. He is bathed and given
clean clothes. He is seated, fed, and asked politely who he is. The king and
queen treat him with perfect courtesy. He tells them about his voyage.

**Zero violence. Nothing bad happens to anybody at all.**

**And on the victim-marks bucket it outscores every tragedy in the corpus except
*Oedipus the King***, which scores 9.58. Only two other texts beat it, and both
are prose compendia.

Meanwhile book 22, in which one man kills a hall full of a hundred people and then
hangs the serving women, is **beaten by a dinner party on the bucket that is
supposed to detect victims.**

## Why

"King" and "queen" saturate a palace scene, because everyone in it either is one or
is addressing one. And `marks` is 44% the word `king`.

>> **The least persecution-shaped narrative in Greek literature contains the most
>> persecution-shaped-looking passage in the corpus.** That sentence is the episode.

## The character anchor

**A smoke alarm going off because of toast.**

Nothing is broken. The alarm is not faulty, it has not been miswired, and it is not
being oversensitive in any way its manufacturer would apologise for. **It detects
smoke, and toast makes smoke.** Every step of its reasoning is correct.

And notice which repairs are on offer. **You cannot fix it by making it ignore
smoke**, because smoke is the thing it is for. You cannot fix it by adding a rule
about toast, because the next false alarm will be a shower or a candle or a hot
pan, and there is no finite list.

**`king` is the toast.** It genuinely is a victim mark, for the reasons episode 8
established, and a palace genuinely is full of kings, and the detector is working
exactly as designed while telling you something absurd.

## The clinical anchor

**A tumour marker that is also raised in pregnancy.**

The assay is specific to the molecule it names. It is not cross-reacting, it is not
contaminated, and the elevated result is a true measurement of a substance that is
genuinely present at that level. **Every step is correct and the patient does not
have cancer.**

Notice which repairs that rules out. You cannot lower the threshold, because the
value really is high. You cannot recalibrate, because nothing is miscalibrated.
**The molecule is a real marker that is not exclusive to the disease**, and the
only fix available is to stop reading it on its own and require it alongside
something pregnancy does not produce.

**That is the minimum**, arriving one season early.

## And the instrument gets the whole poem exactly right

This is what makes it interesting rather than merely embarrassing.

Across the 76-text corpus, the *Odyssey* holds **three of the four lowest
scores**, with book 5 at **2.35** against a corpus top of 23.18. **These are the
Murray books 1 to 5 on the fixed lexicon**, so they are the solid half of the
episode, and they are also only a fifth of the poem. **"The Odyssey scores low" is
a claim about five books**, and the honest sentence says five.

>> So the instrument reads the poem correctly and one chapter of it
>> catastrophically wrongly, **and the same word causes both.** `king` is why the
>> poem scores low overall, because most of it happens at sea. And `king` is why
>> book 7 scores absurdly high, because that part happens in a palace.

## What saves it, and this is where Season 2 is going

Score book 7 by the **minimum** of the four buckets instead of the sum, and it
scores **0.00** and vanishes, because its `crimes` bucket is empty.

**The conjunction requirement from episode 10 kills the false positive
automatically**, without anyone noticing it or patching anything.

>> **And this is the cell the re-run was really about.** `FINDINGS.md` says §2's
>> co-occurrence thresholds "no longer apply" after the lexicon fix, and the fix
>> took `crimes` from 43% to 12% `murder`, meaning words that never fired started
>> firing. **So the whole argument for the minimum turned on whether one cell was
>> still empty**, and for a year nobody looked. **It is still 0.00.** Both false
>> positives still have an empty `crimes` bucket and the minimum still kills both.

>> **Say what that is worth and what it is not.** A prediction that survives a
>> check it could have failed is worth more than one that was never checked.
>> **And it nearly did fail**, because the same re-run broke the two sentences
>> that were sitting next to it.

>> **That is the strongest argument for the minimum in the whole project**, and it
>> is genuinely good reasoning. **Season 3 episode 2 is where the minimum falls
>> apart for a completely different reason** that has nothing to do with this.

## What would count against this episode

1. **Book 7 being a fluke worth no attention.** One passage out of twenty-four, in
   one poem. **The counter is that the fix it motivates is the one Season 2 adopts
   as its primary metric**, so a fluke would mean the metric was chosen on the
   strength of an accident.
2. **The false positive being the lexicon's fault after all.** If `king` is a genre
   signal rather than a victim mark, this episode is not a lesson about metrics at
   all, it is a lesson about a broken word-list, and no clever combination rule
   repairs it. **Episode 8 left that question open on purpose and Season 4 settles
   it.**
3. **The whole-poem result being an artifact of length.** The *Odyssey* holds three
   of the four lowest scores in the corpus and it is also one of the longest texts
   in it. **Nobody has checked whether long texts score low generally**, which
   would explain the poem's position without any of the reasoning above.
