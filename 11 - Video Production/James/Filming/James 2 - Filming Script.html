James 2:1-13 — The Man With No Face

Scripture from the NIV. Beats run in delivery order. Quotes in the shaded blocks are verbatim and can be read aloud as they stand.

The passage

James 2:1-13

1 My brothers and sisters, believers in our glorious Lord Jesus Christ must not show favoritism.

2 Suppose a man comes into your meeting wearing a gold ring and fine clothes, and a poor man in filthy old clothes also comes in. 3 If you show special attention to the man wearing fine clothes and say, "Here's a good seat for you," but say to the poor man, "You stand there" or "Sit on the floor by my feet," 4 have you not discriminated among yourselves and become judges with evil thoughts?

5 Listen, my dear brothers and sisters: Has not God chosen those who are poor in the eyes of the world to be rich in faith and to inherit the kingdom he promised those who love him? 6 But you have dishonored the poor. Is it not the rich who are exploiting you? Are they not the ones who are dragging you into court? 7 Are they not the ones who are blaspheming the noble name of him to whom you belong?

8 If you really keep the royal law found in Scripture, "Love your neighbor as yourself," you are doing right. 9 But if you show favoritism, you sin and are convicted by the law as lawbreakers.

10 For whoever keeps the whole law and yet stumbles at just one point is guilty of breaking all of it. 11 For he who said, "You shall not commit adultery," also said, "You shall not murder." If you do not commit adultery but do commit murder, you have become a lawbreaker.

12 Speak and act as those who are going to be judged by the law that gives freedom, 13 because judgment without mercy will be shown to anyone who has not been merciful. Mercy triumphs over judgment.

Beat 1 — The contradiction that opens it

James forbids favoritism in verse 1. Four verses later he says God chose the poor. That is favoritism.

He is quoting Leviticus 19 throughout this passage, and Leviticus is even clearer than he is:

Leviticus 19:15. "Do not pervert justice; do not show partiality to the poor or favoritism to the great, but judge your neighbor fairly."

Do not favor the great. Do not favor the poor either. Perfectly balanced, perfectly reasonable. And then James 2:5, in the middle of an argument built on that chapter, says God chose the poor.

Either James is careless, or one word here is not being read correctly.

The word is the one translated "favoritism."

Beat 2 — Leviticus 19, and how close the two verses sit

James is not free-associating about rich people. He is reading one chapter of Leviticus and expecting the reader to know it.

  • James 2:8 quotes "Love your neighbor as yourself" and calls it the royal law. That is Leviticus 19:18.
  • James 2:9 condemns favoritism as lawbreaking. The law being broken is Leviticus 19:15.

Those two verses are three lines apart in the same chapter.

So James 2:8-9 is not one nice commandment followed by a separate rule about seating. It is a single reading of Leviticus 19, in which loving the neighbor and refusing to judge by the face are the same commandment, stated twice, a few verses apart, by the same author.

Beat 3 — The word

The Greek is prosōpolēmpsia (2:1), and the verb prosōpolēmpteite (2:9).

It means face-receiving. Face-taking.

It is a Semitism, built on the Hebrew idiom nasa panim, "to lift the face." The picture is a courtroom. The petitioner is prostrate on the ground. The judge lifts his face to see who it is, and then rules.

To receive the face is to decide the case by the face.

The word appears to be a Christian coinage. It is not classical Greek. Someone needed a noun for this sin and had to build one out of the Septuagint.

Three more words, because in each case the English hides the argument:

synagōgē (2:2). The NIV has "your meeting." James wrote synagogue. This is the only place in the New Testament where a Christian assembly gets that word. It means a real room with real seating.

diakrithēte / diakritai (2:4). "Have you not discriminated among yourselves and become judges." Same root as 1:6, where the one who doubts, diakrinomenos, is "like a wave of the sea, blown and tossed by the wind." The word for the man split inside himself is the word for the assembly split by class. One fracture, seen from inside and from outside.

katakauchatai (2:13). "Mercy triumphs over judgment." Not outweighs. Not replaces. It is a boasting-against, a verb of victory shouted in the loser's face.

Beat 4 — The ring is a sign, not a thing

Hilary of Arles, on verse 2:

"What James says here applies not just to rings but to any sign of wealth, for the ring is meant to stand for a treasure house of riches."

The ring is not wealth. The ring is a signifier of wealth. The man is not honored for what he has. He is honored for what the ring makes the room assume others think he has. Nobody audited his estate. They read a sign and deferred.

The historical detail sharpens it. The anulus aureus, the gold ring, was the legal badge of the Roman equestrian order. Not jewelry. Rank you could wear.

So a man walks into a Christian synagogue wearing his civic rank on his hand, and the church reads it correctly and seats him accordingly. Everyone in the room does their job properly. That is the problem.

Beat 5 — The assembly builds a courtroom, then gets dragged into one

2:4: have you not become judges with evil thoughts?

2:6: are they not the ones dragging you into court?

Two verses apart, and placed there on purpose.

The church is playing judge in verse 4. In verse 6 it is being hauled before actual judges. It has been persecuted by the courts of the rich, and its response is to build a small courtroom of its own and seat people by rank inside it.

The victim copies the machine that victimizes him. That is the most ordinary thing in the world, which is why nobody in the room can see it.

And notice what James does not do. He names the oppressors precisely. They exploit you, they drag you to court, they blaspheme the name. He has a perfect target, and the grievance is completely legitimate. He does not use it. He turns around and tells the church to stop imitating them.

He refuses the counter-lynching, and he refuses it while holding a justified one in his hand.

Beat 6 — Why "guilty of all" is not what it sounds like

2:10. "Whoever keeps the whole law and yet stumbles at just one point is guilty of breaking all of it."

The modern ear hears cruelty. Absurd legal maximalism. The God who fails you for one dropped stitch.

Augustine was bothered by it too. His Letter 167 exists for no other reason. It is a letter to Jerome about this one verse, and he cannot let it go:

"Is it possible that the person who has discriminated between rich and poor is guilty of murder, adultery and sacrilege? That does indeed seem to be the conclusion which James is drawing."

The answer is in the two examples James picks. Hilary of Arles:

"Why does James choose these two commandments as his examples? Because they are the ones which deal most closely with loving and with hating one's neighbors."

Adultery and murder are both takings of the neighbor. Adultery takes his wife. Murder takes his life. Both begin as desire pointed at what belongs to someone else.

So James names favoritism, adultery, and murder, and says they are one offense. And they are one offense. All three treat the neighbor as an object in a rivalry. Favoritism prices him. Adultery takes what is his. Murder removes him. The intensity differs. The act does not.

The law is one because the neighbor is one. A relationship cannot be broken partially the way a checklist can be failed partially. Break the relation anywhere and the relation is broken.

That is why "guilty of all" follows from "love your neighbor as yourself" instead of contradicting it. And it is why James can call this same law royal and freedom-giving two verses later without any irony.

Caesarius of Arles, compressed:

"What does it mean to offend in one point and lose all, except to have fallen from the precept of love and thereby to have offended in all the other commandments?"

Beat 7 — A law that gives freedom

2:12. "Speak and act as those who are going to be judged by the law that gives freedom."

A law that gives freedom is a contradiction on its face.

Chesterton's version of the same paradox, from Orthodoxy:

"Doctrines had to be defined within strict limits, even in order that man might enjoy general human liberties. The Church had to be careful, if only that the world might be careless."

Oecumenius got there a thousand years earlier, and he inverted the whole scene:

"The law of liberty is the one which does not recognize classes of persons. This is the law of Christ. Whoever shows favoritism is not free but a slave, for 'a man is a slave to the one by whom he has been overcome.'"

The man doing the seating thinks he is the free one. He is the host. He has the authority. He assigns the chairs. Oecumenius says he is the slave in the room. He has been overcome by the ring.

He is not seating the rich man. The rich man's ring is seating him.

The only free man in the story is the one on the floor.

Beat 8 — Back to the contradiction, and why it holds

The reasonable reading of this passage blends the two halves into something mild. Be fair to everyone. Everyone is equal. And the paragraph dies of it.

Chesterton on why the blend is the error:

"It has kept them side by side like two strong colours, red and white... It has always had a healthy hatred of pink. It hates that combination of two colours which is the feeble expedient of the philosophers."

James keeps both colors at full strength. The prohibition on favoritism is absolute in 2:1. God's preference for the poor is fierce in 2:5. Not averaged. Both.

And the resolution is not a compromise between them. It is that the two acts are not the same operation.

Augustine, on verse 5:

"It is by choosing the poor that God makes them rich in faith, just as he makes them heirs of the kingdom. It is rightly said that he chose this faith in them, since it was in order to bring it about that he chose them."

The choosing does not detect a quality that was already there. The choosing creates it.

So compare the two operations in the room:

  • The assembly reads a sign, prices the man, and seats him. It detects a value the world assigned already. It is a pricing engine, entirely downstream of Rome's valuation. It adds nothing. It is not even wrong creatively. It just repeats the answer it was handed.
  • God chooses the man with no sign, and the choosing makes him rich. It creates a value that was not there. It is upstream of everything.

One of those is favoritism. The other is creation. Favoritism reads a worth that already exists. That is what makes it favoritism.

Beat 9 — Why the commandment was never going to be enough

Zizioulas, in Being as Communion, on the two ways a person can exist:

"When man loves as a biological hypostasis, he inevitably excludes others: the family has priority in love over 'strangers'... a characteristic of the ecclesial hypostasis is the capacity of the person to love without exclusiveness, and to do this not out of conformity with a moral commandment ('Love thy neighbor,' etc.), but out of his 'hypostatic constitution,' out of the fact that his new birth from the womb of the Church has made him part of a network of relationships which transcends every exclusiveness."

Set that against James 2:8, where the royal law is, word for word, love your neighbor as yourself.

Zizioulas is saying the commandment is not enough. Ordering a biological hypostasis to produce non-exclusive love does not work, because exclusion is not a malfunction of it. Exclusion is what it is.

Which means the church seating the rich man is not being wicked. It is being natural. That is worse, and it is more interesting.

And James already knew. 1:18: "He chose to give us birth through the word of truth."

Chose, and birth. Both terms, in the first chapter, before a single commandment arrives. The order is not commandment and then obedience. It is birth, then nature, then act.

Beat 10 — The close

Zizioulas on the pagan word:

"In the ancient Greek world for someone to be a person means that he has something added to his being; the 'person' is not his true 'hypostasis.'"

And on Rome:

"Persona is the role which one plays in one's social or legal relationships, the moral or 'legal' person which either collectively or individually has nothing to do with the ontology of the person."

Prosōpon meant mask. The role. The face worn in the theatre or the forum. The identity the state issued you.

And the sin James names is prosōpolēmpsia. Receiving the mask.

Then Zizioulas on what damnation actually is:

"Condemnation to eternal death is nothing other than a person's being allowed to decline into a 'thing,' into absolute anonymity, to hear the terrifying words, 'I do not know you.'"

And on what is lost outside communion: the person "becomes a being like other beings, a 'thing' without absolute 'identity' and 'name,' without a face."

Without a face.

Now go back to the room. Neither man in verses 2 and 3 has a name. But the rich man has a sign, and the sign does the work a name would do. The poor man has nothing. He is described entirely by what he lacks. Filthy old clothes. And he is told, sit on the floor by my feet.

The sin of face-receiving has produced a man with no face.

That is what it does. It hands out faces to the people the world already gave one to, and to the man with no mask it gives nothing. And a man with no mask, no name, and no seat has been allowed to decline into a thing. Not killed. Just seated.

And Matthew 25, the passage James 2:13 is standing on, ends on the words Zizioulas quotes. "I never knew you."

The judgment for treating men as things is to be treated as a thing. To be unknown. To be, at the last, the man on the floor.

The caveat, said plainly on camera

James is using a Septuagint idiom, not fourth-century Trinitarian vocabulary. The Cappadocians are three hundred years downstream of him. He did not mean "do not receive the pagan ontology of the person," and anyone who tells you he did is selling something.

So this is a convergence, not an intention.

It is still worth the whole video, because the same word had to be fought over twice. Once as a sin in an assembly, once as a term in a creed. Both fights landed on the same answer.

The mask is not the man.

The open loop into the next episode

The poor man in verse 2 arrives in shabby clothes.

Thirteen verses later, in 2:15-16: "Suppose a brother or a sister is without clothes and daily food. If one of you says to them, 'Go in peace; keep warm and well fed,' but does nothing about their physical needs, what good is it?"

Same man. Same clothes.

The works in the famous faith-and-works argument are not abstract works. They are defined by pointing back at the guy from the top of the chapter. James never changed the subject.

And verse 13 is the hinge, not a closing flourish. "Mercy triumphs over judgment," and then immediately, "What good is it if someone claims to have faith but has no deeds?" The deeds are the mercy.

Which means James's works and Paul's works of the law are not the same object. That is next time.
