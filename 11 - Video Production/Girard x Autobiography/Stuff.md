Here's a better prompt structure for what you're doing:
Phase 1 — Backbone extraction (one chapter at a time):

"From Chapter X of Cold Case Christianity only, give me the single most important cause-and-consequence chain. Not a summary. One chain. Then tell me what concept has to exist first before anything else in this chapter makes sense."

Phase 2 — Cross-book relational stitching:

"Take that backbone concept from Wallace. Now find the ONE moment in Strobel or Lewis where the same logic appears but from a different angle. Show me the relationship between them, not just that they agree."

Phase 3 — GRIND backbone building:

"Using only what we've established so far as confirmed backbone pieces, what would the CENTER node of a GRIND map be for this topic? Not a topic label — the actual tension or question that everything else answers."



-----

Step 1 — Test the backbone before expanding it
Send this prompt:

"Using only the three cause-consequence chains and the center node we established, can these three chains be collapsed into ONE single chain that feeds into the center question? If yes, show me. If no, tell me why they must stay separate."

This tells you if your backbone is truly connected or if it's still three islands dressed as one.

Step 2 — Once confirmed, expand ONE node
Pick the chain that feels weakest in your understanding and send:

"Take [the Chapter X chain]. Expand ONLY the CAUSE side. What are the 2-3 sub-causes that produce it? Do not touch the consequence side yet."

You expand one side at a time, otherwise you get a bloated response that loses the thread.



## The Core Prompt For Any Fresh Text

Paste the passage then write:

**"I don't understand this yet. Don't summarize it. Ask me questions about it until I figure out what it's saying."**

This forces the AI into Socratic mode. You wrestle. It guides. You arrive at understanding yourself.

That arrival is what makes it stick.

---

## When You Think You Understood Something

**"I think this passage is saying [your attempt]. What's the most important thing my understanding is missing?"**

Not "am I right." Specifically what's missing. Forces precision.

---

## When You Understand It But Can't Connect It

**"I understand [concept] on its own. I understand [other concept] on its own. I cannot see how they're actually one argument. Show me the single hinge that connects them."**

One hinge. Not a list. The single point where they lock together.

---

## When You Need To Know If It Actually Matters

**"If this argument is completely wrong — what changes? What does the person arguing against God get to say that they couldn't say before?"**

This tells you the actual weight of the argument. What it's load bearing for.

---

## When You're About To Use It In Conversation

**"I'm about to argue [this] to someone who believes [that]. What's the one thing I'm most likely to say that will make me sound like I don't actually understand what I'm arguing?"**

This finds the gap between thinking you understand and actually understanding.
