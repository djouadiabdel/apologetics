## The argument as it is usually run

Nobody dies for something they know is a lie. The disciples died. So they were not lying. So it happened.

The first two steps are fine. The third does not follow, and the gap between the second and the third is where this whole file lives.

## Alex O'Connor concedes it and wins anyway

He gives the response in one move, and it is the correct one.

> I don't think I can actually historically defend that anyone died for something
> that they knew to be a lie. I think that this can be explained by them being
> deceived. They could just be wrong about what they saw.

He grants the sincerity and takes the conclusion regardless. That is what an argument looks like when it terminates one step short.

## The martyrdom traditions are also thinner than advertised

You hear about the twelve. The early attestation covers three or four.

- **James son of Zebedee**, killed by the sword. Acts 12:2.
- **James the brother of Jesus**, stoned. Josephus, *Antiquities* 20.200, an outside source with no interest in helping.
- **Peter and Paul**, in 1 Clement 5, around AD 96. Their deaths, without the details people usually attach.

Everything else is later. Peter crucified upside down comes from the Acts of Peter, second century. Thomas in India comes from the Acts of Thomas, third century.

My own Layer 2 round predicted these would score badly and they do. Argue three deaths that are attested. Do not argue twelve.

## What sincerity actually buys

It is not nothing. Three things.

**It removes conspiracy.** That matters, because deliberate fraud is the cheapest alternative to propose and it costs a critic nothing to say. Sincerity takes it off the table, and it takes it off honestly rather than by assertion.

**It changes the question.** From "were they lying" to "were they mistaken." Those are different conversations and the second one is the real one.

**It constrains what kind of mistake is available.** Whatever they experienced, they reported it as extended, physical, repeated, in groups, across locations, over weeks. So a proposed mistake has to be large enough to cover all of that. A shape in the dark does not.

## The argument that should replace it

Not "they died for it." Instead: what would have to be true for a mistake to produce *this* set of reports.

That is the hallucination hypothesis and it is where the serious work is. Take it from the people who actually argue it, Gerd Lüdemann and Michael Goulder, and from Dale Allison who takes it seriously without endorsing it. Never from an apologetics site's summary of them.

## The ceiling, again

Eliminate fraud and constrain the mistake, and you have arrived at "something happened that they could not explain."

The last step from there to resurrection is not carried by evidence. It is carried by what you already thought was possible. See [[Naturalism as a Presupposition — Not a Conclusion]].

## Why this file exists

Sincerity is the cheapest thing in the world to establish and the least valuable thing to have established.

Everyone is sincere. The men who flew planes into buildings were sincere. Joseph Smith may well have been sincere. If sincerity carried content across, every religion would be true at once.

So any argument that ends at "he was not lying" has ended one step early, and the person you are talking to will notice.

It is the same shape as the isnad problem. A sound chain of transmission tells you the report came down intact. It does not tell you the report was right in the first place. An ex-Muslim will recognise this instantly, because taking that inference apart is how they got out.

Do not build on a floor you would not accept from anybody else.
