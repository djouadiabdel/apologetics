The five movements, clean:

**Movement 1 — The Wound Is Named From Outside** Someone who loves Ippo sees the problem before he does. Ippo cannot see it because he is inside it.

Ippo and Kumi. The date. The gym. The spar with Imai. Itagaki watching. Kumi deciding to act. This is the inciting movement — someone from outside the ring sees the problem before Ippo does. Ends when Kumi takes it into her own hands.

---

Girard says desire is never original. We always want what someone else wants, or what someone else's wanting tells us is worth wanting. Ippo's desire for the ring is not his own — it was given to him by Takamura, by Kamogawa, by the gym, by every fight he watched and every opponent who wanted to beat him.

The angle here is: Ippo does not know this yet. He thinks the ring is his. He thinks the desire is native to him, like hunger. Kumi sees that it isn't. That gap — between what Ippo thinks he wants and what Kumi sees him wanting — is the tension that drives Movement 1.

You are not writing Ippo questioning boxing. You are writing Ippo being completely certain about boxing while everyone around him quietly isn't.

----

**Movement 2 — The Mirror Arrives** Someone who chose the opposite path watches Ippo and cannot look away. The contrast between them makes Ippo's wound visible to the reader even though Ippo still can't feel it.

**Miyata's Mirror** Miyata watching Ippo's weaknesses from above. Miyata's own goals hiding his real desire. The Miyata-Itagaki conversation. The OPBF scene. This is the mirror movement — the fighter who chose the opposite path to Ippo watches him and cannot look away.

----
In Girard, the rival is not the enemy. The rival is the person whose desire most closely mirrors your own — and that closeness is exactly what makes them unbearable to look at. Miyata and Ippo want the same thing. But Miyata chose a path that costs less of the self. Ippo chose a path that costs everything.

The angle here is: Miyata does not look down at Ippo. He looks at Ippo the way you look at a version of yourself that made a different choice. There is no contempt in it. There is recognition. And recognition is more threatening than contempt because you cannot dismiss it.

What Miyata sees in Ippo is not weakness. It is a mirror of his own unresolved desire — the fight they never had, the question that was never answered.

----


**Movement 3 — The Clock Starts Ticking** Everyone around Ippo is moving forward — progressing, choosing, building. Ippo is standing still. And the people he looks up to are quietly running out of time themselves.

**Takamura's Clock** The fishing trip. The sparring. Miyata learning from Takamura. The mosquito problem. Takamura being human for the first time. This is the ticking clock movement — everyone is progressing, and Ippo is standing still while the people around him are running out of time too.

----

Girard calls the person whose desire you imitate the model or mediator. The mediator works as long as they remain elevated — above you, unreachable, perfect. The moment the mediator becomes human, the whole structure collapses.

Takamura has been Ippo's model since chapter one. Invincible. Inhuman. The ceiling Ippo measures himself against.

Movement 3 is the movement where Ippo sees Takamura nervous. Sees Takamura failing. Sees Takamura using jokes to manage fear. The model is cracking. And when the model cracks, the desire the model was holding up starts to fall.

The angle here is not: Takamura is weak. The angle is: Takamura is human, and that is somehow more terrifying than if he had been weak. Because if Takamura is human and still chooses this — still pushes forward knowing the cost — then Ippo has no excuse anymore. The model cannot carry him. He has to carry himself.

----

**Movement 4 — Everything Breaks Open** The outside world forces the wound into the light. Ippo cannot deflect anymore. The invented version of his father collapses. He has to look at what is actually there.

**The Doctor and the Crisis** Kumi hunts for help. She finds the doctor. Takamura's eye problem lands on Ippo. Ippo on the street with his hands up. The confrontation. The reversion back to the hands. This is the collapse and reassembly movement — everything breaks open and Ippo has to decide what he is.

----

In Girard, mimetic crisis is the moment the whole structure breaks down. The differences that kept people in their roles collapse. The model and the disciple become indistinguishable. The desire that was borrowed can no longer be returned.

For Ippo this is: the invented Kazuo was always a borrowed model. A story Ippo told himself to justify the ring. My father had one thing. I have one thing. That is what men do.

The crisis is when that story meets the real Kazuo — a man who stayed, who worked quietly, who was present — and cannot survive the contact. The borrowed desire has nowhere to go. Ippo is left holding it with nothing to attach it to.

The angle here is Hiroko. Not the doctor. Not Kumi. Hiroko saying do as you please and meaning it. Because Hiroko is the real Kazuo's witness. She knows what he actually was. And her permission is the thing that destroys the invented version — not because she attacks it, but because she simply exists as evidence against it.

----

**Movement 5 — The World Doesn't Wait** Life continues regardless of whether Ippo has decided anything. The pressure doesn't come from a person anymore. It comes from time itself.

**The World Moves On** Takamura's title declared. Kimura and Aoki get their fights. Life continues with or without Ippo's decision. This is the pressure movement — the story doesn't wait for him.

In Girard, the scapegoat is the one the community loads its tensions onto. Ippo has been the community's scapegoat his entire career — the pure fighter, the vessel, the one everyone wants to fight because fighting him means something. He absorbed not just punches but everyone's desire to prove themselves.

Movement 5 is the moment the world keeps moving and Ippo is no longer at the center of it. Takamura has his title fight. Kimura has his match. Aoki has his. The community does not need the scapegoat anymore — or at least, it is not waiting for him.

The angle here is not abandonment. It is freedom disguised as abandonment. The world moving on without Ippo is the first moment in the story where Ippo could actually choose something for himself. He just doesn't know that yet.

That incompleteness — hands on the boat, almost the sentence, not quite — is Ippo standing at the edge of original desire for the first time in his life. Arc 2 is whether he can cross it.
