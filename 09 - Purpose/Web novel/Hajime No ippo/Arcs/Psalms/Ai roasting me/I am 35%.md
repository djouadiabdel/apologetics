When you started this project, your raw drafts were event lists. Things happened in order. Characters did stuff. The body wasn't on the page — emotions were named, not felt. If I score that starting point as 0% and a published literary writer who has fully internalized body-first prose as 100%, here's where you are:

**You're at about 35%.**

That sounds low but it's not. Here's why.

The jump from 0 to 35 is the hardest jump in writing. It's the jump from "telling the reader what happens" to "making the reader's body feel what happens." Most people who try to write fiction never make that jump at all. They write for years and stay at 15-20% — competent event narration with occasional good lines. You crossed into sensory thinking in a few months. The light burning Ippo's eyes, the arm that won't obey, recognizing Kamogawa by the feel of his wrinkled hands — that's not imitation. That's your nervous system learning to put itself on the page.

**What the remaining 65% looks like:**

About 25% of it is pacing — you still compress. You put four gifts in one hand. Your sentences carry too much weight because your brain generates images faster than your patience allows you to lay them down one at a time. The slowness you felt writing this chapter? That's the 35% fighting the habit. The habit wants to go fast. The craft wants you to stay.

About 20% is knowing when to stop. Your Kamogawa monologue — "it was my fault coach not yours" — that's you reaching for words when the body has already said everything. The bandage refusal _is_ the scene. Everything Ippo says out loud after that is weaker than what his hands already told us. Learning when the body has finished the sentence and the mouth should stay shut — that takes years. You're not there yet but you _felt_ it this time. You said yourself you didn't know what to hunt for after the hands. That confusion was your taste recognizing that the body had already delivered the beat and the dialogue was filler.

About 15% is consistency. Your best lines in this draft are genuinely at 65-70% — professional-adjacent, alive, doing real work. But they live next to lines that are at 20%. The gap between your peaks and your valleys is wide. Closing that gap — making your _worst_ sentence in a chapter as strong as your _best_ — that's what separates someone who has talent from someone who has a craft. That's the long road. That takes hundreds of chapters.

The last 5% is voice — the thing that makes your writing sound like _you_ and nobody else. You can't chase this. It arrives on its own after the other 60% is built. You'll look back at chapter 50 or 60 and notice that your sentences have a rhythm that isn't Camus and isn't Bukowski and isn't me. That's when you'll know.
