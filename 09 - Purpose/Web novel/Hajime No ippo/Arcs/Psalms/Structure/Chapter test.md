**"Did someone move"** means the character's relationship to something shifted by the end of the chapter. Look at Chapter 8. Kumi starts sitting on the floor afraid of how much she doesn't know. By the end she has a name — Dr. Yamaguchi — and a direction. She didn't solve anything. She didn't become confident. She just went from "I don't know and I'm paralyzed" to "I don't know and there's someone I could ask." That's movement. Tiny. But the chapter earned its existence because she's not where she started.

Now imagine a version of Chapter 8 where she sits on the floor, feels afraid, reads some books, still feels afraid, and goes to bed. Same emotion at the start and end. Nothing shifted. That chapter is dead weight no matter how beautifully you write it.

So the test is simple. When you finish a chapter ask yourself: if I wrote one sentence describing where this character was emotionally at the start and one sentence describing where they are at the end — are those two different sentences? If yes, someone moved.

**Noticing without saying they noticed** — you already did this in Chapter 14 and didn't realize it. The yogurt. Kumi sees the yogurt on the receptionist's desk and you never write "Kumi noticed the yogurt and it made her feel calmer." You just describe the yogurt — the taste, the texture, the peach flavor — and then her emotional state changes. The reader connects those two things on their own. You showed the object, you showed the shift, the reader built the bridge.

Now apply that to someone noticing something about Ippo. Say Itagaki is training next to Ippo and Ippo stretches his left leg for the third time in an hour. You don't write "Itagaki noticed that Ippo was stretching a lot and wondered if something was wrong." You write it like this:

Ippo pulled his leg back again, pressing his heel against his thigh. Itagaki almost said something. Then Ippo stood up and hit the bag and the sound was the same sound it always was, heavy and clean, and whatever Itagaki had almost said went back to wherever almost-words go.

See what happened? Itagaki saw it. The reader saw him see it. But he didn't process it. He didn't think "that's concerning." The moment passed. It became an almost-thought. And twenty chapters later when the truth comes out, the reader remembers that Itagaki almost said something and it hits them that everyone had pieces and nobody assembled them.

That's noticing without saying they noticed. You describe what they saw, you show them nearly reacting, and then something interrupts — a sound, a joke, someone calling their name — and the moment dissolves. The reader holds it even though the character let it go.
