the sacred writer from the distant mountain

**Phase 1 — He's furniture (3-4 mentions across multiple chapters)**

He's just "the new reporter" or "the guy with Fujii." No name. He appears in press rows, post-fight press areas, the gym lobby. One line descriptions buried in paragraphs about other things. "A young man next to Fujii was writing something in a notebook, not even watching the ring." Someone mentions Fujii has a new assistant. Mari makes a passing comment like "he's talented but he's never been punched so he'll never understand what he's looking at." That's it. The reader registers him the way you register background characters — barely.

**Phase 2 — He has a voice but no face (2-3 moments)**

Now you give him dialogue without giving him focus. He asks a question at a press conference that's weirdly philosophical — something about why fighters return to the ring after brain scans show damage. The room goes quiet. Ippo hears the question and it lingers with him but the chapter moves on. Another time, Fujii quotes something the kid wrote in an article and another journalist says "that's not sports writing, that's literature." Fujii just smiles. You're building the sense that this person sees differently than everyone else in the room. But he's still not a POV character. He's still observed from the outside.

**Phase 3 — He gets a name and a single defining scene (1 chapter)**

One chapter where a main character — probably Ippo or Takamura — actually interacts with him directly. Maybe Ippo finds him outside the gym after a fight, sitting on the steps reading something (give him a specific book, something philosophical, something that signals his inner world to YOUR readers who know Girard). They have a short conversation. The reporter says something unexpectedly sharp about what he just watched. Not praise, not criticism, something that shows he understood the fight at a level that surprises Ippo. Then he says something dismissive about boxing itself — "I still think it's barbaric, but you're not" — and leaves. NOW the reader is curious. Who is this guy? First chapter where his name appears. End of interaction. Don't over-explain.

**Phase 4 — We see him through someone else's eyes (1-2 chapters)**

Fujii or Mari gets a scene where they discuss him privately. This is where you drop backstory naturally — the novel awards in high school, the fact that he's brilliant but has no life experience, that they hired him specifically to shake him out of his shell. Mari says something like "he writes like someone who's read about suffering but never sat in it." Fujii defends him — "that's why he's here." This conversation does two things: it makes the reader invested in his potential, and it frames his upcoming arc as something the existing characters are already watching for. The reader now shares their anticipation.

**Phase 5 — His first POV chapter**

NOW you give him interiority. And when you do, it should feel like a door opening into a completely different manga. His internal voice is nothing like Ippo's. It's literary, self-aware, slightly pretentious, full of philosophical references. He's at a fight and he's narrating it in his head like he's drafting a novel — aestheticizing the violence, keeping it at arm's length through language. But then something breaks through. A moment in the fight that his prose can't contain. His narration stutters. He stops composing sentences and just watches. First crack in the wall.

From here you're launched into his actual arc.

**The structural logic:** each phase doubles the space he occupies. Background mention (one line) → dialogue without focus (a few lines) → a single scene (half a chapter) → discussed by others (a chapter) → his own POV (full chapter). The reader's attention grows proportionally. By the time he gets his own chapters, the reader already wants them.

The reporter starts with intellectual contempt for boxing — "these guys destroy their brains for entertainment." He's the rational outsider, the guy who does cardio and eats clean because he treats his body as a vessel for his mind. He thinks he's above mimetic desire. He thinks he's self-directed. Classic Girardian delusion — the person who believes they're free from imitation is usually the most enslaved by it.

Then Fujii and Mari scout him precisely BECAUSE he's sheltered. They don't need another boxing journalist, they need someone who can actually write. But what he doesn't realize is that they're not just giving him a job — they're throwing him into an experiential world that will shatter his carefully maintained distance from life.

The conversion happens through writing itself. He starts writing about boxing cynically, clinically. Then he watches a fight that moves him and he can't articulate why. His prose starts changing before his beliefs do — that's the tell. He starts writing with heat, with rhythm, and he notices it and it scares him. The craft betrays him into caring.

And the Ippo connection is genius — he's the one who eventually writes the piece about cumulative head trauma that forces Ippo and everyone around him to confront what they've been ignoring. So his arc serves your main plot mechanically, not just thematically.

For making him relatable to you specifically: give him your relationship to language. He thinks in English even though his world is Japanese. He reads Girard or some philosophical equivalent. He's Christian in a non-Christian environment. He's physically disciplined but channels it into "safe" exercise rather than combat. He had early creative success (novel awards) that made him think craft alone was enough, and now he's learning that craft without lived experience produces beautiful emptiness.
