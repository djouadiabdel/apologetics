# The Messenger

The village inside me was sacked while I was asleep, and when I woke there was nothing left standing. I do not know the night it happened. I only know I woke into the after — the smoke still hanging, the wells fouled, the small good things of a life pulled down and burned, and the dead lying where they fell, and me sitting in the middle of the ash with my arms fallen straight down at my sides. Whatever army it was had not even bothered to take me. It had left me alive in the ruins, which is the crueler thing, because a man left alive in his own burned village has to keep being the one who remembers what it looked like before. At night I dreamed it sacked again, and woke in sweat and tears, and the sweat and the tears were the only water left in the place.

This is where the messenger found me.

He came with light in his hair. Abdou — long hair, gone gold at the ends, a brightness on him that did not belong to the ash, and he came down into the ruined village as if the smoke were nothing, as if he could not even see the dead, and he held out his hand and said _come on, get up, I'm taking you out of here._ He had places to show me. He wanted to lift me up over the burned walls and fly me out across the world that was still alive somewhere past the edge of my ruin.

I would not take the hand.

I want to be exact about this, because it is the shameful center and the shameful center is always the true one. It was not that I could not see the hand. I saw it clearly. It was that something in me had decided a man whose village is ash deserves to sit in the ash, that to take the bright hand would be to admit I was the kind of creature that needs lifting, weak, small, ruined, a thing that cannot stand on its own. The same gravity that had turned me from a woman's warm arm now turned me from a friend's open hand. I trusted no one with the smoke. If I let him fly me up he would see, from the air, exactly how total the burning had been, and I would rather sit in the ruin intact and unseen than be carried up and known. So I said I was fine. I said the village was fine. I sat in the ash and smiled the arranged smile and let him think the smoke was weather.

He came back. That is the thing about a true messenger — he is not defeated, only delayed. Something held him off from me for a long time, days that stacked into a month, a power I could not see that stood between his hand and my taking it, some prince of the air whose whole work was to keep me in the ruin, whispering the one sentence the curse always whispers: _stay down, you are cursed, you gave nothing and you are nothing and the ash is what you are._ And every time the hand reached through that whisper I slapped it down with a joke. But Abdou kept coming back down into the smoke. He did not stop coming. He outlasted the prince by the simple animal stubbornness of a friend who will not leave you in a fire.

And one day — I cannot tell you why that day — I was too tired to slap the hand down. I let it close around mine. And he took me up.

We went up out of the ash. The burned walls dropped away below me and I saw the whole of my ruin from the air at last, the entire sacked village laid out small and smoking, and the seeing did not kill me the way I had been so sure it would. It just hurt, cleanly, the way feeling comes back into a frozen hand. And I understood, going up, that the thing I had been protecting by staying in the ash was not my dignity. It was the prince's lie. I had been guarding the very fire that burned me, calling the guarding strength.

But going up is not the same as escaping, and the messenger did not pretend it was. He did not fly me away from the ruin into some clean country where the burning had not happened. He set me down on a height above it, where I could still see the smoke, and there was a cross there, heavy, the old kind with the three bars, the lowest one slanted — one end of it lifted toward the sky, the other dropped toward the ground, as if the wood itself were a needle showing which way a soul can go. And the meaning came to me without words, the way it does up there: _the village is not yours to un-burn. Pick this up instead. It is heavier than the ash and it is the only thing that points up._

I picked it up. It was heavier than I can write. And the moment it was on my shoulder the prince came for me in full, no longer whispering, screaming now the whole catalogue of the curse — _you are nothing, you gave nothing, you let the village burn, you are the reason it burned, lie back down in the ash where the ruined belong._ And I understood that this was the fight, that there had always been a fight, and that I had been losing it for years by refusing to pick up the only weapon that works against a thing made of lies.

You do not fight a lie with your fists. You cannot stab smoke. The only sword that bites a thing like the prince is the one that comes out of the mouth — the true word, spoken plainly into the face of the false one. So I opened my mouth and I fought back the way the messenger fought, the way the one with the sword in his mouth fights in the last book — with truth, said out loud, each true sentence a stroke. _I am not nothing. The worth I could not find was given to me, finished, before I was born. The village burned and I did not cause the army and I am still loved in the ruins. You are a liar and your whole kingdom is a lie and I will not lie back down._ And here is the thing I did not expect, the thing this whole ascent was built to teach me: every true word I spoke against the lie lifted me. Not as a feeling. As a fact, a physics. The truth had weight that fell upward. The cross on my shoulder, which should have pressed me into the ground, pulled toward the sky on its lifted arm, and the more truth I put into the air the higher the whole of me rose, the prince's voice thinning below me into the smoke where it belonged, until it was just one more sound coming up off a ruin I was no longer sitting in.

The messenger flew beside me the whole way. He did not carry me anymore. He did not have to. A man with the cross on his shoulder and the true word in his mouth rises on his own, which is the only kind of rising that lasts. Below us the village still smoked. I do not think it ever fully stops. But I was above it now, climbing, the gold-haired messenger at my side and the heavy wood pointing me up and the lie growing smaller, and for the first time since the night I cannot remember, the water on my face was not from the smoke.

I learned what the whole ascent was for, up there, and I will say it once and then shut my mouth, because it is the only thing worth carrying down: emotions do not move up to the skies on their own. You have to refuse the ash, and take the bright hand even when it shames you, and pick up the heavier thing, and fight the lie with the true word out loud — and then the grief itself, the whole sacked village of it, turns into the very weight that lifts you, the way a kite needs the wind that fights it to climb.
