# The Hollow Sky

The room is always the same. It is mine, which means it is empty. There is the bed and there is me on it and there is the ceiling, and at night the ceiling goes far away, up and up, until the room is not a room but a hollow sky with one body lying at the bottom of it. I cry most nights. I do it without sound, because sound would mean someone might come, and no one comes, so the sound has nowhere to go and I have learned not to make it. The tears run sideways into my ears and I lie there and let them, and there is no voice anywhere in the hollow saying _you are alright, you are loved, you can stop now._ I wait for the voice every night the way you wait for a bus you know is not coming, out of habit, out of a refusal to admit the route was cancelled.

There are people. I have friends. I can sit in a room full of them and feel exactly as alone as I feel here, more, because here at least I do not have to do the work. Around them I do the work the whole time — the watching, the measuring. If I let my face go for one second they will see it, and if they see it I become the thing I cannot survive being seen as: a man who is sad, which my whole body has decided means a man who is pathetic, a loser, a person you can put your foot on. So I run the math at the table. What does this one do with weakness, would that one repeat it, who here would take what I gave them and use it later, who would say a small cruel thing about my emotions and laugh and not even know he had cut something out of me. The answer is always the same. I trust none of them with it. I keep the face arranged. I go home to the hollow sky and let the face fall apart where it is safe, alone, which is to say where it does no good at all.

There was one person I could have told. That is the whole wound, really, in one line. There was one person to whom I could have said _I am drowning_ without first running the math, and she is gone, and she was gone on and off for a long time before she was gone for good, in and out of the room like warmth through a door that keeps swinging, and every time the warmth came in I shut it.

I want to tell you what the warmth was, because I never let myself have it long enough to deserve telling it.

She would pull me into her. Her arm would come around and my head would find the place below her shoulder where the arm makes a kind of bowl, and she would hold me there and call me _baby_ in a voice she used for nothing else, low, like the word was only ever built for me. And something in me that had been standing at attention since I was a boy — standing guard, holding up the sky, being the one who provides and decides and never needs — something in me would start to come down off its feet. The body wanted to be small. The body wanted, and I am ashamed to write it, to be a child held by someone bigger than the trouble, to be a son, to be told _you are good, you did nothing wrong, you can put it down now._ For about four seconds I would let it. I would feel the smallness coming, warm and enormous and unbearable, the way feeling comes back into a frozen hand — first nothing, then the terrible ache of it being alive again.

And then the thought would arrive. It always arrived. _Look at you. A man. Curled in a woman's arm like an infant. This is what you are under everything, this softness, this need, this nothing. She will feel how small you are and she will know. She is holding a child and calling it a man._ And the moment the thought arrived I pulled out. Not gently. I made my body hard again, made some joke, made some reason to sit up, made the warmth into something stupid so I would not have to admit it had almost saved me. I crushed it myself. Every time. She offered me the one thing I was dying for and I would rather have died than take it, because taking it meant standing in front of her with my hands open and admitting I had nothing in them, that the sky I had been pretending to hold up was hollow, that the provider could not even provide his own reason to keep breathing.

I have thought about that since, the choosing. How the warmth was right there, free, asking nothing, and how I turned my face from it the way a man turns his face from a fire he could walk into — except I had it backwards. I thought surrender was the burning. I thought if I melted I would be consumed, exposed, finished. So I stayed cold and intact in the hollow room and called that survival. It took me a long time to understand that the cold was the burning. That I had been choosing the fire the whole time, choosing to stay out here alone and rigid and freezing rather than walk into the soft thing that would have cost me nothing but the lie that I did not need it. The warmth was offered like a hand held out across a gap, again and again, on and off, in and out the door — and every time I looked at the hand and decided I would rather keep the gap.

Now the door does not swing anymore. There is no arm. There is no low voice that built a word only for me. There is the ceiling going up and up into the hollow sky, and there is the place below my own shoulder where no one's head will go, and there is the four seconds of warmth I let myself have, total, across all those months, four seconds at a time, which I add up sometimes in the dark like a miser counting the only coins he refused to spend.

I would give anything now to be called that word. I would lie down in that arm and be small and let the whole sky come down on me and not say one clever thing to make it stop. But the offering is what you cannot summon back. It comes to you while you are too proud to take it, and it leaves, and then you are exactly what you were so afraid she would find out you were — a small thing, alone, that needed to be held and could not bear to be.

The room is always the same. I lie at the bottom of it and the tears go sideways into my ears, and I listen, out of habit, for the voice that is not coming. And the worst part, because I am too tired tonight to keep my face arranged even for myself, is that it came. The voice came, in a woman's arm, low, just for me, more times than I deserved.

I am the one who would not answer it.
