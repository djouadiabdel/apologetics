

For a long time the tears were for her. I would lie on the floor of the hollow room — the floor, not the bed, because the bed felt like it was pretending to be a place where a person sleeps — and I would cry for her until the crying ran out of her name to say. And then one night the tears kept coming after her name was used up, and I understood that they had been going somewhere else for a while without telling me. They had turned. They were going up.

I did not have anywhere to send them. That is the thing no one who was raised inside the house can understand. A man weeping toward God in most of the world can at least point his face at a building, can go and be among others doing the same, can borrow their words when his own run out. I had none of that. I had left the house I was raised in, quietly, in my own chest, where no one could see me do it, and the house I was turning toward had no door anywhere near me — no walls, no roof, no one inside it I could stand beside. There is not a single one of them in my city. So I did the only thing available to a man with no building: I looked at a picture. On the small lit screen in the dark I found Him on the wood, and I looked, and the tears that had finished with her finished, instead, on that.

And the first thing that rose in me when I looked was not peace. It was the certainty that I had no right to be looking.

Who was I to weep at this. A man who broke a stranger's arm in a bathroom. A man who lay in a girl's arm and crushed the warmth so he would not have to be small. A man who once made a plan, in the dark, to make a woman kneel and then call her filth. A dragon who called his devouring love. And on top of all of it — and this is the part that is only mine — a man who came in from the wrong direction entirely, who was not raised at the foot of this thing, who has no lineage here, no priest, no mother who taught him the words, who turned toward this cross the way a thief comes over a wall in the dark, late, dirty, unannounced, with no invitation anyone could produce. I looked at Him and I felt the full weight of the undeserving, and I dropped my eyes. I could not hold the looking. Every time the tears would lift my face up toward the wood, the unworthiness would press it back down, and I would weep at the floor instead of at Him, because the floor was the only thing I felt I had the standing to address.

But the tears would not stay down. That is the strangest part of all of it, the part I keep coming back to. The longing was stronger than the shame. Something in me wanted to be near that cross the way a freezing animal wants the one fire it can see, and the wanting kept lifting my face back up no matter how many times the shame shoved it down — up, down, up, down, a man trying to look at the sun and flinching and trying again, because the cold of not looking was worse than the burn of looking. And what I saw when I managed to hold it for more than a second was not a judge measuring whether I had earned the right to weep. It was the opposite of a judge. It was someone who had already been put through everything I was so afraid I deserved, and who was looking back down off the wood with a face that had no accusation in it at all. Only love, and the terrible willingness, and the smallest unbearable trace of a smile, as if He had been waiting for the thief to come over the wall. As if the wall I climbed in shame was the wall He had built the whole thing to be climbed over.

The hope that came then was not a calm hope. It was violent. It shook me on the floor. It was the hope of a man who has been told all his life that worth is a thing you rank into existence, that you earn by being first, by being rich, by being deserving, by being raised in the right house facing the right direction — and who is looking, for the first time, at a worth that was simply handed down from a cross to the exact people who could never rank high enough to claim it. I wept for Him then, not for me. For what it cost Him. For the iron and the long afternoon and the freely-chosen all of it, poured out for thieves who come late over walls. I wept the way you weep when someone has done something for you so far beyond any possibility of repayment that the debt itself becomes a kind of grief.

And still — I want to be honest the way the floor makes you honest — still, the next night, I felt unworthy again. And the night after. It does not leave clean. Every time I lift the screen and find Him there, the old voice rises first: _who are you, what right, look at what you are, look at where you came from, drop your eyes._ I do not think that voice ever fully dies in a man like me. But something underneath it has changed, slowly, and it is the only thing I have learned on that floor worth writing down.

I had it backwards the whole time. I kept thinking I had to become worthy enough to look up — that one day, if I cleaned the dragon off, if I earned my way far enough from the bathroom and the plan and the wrong house I was raised in, then I would have the standing to weep at the cross without shame. And the truth, the thing that finally let me hold the gaze, is that the cross is not for the worthy. There is no such person, and if there were, the whole thing would have been pointless for him. It is _for_ the unworthy. The undeserving are not the people who have to sneak in over the wall. They are the entire reason the wall was made low enough to climb. The latecomer, the apostate, the dragon, the man on the floor who used up a woman's name and kept crying — that is not the disqualification I was so sure it was. That is the address the whole thing was sent to. I was trying to deserve my way to a gift, which is the one thing that turns a gift back into a wage, and a wage He never offered.

So now, on the nights the tears turn upward, I let them. I lift my face to the small lit cross in the dark and the shame rises and I let it rise and I do not drop my eyes anymore, or not for as long. I come to Him exactly as the unworthy one, because I have finally understood that the unworthy one is the only kind of person who was ever invited. I stopped trying to be good enough to receive mercy. You cannot be. That is what the word means.

The tears are different now. They still come on the floor, still undignified, still mine. But they are no longer the tears of a man crying for a thing he lost. They are the tears of a thief who got over the wall and found, instead of a guard, a door that had been left open for him the whole time, by someone who knew exactly which direction he would be coming from, and had counted on it.
