

The room was cold and I left the light off. The screen was enough. It was always enough — a small grey window, my own face in it when nothing was lit, the only mirror I had looked into for months.

My thumb went up. Up through the weeks, the way you climb a stairwell in the dark, by feel, knowing there is a step even when you cannot see it.

The words came back one at a time.

_You never act like a man._

My stomach went out from under me. I was a boy in a doorway again, not knowing where to put my hands.

_A man gives. A man's cup does not run dry._

So I had poured. I poured everything into her and said nothing about the running-dry, because the dryness was the shameful part, the part you swallow and smile over and hope nobody smells on your breath.

_A man leads. A man decides._

I picked the street, the table, the plan, and called it taking care of her. The truth is I could not stand for either of us to see me in the road not knowing the way. I held the wheel hard so my hands would stop shaking. They did not stop. They never stopped.

And under all of it I was keeping a book. She kept hers in the light — the dates she wanted, the flowers, the calendar she wanted full. Mine I kept in the dark, where it could not be checked. The sleepless hours went into it. The hand on the back of her neck the night she shook went into it. Every unbought, unbookable thing I did, I wrote down as proof — that I was the deeper one, the finer one, the one whose love did not need a receipt.

And then I grieved that she never read a book I never let her open.

That is the part I could not see at the time. I thought I was loving her. I was asking her to tell me I was real. There is a difference, and I could not find it from the inside. I made her into the one thing no person can be — the one who says you exist — and she could not say it, because nobody can, and I held that against her for a year.

The screen lit my face from underneath and gave it back to me. That was all it ever did. You look in to be held and it hands you your own face, the face you came here to get away from, and the water closes over.

_You have no heart. You cannot love._

I read it again and it read me back.

Worthless. Worthless. The same tide, all the way in.

I do not know how long I sat. Long enough that the room went from black to grey without my permission. The window on the wall came up pale, then paler — light I had not earned and could not send back. Somewhere a tap was dripping. My chest kept rising and falling on its own, doing the one thing I had stopped being able to thank it for.

Nobody was in the room. And the room was full.

I looked at the screen one more time. Nothing on it had changed. The same words. But something came off them in the grey light, floated up the way the skim floats up when the metal goes soft — the part that said _and therefore you are nothing._ It lifted off, and there was nothing under it. It had never been metal. It could not hold a face.

What was left was small, and I could carry it.

I was loved. I loved. We asked each other for the one thing neither of us had to give, and that is why it hurt the way a real thing hurts, and none of it makes me worthless. Both of us were blind. There was no one to blame, and nothing left to forgive that I had not already forgiven, sitting there in the cold.

I wanted to be welded to you. One thing, no seam. I was too afraid of my own uselessness to say it plainly — _love me back, I am asking_ — so I gave and gave and called the silence noble. You wanted the giving counted. So did I. We were two misers in the dark, each sure the other had spent it all on garbage.

I am not writing this so you will understand. I used to write everything so you would understand. That was the whole sickness — every word a hand held out for a coin. This one I am setting down. I am not waiting by the door.

The screen went dark on its own. My own face came back into it, the way it always did when nothing was lit.

But the light was up in the room now, the grey gone almost white, and it was on me too, and when I looked I could not tell anymore which face was coming back — mine, or the one the light was making out of me.

I put the phone down.

The cold I walked into after was a cold I chose. I opened the door to it myself. It was the first thing in a long time that asked nothing of me and gave itself anyway.
