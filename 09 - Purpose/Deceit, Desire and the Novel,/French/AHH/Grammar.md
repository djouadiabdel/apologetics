# **Topic 1: the subjunctive.**

This is the single highest-leverage grammar point for you right now, and here's why. You're at the stage where your ideas are more sophisticated than your grammar, which means you're constantly reaching for constructions that _require_ the subjunctive — expressing doubt, necessity, emotion, purpose, hypothesis — and then routing around them because the subjunctive isn't reflex yet. Every time you do that, your sentence gets flatter than your thought. A French reader can feel it immediately. It's the clearest single marker of "this person is not yet B2."

The good news is that the subjunctive is _much_ smaller than its reputation suggests. In real French, 90% of subjunctive usage comes from maybe fifteen trigger phrases that you already know and use constantly: _il faut que, je veux que, pour que, bien que, avant que, jusqu'à ce que, à moins que, il est possible que, je ne pense pas que, j'ai peur que, il est important que,_ and a handful more. You don't need to learn "the subjunctive" as a grammatical category. You need to learn those fifteen triggers and the reflex to flip the following verb.

And the conjugation itself is mostly easy. For regular verbs and most irregulars, the present subjunctive is built from the third-person plural present indicative (_ils finissent_ → _que je finisse_), which you already know. There are maybe eight verbs with irregular subjunctive stems — _être, avoir, aller, faire, pouvoir, savoir, vouloir, falloir_ — and those are the ones you'll use constantly anyway, so they become reflex fast.


# **2. _C'est_ vs _il est_.** 

You get this wrong constantly and it's actually a simple rule once you see it. _C'est_ is for identifying or pointing: _c'est un livre, c'est Jean, c'est intéressant_ (when _intéressant_ refers back to something already mentioned — "reading Girard? c'est intéressant"). _Il est_ is for describing a specific person or thing already in play, and for stating facts about time or general truths: _il est médecin, il est tard, il est important de lire_. The rule of thumb that covers 90% of cases: if what follows is a noun (with or without an article), use _c'est_. If what follows is an adjective describing a specific already-mentioned thing, use _il/elle est_. If what follows is an adjective making a general statement ("it is important to..."), use _il est_ followed by _de_ + infinitive, OR _c'est_ followed by _à_ + infinitive with slightly different meaning. Two days of attention and this stops being a problem forever.



 # **3. Prepositions after verbs.** 

This is the silent killer of B1 writing and it shows up all over yours. French verbs demand specific prepositions that English verbs don't, or demand different ones: _penser à_ someone but _penser de_ something (opinion), _jouer à_ a sport but _jouer de_ an instrument, _s'intéresser à_, _se souvenir de_, _avoir besoin de_, _réussir à_, _commencer à_, _finir de_, _essayer de_, _arriver à_. There's no rule — it's pure memorization, but it's finite. Get a list of the 50 most common verb+preposition combinations and drill them. _Bescherelle_ has one; so does any decent B1 grammar book. This one you just grind. Ten minutes a day for two weeks and most of the errors disappear.

# **4. Gender of nouns, and adjective agreement that follows from it.** 

You're getting gender wrong frequently and it cascades — wrong gender means wrong article, wrong adjective ending, wrong pronoun. The fix isn't memorizing every noun; it's memorizing the _patterns_. Endings in _-tion, -sion, -té, -ence, -ance, -ure, -ette_ are almost always feminine. Endings in _-ment, -age, -eau, -isme, -phone, -scope_ are almost always masculine. That covers thousands of nouns. For the irregulars, the trick is: every time you learn a new noun, learn it _with its article_ as a single unit — not _médiateur_ but _le médiateur_, not _désir_ but _le désir_. Never let a bare noun into your memory. This is a habit shift more than a study task.

# **5. Past tenses: passé composé vs imparfait.** 

This is the other big B1-to-B2 gatekeeper. In writing about Girard you don't use past tenses much, so you've been dodging this, but in the Lincoln Lawyer and Camus reading you're absorbing it passively. The rule: _passé composé_ is for completed actions that move the story forward ("j'ai lu le chapitre, puis j'ai écrit mon commentaire"). _Imparfait_ is for background, description, ongoing states, habitual actions, and for setting the scene ("il faisait nuit, je lisais Girard, je pensais à..."). The classic test: if you can translate it with "was doing" or "used to do" in English, it's imparfait. If it's "did" as a single completed event, it's passé composé. The tricky part is when both are possible and the choice changes the meaning — _je savais_ (I knew, ongoing) vs _j'ai su_ (I found out, single moment). Practice: write a paragraph every few days about something that happened to you, forcing yourself to think about which tense each verb needs and why.

# **6. The pronouns _en_ and _y_.** 

These are the two little words that separate "learner French" from "French French." _En_ replaces _de + thing_ ("j'ai besoin de ce livre" → "j'en ai besoin"). _Y_ replaces _à + thing_ or a location ("je pense à mon projet" → "j'y pense"; "je vais à Paris" → "j'y vais"). You're not using either of them in your writing, which makes your sentences longer and clunkier than they need to be. Once you internalize them, your prose compresses by about 20% and starts sounding French. A week of deliberate practice: every time you write a sentence with _de_ + noun or _à_ + noun that could be pronominalized, do it. Over-use them on purpose until the reflex locks in.

# **7. Placement of pronouns in general.** 

Related to the above. In French, object pronouns come _before_ the verb, not after: _je le vois, je lui parle, je m'en souviens_. When there are multiple pronouns, they go in a fixed order (_me/te/se/nous/vous_ before _le/la/les_ before _lui/leur_ before _y_ before _en_). You don't need to memorize the order as a chart — you need to absorb it by hearing and using the common combinations: _je te le donne, je le lui ai dit, il y en a_. These chunks become reflex through exposure, which you're already getting from the Lincoln Lawyer audiobook. Just start _noticing_ them when you hear them.

# **8. Relative pronouns: _qui, que, dont, où, ce qui, ce que, ce dont_.** 

This is what lets you build the long, connected sentences that Girard writes and that you want to write. The rules: _qui_ is the subject of the relative clause ("le livre qui m'a changé"), _que_ is the object ("le livre que j'ai lu"), _dont_ replaces _de + thing_ in a relative clause ("le livre dont je parle," "l'auteur dont j'ai oublié le nom"), _où_ is for places and times ("la ville où je vis," "le jour où j'ai compris"). _Ce qui / ce que / ce dont_ are for when there's no specific antecedent ("ce qui m'intéresse, c'est...", "ce que je veux dire," "ce dont j'ai besoin"). Mastering _dont_ alone will upgrade your writing visibly because it's the French move for compressing two sentences into one, and you currently avoid it. Deliberate practice: rewrite your old Girard passages using _dont_ wherever you can.

# **9. Agreement of past participles with _avoir_.** 

The infamous rule. When _avoir_ is the auxiliary, the past participle agrees with a preceding direct object: _les livres que j'ai lus_ (not _lu_, because _les livres_ comes before and is the direct object). If the direct object comes after, no agreement: _j'ai lu les livres_. This is a small rule with outsized prestige — getting it right marks you as "someone who actually knows French grammar." Getting it wrong is forgivable but getting it right is a tell. Once you understand the rule, it takes about a month of writing for it to become reflex.

# **10. Register and the _nous_ vs _on_ question.** 

In spoken and informal written French, _on_ replaces _nous_ almost universally — "on va au cinéma" not "nous allons au cinéma." In formal writing, _nous_ comes back. Girard writes in formal register so he uses _nous_. Your commentaries are hybrid — personal reflection but on academic material — and you're inconsistent about it. Pick a register per piece and stick to it. For philosophical commentary, _nous_ is the right choice; it matches the source material and it's what gives academic French its weight. For diary-style writing, _on_ or _je_ is fine.

# **11. Connectors and discourse markers.** 

Not grammar exactly, but the mortar between bricks. Your writing relies heavily on _et, mais, parce que, alors_. These are the training-wheel connectors. The upgrade set: _or_ (now, yet — used to introduce a turn in argument), _donc_ (therefore), _ainsi_ (thus), _par conséquent_ (consequently), _en effet_ (indeed, in fact), _cependant_ (however), _néanmoins_ (nevertheless), _toutefois_ (however, yet), _d'ailleurs_ (besides, moreover), _en revanche_ (on the other hand), _au fur et à mesure_ (gradually, as), _dans la mesure où_ (insofar as), _étant donné que_ (given that), _quoique_ (although — takes subjunctive). Learn five of these a week and force them into your writing. This is maybe the single fastest way to make your prose _sound_ more advanced, even before the underlying grammar catches up.

# **12. The _ne_ of negation and the literary _ne_.** 

Two things here. First, in spoken French the _ne_ of _ne...pas_ is often dropped ("je sais pas"), but in writing you must keep it. Second, there's a "pleonastic" or "expletive" _ne_ that appears in certain constructions without being a real negation: _avant qu'il ne parte, je crains qu'il ne soit trop tard, à moins qu'il ne vienne_. This one is literary and you'll see it in Girard but don't need to actively produce it yet. Just recognize it when you see it so it doesn't confuse you.

# **13. _Depuis, pendant, pour, il y a_.** 

Time expressions that English collapses into "for" and "ago" but French distinguishes. _Depuis_ = since or for (ongoing: "je lis Girard depuis trois mois" — and note the _present tense_ for ongoing action, not past). _Pendant_ = during, for a completed duration ("j'ai lu pendant deux heures"). _Pour_ = for, about future planned duration ("je pars pour une semaine"). _Il y a_ = ago ("j'ai commencé il y a trois mois"). Small rule, frequent usage, clean fix.

# **14. Conditional and hypothetical constructions.** 

The "if X then Y" patterns in French are strict and you need to know three of them: _si + présent, futur_ ("si je lis, je comprendrai"); _si + imparfait, conditionnel_ ("si je lisais, je comprendrais"); _si + plus-que-parfait, conditionnel passé_ ("si j'avais lu, j'aurais compris"). Never _si_ with a future or conditional in the _si_ clause — that's the classic mistake. Once you lock these three templates, you can express any hypothesis in French.

# **15. Word order in questions, and inversion in formal writing.** 

In speech you can ask a question by intonation ("tu viens?") or with _est-ce que_ ("est-ce que tu viens?"). In writing, especially formal writing, inversion is expected: "viens-tu?" "Girard a-t-il raison?" You're not writing questions much so this is lower priority, but when you do write one in a commentary, inversion is the register-appropriate form.
