# YouTube System — Packaging, Titles, Intros

> The one rule under everything: **package the video BEFORE you make it.** If you can't write a curious title and rough a thumbnail for the idea, you don't make the idea. Title + thumbnail decide ~80% of views. Spend half your effort here, not on the body.

The order every week: **package first → record → cut fillers → CTA to email.** Same loop forever.

---

## 1. TITLES

The whole job of a title: create a **curiosity gap** the thumbnail can't close on its own. Not information. Curiosity.

**The rules (from the studies):**
- **Short.** 5 to 7 words. Titles under 30 characters got 60% more views. Over ~60 chars YouTube cuts it off.
- **No numbers.** Videos with numbers got 11% *fewer* views. Even years and dollar amounts. Drop them.
- **Negative beats positive.** Only 11% of titles are negative, and they got 22% more views. Fear, "you're doing it wrong," "stop," "the problem with."
- **Simple words.** Aim for a Flesch reading-ease score of 100+ (talk like you're talking to a 9-year-old). 100+ readability = 20% more views. Google "Flesch reading ease", paste your title, check.
- **Emotion.** The winners: joy, controversy, anger. Pick one and let the title carry it.

**The three curiosity moves (steal these every time):**
- **Mindread.** State the belief the viewer already holds, then flip it. "You think you chose what you want. You didn't."
- **Mechanism.** Invent a name for a real thing so it feels new. Girard's idea becomes *the borrowed-want trap*. A made-up name raises the question "what is that?"
- **Curiosity gap.** Never put the answer in the title. Name the question, withhold the payoff.

**Title starters for my lane (person / nature / desire):**
- You are not your personality.
- You didn't choose what you want.
- The wanting that isn't yours.
- Why getting it never works.
- Stop trusting your desire.
- The self underneath the self.

## 2. THUMBNAILS (no design skill required)

Good news from the study: **on small channels, no-face and text-forward thumbnails beat face thumbnails.** So I don't need to be photogenic or skilled. I need a formula.

**The formula:**
- **Text: 4 to 9 characters.** Not a sentence. One or two words. Text should cover only **3 to 6%** of the frame.
- **Colors: cyan, green, or orange** as the pop color. Cyan is #1 for engagement.
- **Bright.** Brighter thumbnails win (short of blown-out). Crank exposure.
- **Three-element rule** — but for my minimal style: one strong element + a couple of words is enough. Don't clutter.
- **Curiosity, not summary.** The thumbnail and title together make a question. Don't answer it in either.

**My signature (this IS the design):**
Clean frame, a few words in my **Obsidian handwriting / note aesthetic**, in cyan or orange, bright. The writing-as-image is the whole look. Nobody else does it. That's the moat, and it happens to require zero Photoshop.

**How to make one with no skill:**
1. Open Canva (or Figma). Blank 1280x720.
2. **Steal like an artist:** find 2-3 thumbnails on videos near my topic that got big views. Copy the *structure*, then change at least two things (better color + one added element). Copy-paste never works; models do.
3. Make **three concepts** before posting. Pick the one that makes *me* curious.
4. Later: once I have a template I like, $5/thumbnail on Fiverr removes even this.

## 3. THE FIRST 30 SECONDS

**Why it matters:** the single biggest drop-off on any video is the first 30 seconds. Lower that drop and the whole retention graph starts higher. This is the only part I script tightly. Everything after is freestyle thinking.

**The four beats (what they must DO, not a script to parrot):**
1. **Confirm the click.** In the first line, prove this is the video they clicked and it's not clickbait. Restate the promise.
2. **Open a loop.** Raise a question I don't answer yet. The unclosed question is what holds them.
3. **Set the pace.** Tell them it's slow and why, so "slow" reads as intentional, not boring. "No hacks. Think with me."
4. **Establish singularity.** One line on why *me*, why this take is different. (See note below — for now this is obsession, not a title.)

**IMPORTANT — the credential fix.** I am not a psychiatrist yet. So beat 4 does NOT lean on a title. My authority right now is **obsession and synthesis**: a medical student who has spent years chasing one question across theology, philosophy, and psychiatry, and writes it down. Lead with the *depth of the idea and the honesty*, not a badge. When I qualify, the title gets added on top. Not before.

**My voice = confession, not salesman.** The YouTube-bro "in the next 10 minutes I'm going to show you" opening is fake for me and I can feel it. My hook is the confessional one: open in the middle of a real, unsettling thought. That IS an open loop, and it's true to how I actually talk.

---

## 4. IDEATION (light, until I find rhythm)

For now: **just post.** Consistency matters more than idea-optimization at the start. But when I want a proven idea:

- **Supply and demand.** Low supply + high demand = near-guaranteed views. Find a question lots of people ask that few good videos answer *through my lens*.
- **Steal from adjacent niches.** Take an outlier video from a niche with the *same desire* as mine (psychology, self-improvement, stoicism, "why you're unhappy") and bring it to my lane where it's never been done. Proven demand, no competition.
- **Cheat vs evergreen, 50/50.** Cheat videos (trend/news/big-name hacking) spike then flatline. Evergreen depth compounds. My person/nature/desire essays are the evergreen half — the half that builds the real audience. Depth is not indulgence, it's the compounding engine.
- **New-viewer vs returning-viewer, 50/50.** Some videos to go viral (reach), some to nurture the people I have (trust). Don't post only bangers or I create viewer fatigue.

---

## 5. CADENCE + WORKFLOW

- **Once a week, minimum. For a year.** 99% of channels die because they *stop posting*, not because they're bad. Sign a one-year weekly contract with myself. More than weekly is fine, never less.
- **Editing = cut the uhs/ums/dead air. That's it.** Use **Descript**: it transcribes the video and I edit it by deleting words from the text. Delete the "um" from the transcript, the cut happens. One click removes all filler words. I edit video the way I edit a note. No timeline, no skill.
- **The pipeline:** bullet script in Obsidian → record face + notes overlay → Descript (delete fillers + bad takes) → export → post → CTA to the free download → email list.
- **Every video ends with a CTA** to a free download (lead magnet). The email list is the actual business; the videos just feed it.

---

## The 80/20 to never forget
1. Package before you make it.
2. Short, no-number, curiosity title.
3. Text-forward thumbnail: 4-9 chars, cyan/orange, bright.
4. First 30s: confirm, open a loop, set pace, obsession-not-title.
5. Post weekly for a year. Cut only fillers. CTA every time.


----

Overall, this is **a strong operating system with a few dangerous absolutes**. It gives you constraints, reduces production friction, and fits a thoughtful psychology/philosophy channel better than the usual high-energy creator advice.

I would rate it **8/10 as a working playbook**, but closer to **6/10 if every statistical claim is treated as a law**.

## What is especially good

### Packaging before production

This is the strongest principle in the document. Developing the title and thumbnail first forces you to answer:

- Who is this for?
    
- What tension does it create?
    
- Why would someone care now?
    
- Is there actually a video-sized idea here?
    

It also prevents a common failure: making an intricate essay and then discovering that it has no compelling public-facing premise.

The important distinction is that packaging should shape the idea, **not distort it**.

### Your thumbnail identity

The Obsidian-handwriting concept is promising because it is:

- Repeatable
    
- Recognizable
    
- Cheap to produce
    
- Consistent with the subject matter
    
- Authentic to your existing way of thinking
    

But I would call it a **brand asset**, not yet a moat. A moat is harder to copy. Your eventual moat will be the combination of your voice, intellectual synthesis, visual language, archive of ideas, and audience trust.

### Confession rather than performance

This is probably the most strategically important insight:

> “My voice = confession, not salesman.”

That can genuinely differentiate you. Your opening should feel like the viewer has entered a thought already in progress, rather than being subjected to a rehearsed marketing pitch.

For example:

> “I’ve started to suspect that some of the things I want most were never really mine.”

That confirms the subject, opens a loop, and establishes tone without sounding like a formula.

### Obsession instead of premature authority

This is ethically and creatively sound. “I have spent years following this question” is credible. Pretending to possess clinical authority you do not yet have would weaken trust.

You can be authoritative about:

- What a philosopher argued
    
- How two traditions differ
    
- What you have observed in yourself
    
- The connections you have researched
    

You should remain careful about diagnoses, treatment claims, or speaking on behalf of patients.

## What I would change

### Replace “curiosity, not information” with “clarity plus curiosity”

Curiosity without clarity often becomes vague:

- “The Self Underneath the Self”
    
- “The Wanting That Isn’t Yours”
    

These are elegant, but a new viewer may not know what concrete experience the video addresses.

The strongest titles usually do two jobs:

1. Identify a recognizable problem or desire.
    
2. Leave an unresolved tension.
    

For instance:

- **Why You Want What Others Want**
    
- **Your Desires May Not Be Yours**
    
- **Why Success Changes Nothing**
    
- **You Mistook Approval for Desire**
    

These remain curious, but communicate more clearly.

A useful rule would be:

> The viewer should understand the territory, but not yet know the conclusion.

### Do not ban numbers

“No numbers” is too rigid. Numbers can feel cheap when used artificially:

- “7 INSANE Ways to Become Yourself”
    

But they can also supply necessary specificity:

- “I Tracked Every Desire for 30 Days”
    
- “What 10 Years of Wanting Taught Me”
    
- “The $100,000 Life I Didn’t Want”
    

The relevant question is not whether a title contains a number. It is whether the number makes the premise **more concrete or more generic**.

### Do not force negative framing

Negative titles can earn attention, but repeatedly telling viewers that they are broken, fooled, weak, or doing life incorrectly can create three problems:

- The channel begins to feel manipulative.
    
- The audience becomes attracted mainly to anxiety.
    
- Your thoughtful voice becomes indistinguishable from adversarial self-help.
    

Your subject matter naturally contains darkness. You do not need to manufacture more of it.

Use negative framing when the idea genuinely involves error or danger. Alternate it with wonder, recognition, relief, or revelation:

- **You Didn’t Choose What You Want**
    
- **The Strange Relief of Wanting Less**
    
- **What Remains When Desire Goes Quiet**
    
- **Maybe You Don’t Need a New Self**
    

### Treat title statistics as hypotheses, not commandments

Rules such as “five to seven words,” “under thirty characters,” “Flesch 100+,” and specific percentage advantages are useful starting points, but they can become cargo cults.

Titles are not consumed in a laboratory. Their success depends on:

- Topic
    
- Audience familiarity
    
- Thumbnail
    
- Traffic source
    
- Channel reputation
    
- Competitive context
    
- The emotional state of the viewer
    

A more robust rule:

> Prefer the shortest title that preserves the premise, tension, and natural language.

Do not reduce a strong title to six awkward words merely to satisfy a spreadsheet.

### Do not explain that the video will be slow

This part may create the exact concern you are trying to prevent.

Saying “this will be slow” can sound like a warning or apology. Instead, **demonstrate intentional pace immediately**. Use a strong sentence, deliberate editing, a visual detail, and no wasted language.

“No hacks. Think with me.” might work occasionally, but repeated in every video it will become a slogan rather than a genuine invitation.

### Packaging gets the first click; substance gets the next one

“Title + thumbnail decide 80% of views” overstates the case. Packaging determines whether someone begins. The video determines whether they:

- Keep watching
    
- Feel satisfied
    
- Subscribe
    
- Return next week
    
- Recommend it
    
- Trust your future titles
    

For this kind of channel, the goal is not merely high click-through rate. It is a reliable relationship between **promise and payoff**.

A useful model:

> Packaging earns attention.  
> The opening earns time.  
> The argument earns trust.  
> The ending earns return.

### Editing should remove more than filler words

Removing “ums” is operationally easy, but filler words are not always the main problem. The larger retention killers in intellectual videos are often:

- Repeating the same insight three ways
    
- Taking too long to reach the concrete example
    
- Abstract language without imagery
    
- Explaining the structure instead of advancing it
    
- Including a tangent merely because it is interesting
    
- Ending after the central idea has already landed
    

Edit for **thought density**, not just verbal cleanliness.

Also, removing every hesitation can make a reflective speaker sound unnaturally polished. Some pauses and imperfect phrases communicate sincerity.

### Weekly should be a floor only if it is sustainable

A public commitment can be helpful. But “never less than weekly” can turn into a machine for producing underdeveloped ideas.

For essays involving philosophy, theology, psychiatry, and personal reflection, one excellent video every ten or fourteen days may outperform rushed weekly publishing.

The real commitment should be:

> Maintain a cadence that survives difficult months without degrading the work.

Consistency matters because it permits learning. Frequency itself is not the final product.

### Avoid making every ending feel transactional

A CTA in every video is fine in principle, but the viewer should not feel that every philosophical question was merely a funnel into a lead magnet.

The download must be the natural continuation of the video:

- A desire-audit worksheet
    
- An annotated reading list
    
- A reflective set of questions
    
- A diagram of the argument
    
- A short companion essay
    

Some videos may be stronger with only a subtle invitation. Trust is also a business asset.

## The biggest strategic risk

The system is trying to protect you from being too intellectual, too slow, too perfectionistic, and too obscure. That is useful.

But it could overcorrect and make you overly conscious of performance:

- Manufactured curiosity gaps
    
- Mandatory negativity
    
- Strict title lengths
    
- Repeated hook formulas
    
- Constant funnel thinking
    
- Content selected primarily for predicted views
    

Your strongest possible channel is not “YouTube optimization applied to philosophy.” It is **a distinct intellectual voice that understands YouTube well enough to be discovered**.

That difference matters.

## A better governing principle

I would replace the central rule with:

> **Find the sharpest honest promise before making the video. Then make the video deliver more than the promise.**

And compress the system to:

1. Define one human tension.
    
2. Write a clear-curious title.
    
3. Design a thumbnail that adds, rather than repeats.
    
4. Open inside the tension immediately.
    
5. Move between abstraction and concrete experience.
    
6. Remove repetition, not humanity.
    
7. End with a thought worth carrying—or a genuinely relevant next step.
    
8. Publish consistently enough to keep learning.
    

The document is already much better than having no system. Just keep it as a **set of tested defaults**, not a constitution. Your subject deserves enough structure to reach people, but enough freedom to remain alive.
