# Chapter 23

---

A voice cracked across the gym from the side. Coach Kamogawa, his tone carrying the weight of decades of silence broken only when silence was no longer useful:

"Three minutes, two rounds. Don't hold back on account of who your opponent is."

The room changed. The way a room changes when someone calls for prayer — the shuffling stops, the noise compresses, the air thickens with attention. People moved to the edges. Bags stopped swinging. Conversations died mid-sentence as if someone had reached into each one and pinched the thread.

Imai was already in the ring. He stood still. No movement at all — no bouncing, no rolling of shoulders, no last-second loosening. Just a body that had been ready before it was asked to be ready, his eyes tracking Ippo the way a camera tracks a subject it has already framed.

Coach lifted the ropes. Ippo ducked under them, and Itagaki watched the top of Ippo's head pass below the middle rope, and Coach was holding the rope with one hand and looking at the ceiling as if checking whether the ceiling had an opinion about what was about to happen.

Ippo settled into his stance. He began throwing light punches — the usual shadowboxing form, the peekaboo rhythm, his hands moving in front of his face like two birds circling the same branch. His eyes stayed on Imai. The spar was seconds away and Ippo's body was already in the ring the way a swimmer is already in the water before the gun goes off — everything aligned, everything pointed at one place.

Itagaki was still on the bench.

He watched Ippo for a moment, then let his eyes drift to the crowd. The gym's edges were lined with bodies now — everyone had stopped training to watch. Kimura was near the window, his back leaning against the glass, arms crossed, and next to him someone was standing on the other side of the window from outside, black hair, watching through the pane. Takamura was talking to the guy, and Kimura had tilted his head toward them, dropping one ear into their conversation while pretending to watch the ring.

Itagaki swept the crowd. Quick, unconscious, the way you scan a room when you're not sure what you're looking for.

Fujii was there. Press row, usual spot, his recorder on the bench beside him like a second set of ears. Next to Fujii, someone Itagaki didn't recognize — younger, not looking at the ring, writing something in a notebook. The pen moved. Stopped. The young man looked up at Ippo.

And Itagaki saw Ippo's face change.

It happened in real time. Ippo had been shadowboxing — focused, ready, his weight centered and his hands cycling through the peekaboo — and then his eyes caught something in the crowd, something at a specific angle, and his whole face opened.

Not opened like surprise. Opened like a wound.

Itagaki's first thought was sharp and loud: _Wait — what is Ippo looking at with that face? IPPO. You are supposed to focus._

He followed Ippo's gaze. Tracked the angle. Moved his eyes from Ippo to the crowd, past Fujii, past the notebook man, to where Takamura was standing with Kimura and Aoki, and next to them — the black-haired guy who'd been watching from outside the window.

Miyata.

Itagaki looked at Miyata. Saw a serious face, a slight frown, the expression of a man watching something he had predicted and was now confirming. No surprise. No awe. No emotion that moved. Just the still surface of someone who had come to measure.

He looked back at Ippo.

And Ippo had a face Itagaki had never seen outside the ring. A face Itagaki had only seen when Ippo was on the verge of defeat — when the body was failing and the legs were gone and the only thing left was the thing underneath the body that refused to sit down. That face. Except Ippo wasn't losing. He was standing in the ring before a spar had even started and he wore the expression of a man being beaten by something invisible.

_I thought he already had enough determination when he entered the ring. But this — I don't understand this._

Ippo's shoulders dropped. His hips lowered. His center of gravity sank toward the canvas as if the floor had called his name and he'd answered by getting closer to it. It was the Peek-a-Boo pushed to its lowest point — the crouch that says _go ahead, hit me, I dare you_ — except it wasn't aimed at Imai. It was aimed at something else, something in the crowd, something with black hair and a frown that didn't move.

Itagaki's chest boiled.

He had watched Ippo become someone he didn't recognize, and the cause was not the fight. The cause was not Imai. The cause was not Itagaki.

He looked at Miyata one more time. Miyata's face hadn't changed. The frown, the seriousness, the stillness — the same mask he'd walked in with.

He looked at Ippo again.

That's a man who saw a ghost.

---

The bench was still warm.

He was sitting on it again — or maybe he'd never stood up, maybe his body had stayed in place while the rest of him had traveled somewhere he wasn't invited. Either way, the wood under his thighs was the same wood, the same grain catching on the same fabric, the same warmth his own weight had made. But the warmth felt different now.

At the start of the chapter in his life that began when they told him to sit, the bench was humiliation. A place he was put because he wasn't needed.

Now it was something else.

He wasn't Ippo's rival. He understood that with the kind of clarity that doesn't come from thinking but from watching a face transform in front of you and knowing you were not the cause. He was not even in the equation. Miyata was the equation — had always been the equation, maybe since before Itagaki had even joined this gym, since before he'd started collecting Ippo's glances and corrections and moments of attention like coins in a jar he thought was filling up toward something.

Imai got to step into the ring because the door was open and he walked through it. Itagaki got to sit on the bench because Ippo was kind. Because Ippo noticed when someone disrespected his kouhai. Because Ippo cared the way Ippo cared about everyone — openly, freely, without keeping a ledger.

And that was the thing. Ippo didn't guard his attention like it was sacred. He gave it to everyone. Every boxer who walked through the door got the same warmth, the same interest, the same willingness to spar and study and learn. Itagaki had been treating that attention like it meant something — something specific, something earned, something that accumulated — and Imai had walked in off the street and gotten it for free.

Not because Imai was special.

Because Ippo was.

The bell rang. The spar began. The sound of it cut through the room like a door slamming open, and Itagaki's body flinched — not at the bell, but at Ippo's first step forward, because Ippo moved with a weight Itagaki had never seen in him before, a heaviness in the feet that wasn't slowness but depth, as if every step was pressing the canvas into the earth.

Imai came forward. Clean, efficient, the same footwork Itagaki had watched during the warm-up. And Ippo — Ippo, who should have been in the ring with only one opponent — was fighting something behind his own eyes while Imai's first jab traveled toward his face.

Itagaki gripped the bench.

The wood held him. It was all it knew how to do.








Then Chapter 23 hits different. When Miyata physically walks in during the spar, it's not new information — it's the information from Chapter 22 taking a body. The thing that was already splitting Ippo's focus just materialized at the door. And his body — already halfway toward Miyata's world — finishes the turn. The guard opens. Imai finds the gap.
