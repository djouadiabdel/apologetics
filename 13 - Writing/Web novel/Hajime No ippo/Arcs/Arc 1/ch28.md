# Chapter 28

His coach's voice reached him from across the ring. The corner. Sit down. Something about water.

Imai turned away from Ippo and the turning felt wrong — not painful, not dramatic, just incorrect, the way a sentence feels when the period comes in the middle instead of the end. He had been looking at Ippo and the looking was supposed to feel like something and it didn't and now his back was to the canvas and his feet were carrying him to his corner and the ropes passed on either side of him like walls of a hallway he hadn't asked to walk through.

He sat down on the stool. The cold of it reached him through his shorts — a small sharp fact, the first clear sensation since the spar ended. The rest of the ring was warm. The lights, the canvas, the air above the ropes where the heat of two bodies fighting had gathered and stayed. But the stool was metal and the metal was cold and Imai's weight pressed into it and the cold pressed back and for a moment that was all there was — a man sitting on a cold surface in a warm room, his hands still gloved, his breathing even, his coach smiling in front of him.

His coach was smiling.

His coach said something. His coach's hands were moving, unfastening the gloves, pulling the laces loose with the quick efficient motions of a man who had done this a thousand times after victories that meant something. The gloves came off and Imai's hands appeared — wrapped, white, the tape circling his knuckles in the familiar pattern, the same pattern from every fight, every spar, every morning in the gym when his hands became instruments and the wraps became the preparation and the whole ritual was aimed at the moment when the wraps would come off and the hands would carry the evidence of what they'd done.

Imai started unwrapping.

The left hand first. The tape circled his wrist three times — he could feel each layer separate from the one beneath it, the adhesive pulling slightly, the skin underneath pale and compressed where the wrap had held it flat. He pulled the tape across his palm, following the line it had taken on the way in but in reverse now, the loop-pull-loop that his hands knew better than any combination his coach had ever taught him. The tape crossed his knuckles. He pulled it free. The knuckles appeared.

During the Inter-High — the second one, the final against Saeki — his knuckles had come out swollen. The left hand had been worse than the right. The skin over the second knuckle was split, not bleeding but parted, the edges of the cut white with the pressure the wrap had put on it, and when he flexed his fingers the cut opened and closed like a mouth trying to speak. He had stared at it. He had stared at it the way you stare at a thing your body did that your mind didn't plan, and the feeling that rose in his chest was not pain but proof — the proof that the fight had passed through his body on its way to the result, that the win was not just a number on a scorecard but a physical event that had marked him, changed him, left him different from the man who had wrapped his hands two hours earlier.

He looked at his left hand.

The knuckles were flat. The skin was smooth. There was a faint redness where the wrap had pressed — circulation, not impact. No swelling. No cut. No mouth trying to speak. His hand looked like it had spent the afternoon resting on a desk.

He started on the right hand. The tape came off in the same sequence. Loop. Pull. Loop. The same mechanical unwinding that always followed the same mechanical winding and the difference was supposed to be in the hands underneath — the before and the after, the clean and the marked, the prepared and the spent. The right hand emerged. He turned it over. Looked at the palm, at the fingertips that still carried the crescents where his fist had clenched inside the glove.

The crescents were the only marks on him.

---

Imai held his hands in his lap. The wraps were balled up beside him, loose and damp, collapsed into the shapeless mass that tape becomes when it is no longer doing its job. He looked at the ball of tape the way he sometimes looked at a plate after a meal that hadn't filled him — briefly, without interest, the glance of a man confirming that the thing was finished without being satisfied that it was done.

His eyes moved to the ring.

The canvas. The spot. He could see it from his corner — not the whole ring, just the near side, the area where Ippo had fallen, where the body had met the surface with the sound he hadn't heard because his blood was too loud in his ears at the time. There was a mark on the canvas. Not blood — a smudge, a slight discoloration where sweat and skin and the friction of a body arriving against its will had left a trace. A faint depression, maybe, though from this distance it might have been a shadow.

He looked at it. Looked away. Felt his eyes pull back to it.

In the Inter-High — every Inter-High, all three — the canvas after a knockdown had meant something. It was the period at the end of a sentence he'd been writing with his fists since the first bell. When Hoshi went down in the second fight, the canvas had a mark where his mouthguard had bounced, and Imai had looked at that mark from his corner and felt the whole fight compressed into that single point — the preparation, the distance, the exchanges, the moment when Hoshi's legs went and the rest of Hoshi followed. The mark was a signature. His signature. Proof that a fight had happened between two people who were both trying to win and one of them had been him.

This mark said nothing. It was just a place where a man had fallen because the man was fighting something that wasn't in the ring. The canvas didn't know Imai's name. The canvas didn't know anyone had won.

He looked away.

---

His coach was talking. Something about the performance. Something about timing, about the left hook, about the distance management in the third exchange. The words had shape and grammar and they were aimed at Imai and they were correct — technically, structurally, the fight had gone the way fights go when one fighter is present and the other is not, and Imai's coach had the right to be pleased because the things he had coached had worked.

Imai nodded. The nod was real — it came from the part of him that respected his coach, that respected the work, that understood the weeks of preparation that had led to this gym and this ring and this spar. The nod was real and the feeling behind it was not.

He stood. Gathered his things — the wraps, the gloves, the water bottle his coach had placed beside the stool. The motions were automatic. Pack the bag the way you always pack the bag. Zip the compartment. Lift the strap. He ducked under the ropes.

The ring shifted behind him. He could feel it — the vibration of other people climbing in, or moving around the apron, or doing the things that needed to be done for the man on the canvas. He didn't look. He took one step toward the edge of the ring and then his body stopped and his head turned, just slightly, and he looked back.

Kamogawa's back.

The old man was moving now — finally moving, finally stepping toward the ring with the slow heavy certainty of a man whose body was older than his worry. He was stepping between Imai and Ippo. His back was wide and his shoulders were set and his body filled the space between the ropes and the canvas and Imai's view of the fallen champion was blocked. Completely. Kamogawa's back was a wall and on the other side of the wall was Ippo and on this side of the wall was Imai and the wall was not a wall built against him — it was a wall built around someone else, and the difference was worse.

Imai stepped through the ropes. His feet found the gym floor — concrete through the thin mat, harder than the canvas, colder, the surface of a room he did not belong to. He walked toward the door. His bag was over his shoulder and his hands were at his sides — bare, unmarked, the wraps balled up in the bag's side pocket where they would stay until he got home and pulled them out and washed them and hung them over the back of the chair in his room where they always dried.

The air outside the ring was neither hot nor cold. It was nothing. It touched his face the way air touches a wall — without opinion, without temperature, without the sting of a gym that had just witnessed something. He walked and the floor was under his feet and the door was ahead of him and behind him the gym was doing things that had nothing to do with him and his hands were clean and his jaw was clean and his ribs were clean and his heart was beating at a rate that had no right to be this calm after a fight with the man whose name he'd said out loud in his room.

He touched his chest with his right hand. The fingers pressed against his sternum — the spot where the drumming always lived, where the rhythm of a fight settled after the fight was over and became the thing his body carried home and replayed in his sleep and woke him up at four in the morning with his fists clenched and his teeth together and the feeling that the fight was still happening somewhere inside him. He pressed. He waited.

Clean.

He dropped his hand. Walked through the door. The gym closed behind him like a sentence he hadn't been part of.
