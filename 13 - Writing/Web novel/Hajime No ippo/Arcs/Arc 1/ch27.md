# Chapter 27

Imai's fist was still in the air.

Not extended — retracted, pulled back to his chin the way it always returned after a clean shot, the elbow tucked, the shoulder covering the jaw, every joint in the chain locked into position like a door bolted from the inside. His left foot was forward. His right foot was back. His weight was centered between them, distributed the way his coach had drilled into him since he was fifteen — sixty-forty, front-back, never flat, never caught leaning. His knees were bent. His hips were square. His guard was up.

He was defending against nothing.

Ippo was on the canvas. Imai could see him from behind his gloves — the shape of a man on the floor, the limbs arranged in the wrong order, the head turned at an angle that had nothing to do with looking at anything. The canvas was holding him the way a table holds a glass someone set down too hard. Still. Final. The kind of stillness that doesn't ask permission.

Imai's guard stayed up.

His body hadn't received the signal. The part of him that fought — the part that had walked into this gym with his bag over his shoulder and his wraps already half-wound and his jaw set against the weight of what he'd come here to do — that part was still waiting. Still watching the canvas for movement. Still expecting Ippo's hands to find the floor, the knees to bend, the legs to push, the body to rise the way it had risen in every fight Imai had watched on the screen at home, the way it always rose, because that was the thing about Makunouchi Ippo: he got up. That was the whole point. That was the thing Imai had come here to see from the inside — not the punching, not the technique, but the getting up. The moment after the knockdown when the champion's body refused the floor and chose the fight and the crowd noise hit a pitch that made the speakers in Imai's television rattle in their frames.

Five seconds passed. Maybe more. The gym was making noise around him but the noise was the wrong shape — it didn't sound like the end of a fight, it sounded like the beginning of something else, something that had nothing to do with him. Voices. Movement. The sound of bodies rearranging around a problem.

Ippo did not get up.

Imai lowered his guard.

---

His fists opened. The fingers uncurled one by one, stiff from gripping, the leather of the gloves creaking as the tension left his hands. He flexed them. Closed. Opened. Closed again. The motion was automatic — the same check he ran after every spar, every bout, the body's way of asking itself _what did you earn?_ After the Inter-High finals his hands had been trembling, the knuckles swollen against the inside of the gloves, a dull heat radiating through the bones of his right hand where the third-round hook had connected with the side of Hoshi's skull and the impact had traveled back through his wrist and up his forearm and settled somewhere behind his elbow like a coal that wouldn't cool. That heat was the answer. That heat was the receipt. His hands had gone somewhere difficult and come back changed and the change was proof that the fight had been real.

He opened his fists again. Flexed the fingers. Searched for the heat.

Nothing.

His hands were fine. The knuckles were fine. The skin inside the gloves was damp with sweat and that was all — no swelling, no ache, no trace of the kind of exchange that leaves a mark on the man who threw the punch. He had hit the Japanese featherweight champion and his hands felt like they'd been shadowboxing.

Someone was calling the spar. Imai heard the voice from far away — coach, or the referee, or whoever had been standing at the edge of the ring keeping time. The voice said something about it being done. The voice said something about Ippo. The words passed through Imai the way a train passes through a station it doesn't stop at.

He lowered his hands to his sides.

The gloves were heavier now. Not from damage — from the opposite. They hung at the ends of his arms with the dead weight of tools that had done less work than they were built for. Imai stood in the center of the ring and his hands hung at his sides and the gloves pulled his arms toward the floor and the floor had nothing to say to him.

---

He turned his head. Not to look at anything — his eyes moved the way eyes move when the body is trying to locate itself in a room that has changed while it wasn't paying attention.

Aoki was on the ropes. His mouth was open and a sound was coming out of it that hadn't finished becoming a word. His legs were braced in a wide stance, the stance of a man who had stood up fast and hadn't decided what to do with the standing. Beside him, Kimura was moving forward — not running, not walking, something between the two, the controlled forward motion of a man whose body knew how to move efficiently under pressure even when the pressure had nothing to do with him.

The old coach had not moved.

He was where he had been before the spar started, at the edge of the ring, his hands on the apron, his face pointed at the canvas where Ippo lay. He was not calling instructions. He was not moving toward his fighter. He was standing with the stillness of a man watching something he had already seen in his mind before it happened in front of his eyes.

Imai watched him for a moment. Watched the way the old man's jaw was set, the way his shoulders held their position without adjustment, the way his eyes stayed on one spot and did not scan the ring and did not move to Imai and did not acknowledge the man who was standing over his fighter with clean gloves and a pulse that hadn't broken eighty.

No one was looking at Imai.

The gym was full of people and all of them were moving and none of them were moving toward him. They were flowing past his corner like water past a stone that wasn't blocking anything. The men from the showers — still wet, one of them naked from the waist up, a towel over his shoulder that he'd forgotten he was holding — they passed within arm's reach of Imai's corner and their eyes went to the canvas and they did not look up. Fujii, the reporter, had his notebook out, his pen already moving, and the young man next to him — the one with the neutral face and the mouth that lifted only on one side — was writing too, writing about the thing that had happened on the canvas, which was Ippo falling, which was the story, which had nothing to do with the man who had made him fall.

Imai had won and the room did not care.

Not because they disrespected him. He could have handled disrespect — disrespect was a form of attention, a shape that acknowledged the thing it rejected. This was not disrespect. This was absence. He was standing in the ring and he was not part of what was happening. He had won the spar and the spar was not the event. The event was the man on the canvas and the people who belonged to the man on the canvas and the silence that was spreading through the gym like water filling a container from the bottom up.

Imai rolled his right shoulder. The post-fight check — automatic, the body asking itself _what got hit? What needs attention? Where is the damage?_ He waited for the report to come back. Waited for the tightness in the joint, the small sharp ache that meant a punch had landed clean enough to compress the muscle against the bone, the satisfying burn that told you the fight had cost you something.

The shoulder rolled smooth. No tightness. No ache. Nothing compressed, nothing bruised, nothing that would remind him tomorrow morning that he had been in a ring today with a man whose name he had spoken out loud in his own bedroom, alone, at night, practicing the tone he would use when he finally stood across from him.

Clean.
