Yamaguchi. Alone.

She sat at her desk after Ippo left and closed the file and placed her pen parallel to the notebook the way she always did. Flat against the grain of the wood. Lined up with the edge of the paper. A habit no one had ever asked about because no one had ever noticed.

She replayed his last sentence.

_I'll try my very best._

What does that mean?

What does "try" mean to a man who threw punches on a torn muscle because stopping wasn't a thing his body knew how to do? What does "very best" mean to someone whose very best has always meant the absolute limit, the place past the place where the body says stop?

She opened her notebook. Wrote one line.

_If we strengthen the muscles in his back and his legs, he can strike using those punches without breaking himself. The bomb is there. We know where it is now. The question is whether he will let us defuse it or whether he will keep fighting until it decides for him._

She closed the notebook.

She sat with one hand on her lap and the other pressed flat against her cheek, her face resting in her palm, and for a moment she was still. The remnant of his voice was still in the room — the sound of Ippo saying that he would do everything in his ability to win — and underneath it the memory of blood, the faint copper scent of it, from the last time he fought in the ring and took a beating yet won. That Makunouchi.

Her eyes widened. She lifted her head from its near-sleeping position, and with her right hand she raised a finger and tapped it against her temple — once, twice, three times — the way a person does when a thought strikes too suddenly to be trusted. She pushed back from the chair, stood, crossed the room to the window of her office and leaned against the wall beside it. Felt the coolness of it against her forearms, almost like an embrace, and then her eyes softened as she looked down at the ground below.

The meeting with Ippo. His determination, his will, the way he looked at her when she told him the truth about his body. And then the Kumi incident — the girl who watched him with a worry that had no clinical name, no textbook entry, nothing to prescribe against. That incident had confirmed what the notebook entry only implied: Ippo would always be who he was. He would always fight the way he fought. He would always say _I'll try my very best_ and mean it in the way that terrified the people who understood what it cost.

She sighed, and the sigh carried weight — the kind that settles into the shoulders and stays. She reached back with her right hand, grabbed the back of her neck, tilted her head upward. Her fingers moved through her hair slowly, eyes closed. She leaned with her back flat against the wall and stood there for a breath, two breaths, three.

Then in a sudden movement she crossed back to the desk and reached for her phone.

She dialled. Waited.

In English, she said: "Hello. Long time no see."

Her pen was still parallel to the notebook. She hadn't moved it.

Ippo could train.

She had cleared him for it and the words were still warm in his ears when he left the clinic, and before the door had fully closed behind him his legs were already moving — not walking, running — toward the gym.

He still had the crutch. He had been told he didn't need it anymore but he hadn't put it down yet, and so he ran with it gripped in his right hand like a baton in a relay, his arm swinging it forward with every stride. He didn't mind the awkwardness. It felt like a new kind of training.

The impact of his feet on the pavement sent a jolt upward through his calves and into his thighs, and the sensation was so sharp and so familiar that it made his chest ache — not with pain, but with the recognition of something that had been missing. His breathing came heavy and ragged and loud, and the beating of his heart was there again, right there in his ears where it belonged, where it always was when he ran, and God how he had missed it. He had missed the weight of his own lungs working. He had missed the sound of his own body telling him it was alive.

The gym appeared ahead of him. He could hear the shouts from inside — the rhythm of combinations hitting pads, the skipping of ropes against the floor, the grunts that had no shape but filled the room like music. The scent of sweat hung in the air even outside the door, embedded in the walls, in the paint, in everything.

He stopped. He stood there with the crutch still in his hand. Looked at the door handle.

He reached for it the way he reached for a punch — shoulder forward, weight committed — and when his fingers closed around the metal his heart beat faster and his pupils widened and for one second, one single second, the door handle was the most important thing in the world.

He pushed inside. Nothing had changed. Everyone was in their corner. The bags swung. The timers beeped. The gym breathed the way it always breathed.

Ippo walked through the noise to the coach's office, the crutch tapping against his leg with every step, and Coach Kamogawa was there. Yagi too, sitting across from him with a stack of papers in his hands.

"Sorry I'm late," Ippo said, bowing slightly. "And thank you — for your concern the other day, Coach."

The crutch was still in his grip. He pulled it closer to his body without thinking, the way a child holds something familiar when they enter an unfamiliar room, even though this room was the least unfamiliar room he had ever known.

Yagi glanced at the crutch. Then at Ippo's face.

Kamogawa straightened his posture. His jaw tightened, teeth pressing together behind closed lips. Yagi felt the shift — the room getting heavier, the way it always did when the Coach moved from conversation to something else — and looked at Ippo expecting the weight to fall.

Ippo opened his mouth and for a moment his voice failed him. A strange half-sound escaped, something between a word and a breath, as though a thing he had said a hundred times before had suddenly become difficult. He swallowed. Steadied himself.

"She said that my muscle fibres have healed," Ippo said. "The concern is the pattern. The way I fight."

Coach gave him a straight look. No words. But the look itself was sharp enough to press against Ippo's skin, and Ippo felt the silence the way you feel heat from a fire you haven't yet touched.

"The torn fibres from my last fight — that wasn't random." Ippo gripped the crutch tighter. "It was because of how I fight. The strength I carry with my rotational punches. My body gave me a warning. And she said most don't get one. Most get a death sentence."

Coach's expression softened. Not much. His mouth opened slightly without releasing a word, his eyes less sharp now, and he leaned forward — not toward Ippo but toward whatever Ippo was about to say next.

"She said I was a ticking bomb," Ippo said. "Because of the Dempsey Roll. Every time I twist my hips — that's stress on my knee, on my tendons, on everything that holds the rotation together. And if I can't find another way..." He paused. Looked down at the crutch in his hands. "She said I would have to retire. Or limit the Dempsey to the point where it's not a weapon anymore."

The word sat in the room.

Retirement.

It didn't sound like a word. It sounded like a door closing somewhere far away that you could already hear.

Yagi looked at Kamogawa. Kamogawa swallowed — a hard, deliberate swallow — and then exhaled through his nose, a heavy sound, the sound of a man processing something he may have already known but had not yet been forced to hear spoken aloud.

He nodded once. Then he brought both hands together in front of him, fingers interlocked, elbows on the desk, the posture of a man preparing to give instructions rather than comfort.

"Based on what the doctor has told you," he said slowly, "and based on her report — though your version, kid, was more detailed than the paper she sent me —" a small pause, almost amused, "— we have to strengthen your lower body."

He told Ippo what the rest had cost. Not just the injury — the deconditioning. The fitness lost in weeks of inactivity, the muscle memory that fades when the muscles stop being asked, the endurance that has to be rebuilt from a place further back than where it started.

"The problem of the rotational stress," Kamogawa said, his voice gaining the low steady rhythm it took on when he was laying plans, "will be solved by the strengthening. The more you train, the more you load those muscles properly — the more your tendons can withstand what your body demands of them. Your issue, kid —"

He pointed at Ippo.

"— is that you are always pushed to the edge in your fights. Always. You let your opponents take you there. And a body that reaches the edge every single time will eventually stop coming back from it."

Ippo held the crutch close. His jaw tightened. Yagi watched the way Ippo's knuckles whitened around the grip, the way his eyes tracked Kamogawa's face like a student memorising a lecture he knows will save his life.

"The essence of a fighter," Kamogawa continued, "a boxer — is to hit. Gather as many points as possible. Stay fresh for as long as possible. Invest as little energy as possible. You do that by hitting before you get hit. Not by absorbing punishment and hoping the other man falls first."

Ippo understood. That was the opposite of what he did in the Sawamura fight. The opposite of what he always did. He clenched his teeth and said nothing and the understanding moved through him like cold water.

"Strengthen your lower body," Kamogawa said. Then, quieter: "The essence of a fighter — a synthesis of technique and raw spirit, a solid grasp of boxing theory. Like you, Ippo. That is the way I am teaching you. That has always been the way."

Ippo felt a relief so sudden it startled him. Those words — that he was on the path the Coach had set for him, that the path still existed, that the Coach still believed it led somewhere — those words landed in the place where the word _retirement_ had been sitting and replaced it with something warmer.

He heard a word he loved. _Pure fighter._ Kamogawa's dream — the legacy of a man who believed that the gap between raw, emotional fighting spirit and the technical necessities of boxing could be bridged. That strength and punching power were not enough alone, and heart was not enough alone, but together, trained correctly, guided patiently — together they were everything.

Ippo looked at Coach with trust. The crutch was still in his hand but he had forgotten it was there.

Kamogawa looked back at him. A raw look. A proud one.

"Now," the Coach said. "We have to talk about your next match."

The world. He meant the world.

With no regard to the injury, no hesitation in his voice, Kamogawa wanted to push him toward the world.
