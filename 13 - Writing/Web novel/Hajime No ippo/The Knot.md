# The Knot

The alarm goes at ten past four and it does not need to. I am usually awake before it. I lie there and wait for it anyway, because turning it off is the first thing I do right that day.

My mother is already dressed. She is always already dressed. She has been getting up at this hour for eleven years, first with my father and then without him, and in all that time I have never once come down and found the kitchen dark.

She does not talk in the morning. Neither do I. We eat rice standing up.

It is a fifteen minute walk to the harbour and the cold gets into your hands on the way, so you keep them in your sleeves, and then you take them out at the boat and they hurt for the first ten minutes and then they stop.

The boat is small. It was my father's and now it is my mother's, and one day people will say it is mine, and I have not decided what I think about that.

I do the ropes. That is my job and it has been my job since I was seven.

Loop, cross, pull.

My father taught me on the deck one afternoon with his hands, which were bigger than mine and moved without looking, the way hands move when something has been done ten thousand times. He did not correct me when I got it wrong. He undid it gently and let me do it again.

I learned it because he told me he would come back, and I wanted to have something ready for when he did.

He did not come back. The knot stayed.

We go out in the dark. The engine is loud for the first minute and then your ears adjust and it becomes a kind of quiet. The water is black and it slaps the side in a rhythm that does not repeat exactly, and if you try to hear the repeat you will be listening all morning and never find it.

There is a bruise on my ribs from Tuesday. Three of them held me and one of them did the rest. My mother saw it when I was pulling my shirt on and she did not say anything and I did not either, and that is not a bad thing between us. There are some mornings when saying it would take the whole day.

I work.

That is the part I do not know how to make sound like anything. I pull the line. I stack the crates. I get the ice out of the hold and my fingers go numb and I bang them against the rail to bring them back. The nets come up heavy and cold and they smell the way they smell, and I hold them the way he held them, higher on the left because of the way the boat sits.

Nobody is watching. That is not a complaint. There is nobody out here at all. There are four other boats and they are far enough away to be lights, and the men on them are doing exactly what I am doing and none of us can see each other well enough to be impressed.

The light comes slowly and then all at once. First the sky goes grey in one part and the water stops being black and starts being the colour of an old coin. Then a line of orange under the cloud. Then it is morning and you did not see the moment it changed, even though you were looking directly at it the whole time.

A bird landed on the rail this morning, close enough to touch, and looked at me, and left. That is the whole story of the bird. It was not a sign.

Here is what happened, and it is small.

Somewhere between the second haul and the third, I stopped asking.

I have a question I carry around. I will not write it out because writing it out makes it sound bigger than it is when it is happening. It is the one about whether I am anything. Whether there is a me underneath the things I am trying to be good at, or whether I am only a person on the days somebody sees me do something well.

I carry it to school. I carry it to the gym at six, where there is a room full of people who hit each other on purpose and are kind to me afterwards. I carry it to bed.

And for about an hour on the water this morning, I was not carrying it.

I was pulling rope. My hands were cold. I was thinking about whether the second net had come up light and what that meant for the afternoon. That is all that was in me.

Then I noticed.

And the noticing ended it, the way it always does, because the moment you turn around to look at a thing like that it is already behind you. You cannot hold it by looking at it. You can only find out afterwards that you had it.

But I had it. That is the part I want to keep. For an hour I was a person doing work and not a person auditioning to be one.

My father did that every day for twenty two years and I do not think he ever once knew it was remarkable.

We came in at half nine. I tied the boat.

Loop, cross, pull.

My fingers did it before I did. They have done it so many times that they no longer consult me about it, and there is something in that I keep almost understanding.

My mother was coiling the line at the bow. She has a way of doing it fast, flat, with her wrist, that I have tried to copy for years and cannot.

She said, you were quick today.

That is all she said. She said it to the rope, not to me, and then she picked up the crate that is too heavy for her and carried it up the ramp before I could take it, because she is like that.

I stood on the deck for a second with my hand still on the knot.

Three words. I put them somewhere I would not lose them.

Then I carried the ice up, and we sold what we had, and I went home and ate and slept for two hours, and at six I will go to the gym and get hit and get up and get hit and get up.

The alarm goes at ten past four tomorrow. It does not need to.
