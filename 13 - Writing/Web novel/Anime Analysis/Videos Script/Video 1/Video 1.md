# DOMESTIC GIRLFRIEND — Script Draft

## "Why Does the Boring Guy Get All the Girls?"

---

Have you ever read a manga that everyone tells you is trash — and you know it's trash — and it still gets you?

Like, you're sitting there reading Domestic Girlfriend, and yes what a weird name for a manga/anime but that is beside the point.

Everyone is calling it trash but I don't, I know it's problems and you know this is objectively insane. This kid sleeps with a stranger, falls in love with his teacher, and then his dad remarries and suddenly both of them are living in his house as his stepsisters. 

That's not a premise. That's a car crash. Every review you've ever read about this manga says the same thing — it's trashy, it's incest bait, it's melodrama for people who don't read real literature.

And you agree with all of that.

And it still got you.

===
It still made you feel something. Something you can't fully name. Something that kept you turning pages at 2am even though you could see every plot twist coming from three chapters away. And that's the part that bothers you, right? Not that it's bad. But that it works anyway. That the feeling is real even when the story is ridiculous.

And they too, they read it, they deeply enjoyed something about it but it feels bad to says so.

I want to talk about why.

Not to defend the manga. Not to tell you it's secretly a masterpiece. But because there's something happening underneath Domestic Girlfriend that the author probably doesn't even know she built. 

And once you see it, you'll see it in every harem manga, every love triangle, every story where some ordinary guy has five women fighting over him for no apparent reason.

The obstacle IS the desire. Make it forbidden and you make it irresistible. And I am going to explain this to you, why is it the case.

Let's start with the thing that annoys everyone.

Natsuo Fujii. The protagonist. The guy every woman in this story apparently can't resist.

What does he actually do?

He's a high school student. He writes — badly, at least at the start. I mean even in the middle of the story? near the end? Well average as Suzuko Azamina said, the genius writer who is 15 btw.

He joins the drama club. He's kind, I guess. He listens when people talk. He has that generic protagonist energy where he'll run across town in the rain to help someone.

And that's it. That's the whole package.

He's not funny. He's not intimidating. He's not mysterious. He doesn't have some dark past that makes women want to fix him. He's just... there. Existing. Being vaguely decent. And somehow every woman who enters a scene with him develops feelings within three chapters.

Your first reaction is to blame the author. Kei Sasuga must just be lazy, right? She can't write realistic attraction so she just has women fall for Natsuo because the plot needs them to. Standard harem writing. Move on.
![[Pasted image 20260402180601.png]]

Look, there is something that makes me stop thinking like that.

The central triangle — Natsuo, Rui, Hina — that one actually works. 

Not works based on no scientific psychological explanation, no no when I say a traiangle I mean a triangle of desire rooted in the question of WHY DO WE DESIRE THINGS?

There's something in the mechanics of that triangle that produces real emotional tension, even for readers who can see how manipulative it is. And I couldn't figure out why until I started reading this French literary theorist named René Girard, who spent forty years studying one question:

Why do we want what we want?

---

Girard's answer is uncomfortable.

He says desire is not a straight line. You don't just see something and want it. Desire is a triangle. There's you, there's the thing you want, and there's a third person — someone Girard calls the mediator — whose wanting is the actual reason you want it.

Think about it like this. You walk past a restaurant. It's empty. You keep walking. You walk past another restaurant. There's a line out the door. Suddenly you're curious. Suddenly you're hungry. The food might be identical. But the second restaurant has something the first one doesn't — other people's desire. And their desire is contagious. It infects you. It tells you: this is worth wanting.

Girard says this is how ALL desire works. Not just restaurants. Romantic desire. Career ambition. What you think is cool, what you think is beautiful, what you think matters. There's always a third point in the triangle. Someone whose desire came first and taught yours where to aim.

Now. Apply that to Domestic Girlfriend.

---

Rui doesn't fall for Natsuo in a vacuum.

Go back to the actual sequence. Rui sleeps with Natsuo as a transaction — she wants to understand what sex feels like, he's available, it's clinical. There's no love there. 

Even Rui  later said t hat her first time Natsuo felt like nothing, a bit painful

There's barely attraction. It's an experiment.

But then Hina enters the triangle.

The moment Rui realizes that Natsuo is in love with Hina — her older sister — everything changes. Suddenly Natsuo isn't just the awkward kid she had a meaningless encounter with. He's the person her sister wants. And Hina's desire coats Natsuo in a kind of brilliance he doesn't actually possess.

Girard has a word for this. He calls it crystallization — from Stendhal the writer if you know him. The idea that desire takes an ordinary object and covers it in false diamonds borrowed entirely from the rival's wanting. The branch doesn't change. The salt does the work.

Rui's feelings for Natsuo intensify in direct proportion to Hina's presence. Every time Hina shows affection, Rui gets more desperate. Every time Rui gains ground, Hina gets pulled back in. It's a loop. And the loop doesn't need Natsuo to be interesting. It doesn't need him to be special. It needs him to be desired by the other person. That's all. The rivalry IS the desire.

And here's the part that should disturb you a little.

This isn't bad writing. This is how it actually works.

Girard studied this in Dostoevsky, in Proust, in Stendhal — the greatest novelists in history all understood this mechanism. Swann doesn't love Odette until he suspects she's with someone else. Jealousy doesn't corrupt love — jealousy creates love. The rival's desire is the engine. Remove the rival and the desire deflates like someone unplugged it. Rui without Hina is just a girl who had an awkward first time with a stranger. Rui WITH Hina in the picture is consumed, desperate, willing to destroy her relationship with her own sister over this boy.

Sasuga gets this right. Probably without ever reading a word of Girard. She gets it right because she understands the emotional truth of triangles even if she can't name the theory. The taboo — step-siblings, student and teacher — isn't just shock value. 

From Girard's perspective, the obstacle IS the desire. Make it forbidden and you make it irresistible. Not because humans are rebellious. Because the obstacle functions as another mediator, another voice saying "this is so valuable it had to be locked away."

That's why the central triangle works. That's why it produces real feeling even in readers who think the premise is garbage.

---

But here's where Sasuga fails. And this is where the harem problem lives.

Every other woman who falls for Natsuo — every side character, every club member, every girl who shows up for three chapters and catches feelings — is presented as if her desire is spontaneous. As if she just independently noticed what a great guy Natsuo is. She saw his passion for writing. She saw him help someone. She fell for him.

But that's a lie. And not just a narrative convenience — it's what Girard calls the Romantic Lie. The founding myth of modern storytelling.

The Romantic Lie says: my desire is my own. I want what I want because of who I am. My feelings are original, authentic, spontaneous. Nobody taught me to want this.

The Novelistic Truth — what Girard found in the great novels — says the opposite. Your desire was never yours. It was borrowed. Copied. Inherited from a model you didn't choose and probably can't name.

Sasuga runs a Girardian engine — mimetic contagion, triangular desire, rivalry as the source of love — but she paints over the gears. She never lets you see that these women are copying each other's desire. She never lets Natsuo realize he's an empty center around which other people's wanting circulates. She presents the harem as proof that Natsuo is special, when Girard would say it's proof that desire is contagious and Natsuo just happened to be standing in the right spot when the first infection started.

A great novelist — Dostoevsky, Stendhal, Proust — would turn the camera around. Would let you see the contagion. Would let the character see it too and be destroyed by the recognition.

Sasuga never does that. She built the machine and then told you it was magic.

---

And yet.

This is the part I keep coming back to.

Even knowing all of this — even seeing the triangle, the contagion, the crystallization, the whole Girardian machinery grinding underneath — the manga still makes me feel something.

Not the plot. Not the twists. Something underneath all of that.

I'm watching these characters live inside their desires — consumed by them, destroyed by them, fully alive inside the triangle even when the triangle is eating them — and I feel... envious? No. That's not exactly it. It's more like nostalgia. But for something I never had.

I didn't have the drama, the jealousy, the running across town in the rain. None of that.

And when I watch Domestic Girlfriend, I don't actually wish I'd had it. I know the mechanism now. I can see the strings on every character. I know Rui's desire is borrowed from Hina's, I know Hina's guilt is what makes the desire feel significant, I know Natsuo is a blank screen these women are projecting onto.

But what I miss — what the manga makes me ache for — is the ability to be fully inside something without seeing the strings.

The ability to want something so completely that the wanting itself feels like proof you're alive.

Not a person. Not a relationship. A mode of being. The state of being consumed.

---

This is deviated transcendency.

The idea is that humans have a religious impulse — a need to orient themselves toward something infinite, something beyond the triangle. And when that impulse gets cut off from anything genuinely transcendent, it aims itself at human-sized targets instead. At lovers. At mentors. At fictional characters. At the feeling a manga gives you at 2am when the house is quiet and you're three hundred chapters deep and you've forgotten you exist.

The manga is selling you the promise of unmediated desire. A world where wanting someone is just... wanting them. Clean. Simple. No triangle. No mechanism. No Girard in the background explaining why the branch is just a branch.

And the ache you feel watching it isn't weakness. It isn't immaturity. It's the honest response of someone who can see the mechanism but still needs what the mechanism was trying to provide.

Connection. Consumption. The feeling of being fully inside something that matters.

---

So what makes this "trash" manga actually good?

Not the incest. Not the plot twists. Not Natsuo.

What makes it good is that Sasuga accidentally built a machine that runs on real emotional mechanics. 

The triangle between Rui and Hina is not fake drama — it's mimetic desire operating exactly the way Girard described it in the greatest novels ever written. The jealousy creates the love. The rivalry creates the value. The obstacle creates the wanting. That's not lazy writing. That's an author who understands the human heart well enough to trigger it, even if she doesn't understand why it's triggering.

What keeps it from being great is that she never turns the camera around. She never shows you the engine. She lets you feel the feelings without ever asking where the feelings actually come from.

And what makes it hit YOU — specifically you, watching at 2am, feeling something you can't name — is that the manga offers a world where desire is still innocent. Where people want each other for real reasons. Where the triangle produces genuine passion instead of the quiet, mechanical awareness that everything you've ever wanted might have been borrowed from someone else.

You're not crying because the story is sad.

You're crying because you can see how desire works, and you miss not being able to see it.

And honestly? That might be the most Girardian thing about the whole experience.

## STEP 5: Add Transform to EVERY manga panel

This is what makes your video not bland.

**Default recipe for a single panel (5-10 second hold):**

1. Add the panel to Track 2
2. Add **Transform** effect to it
3. Click the **keyframe button** (little diamond) at the start of the clip
4. **Zoom in slightly** on a meaningful detail (a character's eyes, two characters in frame, the central object)
5. Move the playhead to the END of the clip
6. Click the keyframe button again
7. **Adjust the zoom or position slightly** — either zoom in a bit more OR pan left/right to include more of the panel

**What this does:** The panel now slowly moves instead of sitting static. It feels alive.

**How much movement?**

- If the beat is **emotional or introspective** → VERY slow push-in (barely noticeable)
- If the beat is **building tension** → slightly faster push-in or pan across two characters
- If the beat is **a reveal or realization** → start tight on a detail, pull out to show the whole panel

**Do this for every manga panel.** It will take time the first few videos. Then it becomes muscle memory.

## STEP 6: Add Dissolve transitions between manga panels

Your manga panels should NOT hard-cut to each other (unless you're doing a rapid montage).

1. Make sure two manga panels overlap by **0.5 to 1 second** on the timeline

**Exception:** During FAST MONTAGES (like listing all the girls who fall for Natsuo), you DO want hard cuts. Speed and rhythm matter there. No dissolves.
