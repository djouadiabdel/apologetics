### "Why Nobody Can Ignore This Man"

**Runtime:** ~10 minutes. Full voice-over. No face cam.

---

### HOOK [1 minute]

There's a man who lived two thousand years ago. Half the people who study him say he changed everything. The other half say he ruined everything.

But here's what's strange. The people who say he ruined everything can't stop talking about him. They write books about him. They make videos about him. They build entire careers around proving he was a fraud. Some of them have recently argued he didn't exist at all.

If someone is irrelevant, you move on. If someone is wrong, you correct the mistake and forget about it. You don't spend centuries fighting a nobody.

So why can't they stop?

In 1961, a French literary critic named René Girard published a book. He wasn't a theologian. He wasn't defending Christianity or attacking it. He was studying novels — Dostoevsky, Stendhal, Proust. And he discovered a pattern in human behavior that had been hiding in plain sight. A pattern that explains not just why people hate this man — but why they hate him in exactly the way they do.

---

### THE PATTERN [2.5 minutes]

Here's what Girard found. And it's simple enough that you can test it on yourself before this video is over.

You almost never want something on your own. You want it because someone else wants it first. Or has it. Or points to it. Your desire passes through another person before it reaches the thing you think you want.

You don't care about a restaurant until your friend won't stop talking about it. You don't care about a job title until your colleague gets promoted. You don't want the relationship until someone else is in it. The thing didn't change. You changed — because someone else stood between you and the thing and made it valuable.

Girard called that person the mediator. Not a priest. Not a middleman. The person whose desire teaches you what is worth wanting.

Now here's where it gets interesting. When the mediator is far away — a celebrity, a dead hero, a fictional character — you can admire them openly. No shame. No rivalry. But when the mediator is close enough to be your equal, something shifts. You can't admit you're copying them. That would mean you're dependent. That would mean your desire isn't yours. And that is the one thing the modern mind cannot accept.

So what do you do? You hide the imitation. And the hiding turns admiration into something else entirely.

It turns it into hostility.

Not because the person did anything wrong. But because they're close enough to threaten your self-image. And the closer they are, the worse it gets.

Girard didn't invent this. He observed it — in fiction, in history, in everyday life. It works whether you believe in God or not. It works whether you've read a Bible or not. It's a description of how humans behave when someone stands between them and the thing they most want.

And it produces three predictable effects. Every time.

---

### THE THREE LAWS [5 minutes]

**Law one. The more you depend on someone, the harder you work to deny they matter.**

Watch this in action. The man I'm talking about is Paul. The apostle. First century. Wrote roughly half the New Testament. And for two thousand years, a specific group of people has been trying to destroy his credibility.

They say he hijacked Christianity. They say he invented doctrines Jesus never taught. They say he was a liar who admitted it in his own letters. Some critics have recently argued he may never have existed at all.

But here's the test. If Paul is a fraud — walk away. Build your version of Jesus without him. Go straight to the gospels and construct your theology from scratch.

Nobody does this. Every critic of Paul starts with Paul. Their entire argument is organized around dismantling him. Their version of Jesus has no independent content — it's defined entirely by what Paul supposedly got wrong. Remove Paul from the conversation and their framework collapses. They have nothing to push against.

That's not independence. That's dependence wearing a mask.

Girard predicted this. When the mediator is too close — too central, too unavoidable — the subject can't admit the mediator's importance. So he inverts it. He turns importance into hostility. He turns "I can't get around this man" into "this man must be destroyed."

**Law two. Whatever you accuse someone of doing, you are doing yourself.**

The critics say Paul imported foreign ideas into Christianity. But they are importing their own frameworks — modern secular assumptions, Islamic theology, Enlightenment rationalism — into their reading of the text. And they don't see it.

The critics say Paul selectively read Jesus to serve his own agenda. But when you actually check — and this is verifiable, not a matter of faith — there are dozens of direct parallels between Paul's teaching and Jesus's teaching in the synoptic gospels. Love fulfills the law — both say it. Bless those who curse you — both say it. Don't judge others — both say it. Return evil with good — both say it. The Lord's Supper — both describe it in nearly identical terms. The critics cherry-pick four or five points of apparent tension and ignore the rest. That is selective reading. And they're accusing Paul of doing it.

The critics say Paul constructed a version of Jesus that conveniently supports his own theology. That is precisely what they do when they build a "real Jesus" who happens to agree with whatever they already believed before they opened a single page. The secular critic's Jesus is a secular moral teacher. The Islamic critic's Jesus is a Muslim prophet. The Enlightenment critic's Jesus is a rational philosopher. Each one looks remarkably like the critic who constructed him.

Girard's term for this is the psychological circle. The accusation is never a diagnosis. It's a mirror. The louder the accusation, the more precise the confession.

Some critics go further. They claim Paul admitted to being a liar — in his own letters. They point to Romans 3:7, where Paul writes "if through my lie the truth of God abounded." But if you read the next verse — the very next sentence — Paul calls this reasoning slanderous and says the people who use it deserve condemnation. He's not confessing. He's quoting an accusation against him in order to demolish it. They point to 2 Corinthians 12:16, where Paul says "crafty fellow that I am, I caught you by trickery." But this is sarcasm — Paul is mocking people who accused him of secretly profiting from churches he refused to take money from. He immediately follows it by asking: "Did I exploit you through anyone I sent? Did Titus take advantage of you?" He brings witnesses against the charge.

The pattern is always the same. Take a sentence out of context. Strip the tone. Ignore what comes before and after. And then use the result to prove Paul is doing exactly what the critic is doing: reading selectively to serve an agenda.

**Law three. When the fight gets intense enough, you forget what you originally wanted.**

This is Girard's most unsettling observation. In any rivalry, there comes a point where the object disappears entirely. You're no longer pursuing the thing. You're locked in combat with the person who stood between you and the thing. And you can't walk away. The fight itself has become the point.

Look at what Paul's critics actually produce. Do they write detailed, positive accounts of who Jesus really was? Do they construct an alternative theology with its own content, its own depth, its own answers to the questions Jesus was addressing? Almost never. What they produce is: Paul was wrong. Paul lied. Paul corrupted. Paul didn't exist. The output is entirely negative. It's entirely organized around one man.

Girard had a word for this. Skandalon. The stumbling block. The thing that offends you is the thing that holds you captive. You can't get past it and you can't stop returning to it.

And here's the part that should give everyone pause — whether you're a believer or not. Paul himself used this exact word. First Corinthians 1:23. He said the cross is a skandalon. A stumbling block. The cross doesn't compete for power. It doesn't fight back. It doesn't play the game of rivalry. And because it refuses to play, you can never beat it. You can only keep tripping over it.

Paul diagnosed the mechanism that would be used against him — two thousand years before it happened.

None of what I've said requires you to believe Paul was right about God, or about Jesus, or about anything. Girard's framework is a secular observation about human behavior. It describes what happens when someone moves the mediator — when someone changes the structure through which people access the thing they most desire.

The question is not whether Paul was telling the truth. The question is simpler than that.

Why does he produce this much hatred — and not indifference?

The intensity of the accusation is the evidence. Not evidence of what Paul believed. Evidence of what he moved. And the people who attack him are not doing what they think they're doing.
