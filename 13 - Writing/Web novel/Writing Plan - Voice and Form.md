# Writing Plan — Voice and Form

*Working doc for the web novel. Read this first next time we pick the writing back up.*

## The situation (what was blocking me)

I kept believing I couldn't write novels, because I measured myself against the immersive **show-don't-tell / never-explain** style (Mushoku Tensei). Get inside the senses, let the world come only through description, camera behind the eyes, author never steps in. Trying to write that way felt alien and joyless and made me not want to write at all.

The reason it felt like death: that style shuts off the exact faculty that is my strength. I don't write by describing a world. I write by going inward and saying what a thing **means**. Being told to stop explaining was being told to stop talking.

I also over-relied on AI to "fix" my writing, and that was part of the block. Every time I outsourced the fix I stopped trusting my own hand and it went quiet. The panic in a draft ("stop stop I hate this, I don't even know what I'll write") is me flinching and waiting for a rescue. The material underneath the panic is the real thing.

## What my voice actually is

**Confessional first-person interiority. The addressed monologue.** I'm fluent writing *toward* a listener who isn't answering back: a diary, a letter, a long complaining post to someone. I'm bad at live conversation and that's fine, because this form is one-way and unhurried, which is exactly what lets me reach for the true word.

Two instincts I already do without trying, and they are the whole engine:

1. **Refuse the cliché, find the true word.** ("My heart keeps racing... it's not even racing, it's something else, my heart is steady, there's a knife in there.") I caught the easy image and corrected it to the exact one in real time. That reflex is rare. Trust it.
2. **Track a feeling against everything it survives.** (The knife: ran and it stayed, ate and it stayed, sat next to my mother and it stayed, writing now and it's still poking.) Same move as in *Once I was*, where the word keeps going in "at the same place it went in then." This is my signature. Locate a feeling, then test it against everything.

Evidence in my own files: `Short Stories Of her/1. Useless.md`, `Short Stories Of her/Once I was.md`. These already do it. My stuff naturally arrives in **charged fragments with white space between them**, not smooth connected chapters. That's not a gap in skill, it's a form.

## The form we chose

**The diary-and-letter novel, told in fragments.** Both things I already do, combined.

For the Ippo novel specifically: structure it as **Ippo's fight-diary plus the letters he writes and never sends.** Dated entries the nights before and after fights. No smooth third-person chapters, no scene-painting, no camera rules. A man addressing the dark in shards. This lets me write the only way I write well, and makes the book distinctive instead of one more third-person shonen novelization.

The thematic spine is already set (see `Me and Ippo.md`): *was I ever real, or only real in reflection?* — I needed her gaze to feel real, Ippo needs the ring. The diary form is perfect for that question because a diary is a man checking whether he's real when no one is watching.

## Craft rules for me (keep these)

- Write toward a listener. Diary voice, letter voice. If it feels like just talking, that's the signal I'm home, not a sign it's lesser. Chase the weird-easy feeling.
- Refuse the first image. Hunt the exact one.
- Don't describe a feeling. Track it against what it survives.
- Fragments. Charged blocks, hard white space between them. Don't force smooth transitions.
- No scene-painting, no "camera behind the eyes." That's not my instrument.
- No AI rescue mid-draft. Blurt first, all the way through, then look.
- Prose punctuation: no em-dashes, no ellipses, no dramatic colons.

## Reading to absorb the form

**THE ONE TO READ (matches me exactly — fragmented, short bursts, a real story):**
- **Mary Robison — *Why Did I Ever*.** A novel built out of 536 numbered fragments, many just a line or two. A wrecked, funny woman (Money, a script doctor), her kids, one in serious trouble, her chaos. No long passages ever — just short discrete hits that add up to a whole story. Robison wrote it on index cards, one fragment at a time, *because she couldn't sustain the long form either.* The exact limitation I named ("can't hold a long letter") is the method that made this book. This is my model.

**Others with a story, if I want more later:**
- **Justin Torres — *We the Animals*** — short novel in hard vignettes; three brothers growing up poor and wild; real arc, gut-punch turn. Raw and lean.
- **Jenny Offill — *Dept. of Speculation*** — clearest plot: a marriage, a baby, an affair that nearly ends it. Fragments holding a real arc.
- **Ocean Vuong — *On Earth We're Briefly Gorgeous*** — closest voice-match, but it's ONE long letter, not discrete fragments. Read for the voice, not the structure.

**More meditative / for the technique itself:**
- **Sarah Manguso — *Ongoingness: The End of a Diary*** — a book about diary-keeping, in fragments (~90 pages). Secretly about my exact question: making a life real by witnessing it yourself.
- **Max Porter — *Grief Is the Thing with Feathers*** — short, raw, shards and voices.
- **Renata Adler — *Speedboat*** — the cult benchmark for the fragment novel at full power.
- Bonus: Sarah Manguso — *300 Arguments*; David Markson — *Wittgenstein's Mistress* (advanced, a mind alone speaking to no one).

## Next action

Write one real fragment: **Ippo's diary, the night before a fight, addressed to no one, in fragments. No AI.** Then bring it back and only mark the strongest lines, don't fix them.
