We were doves before we were dragons. I have to begin there or none of the rest of it is true. Two small soft things that wanted only to be safe, that came together not to conquer anything but to fold a wing over the other and hide the trembling part where no one could get at it. That is what we were in the beginning and that is what we were even at the end, underneath, though by then you could not see it for the smoke.

The night it started we were doves. The university had emptied out into evening, the corridors going quiet and blue, and we found a small tiled room at the end of a hall and shut ourselves inside it, two birds nesting in the one warm corner of a cold and hostile building. I kissed her and she kissed me and the soft thing in my chest came open the way it only ever did with her, the breastplate sliding off, the underneath exposed and unguarded and glad to be. For a few minutes there were no dragons anywhere in the world. There was only the tile and her breath and the small folded safety of two creatures who had stopped, for once, expecting the attack.

Then the door, and the demons, and the attack.

I felt it happen in my own body, the change. The soft open chest where the breastplate had slid away — I felt the scale come up over it, fast, hard, plate by plate, the dove sealing itself inside something that breathes fire. I did not decide it. The dragon does not ask. One of them put a hand on me and the fire went out of me into my fists, and I hit them, and it did not stop at one, and somewhere in it a man's arm broke under what I had become, the sound of it the sound of the dove dying inside the scale. I was not protecting her by then. I want to be honest the way the other stories taught me to be honest. By the time the arm broke I had forgotten she was in the room. The dragon does not fight for the dove. It fights to prove it is a dragon.

And then the flood came, the way it comes in the last book, pouring out of the serpent's mouth in a river to sweep the woman away. By morning the whole university knew, and the knowing was a water rising. I ran from it. I climbed — and I will put this on the page because the shame of it is the realest part — I climbed a wall four, five, six meters of it, up to the women's dormitory, a grown man scrambling up sheer concrete in full daylight to hide, every hand- and foothold a small public humiliation, the dragon reduced to a lizard clinging to a wall while the flood rose under him. Then the council. The long table, the older men, the professors who looked at me and opened their mouths and let their own water out — accusation, contempt, the whole town gathering into one voice the way a town does when it has found the one body it can pour all its disorder onto. I was the thing that had to be cast out so the building could be clean again. I sat there and took it and did not really hear it, because a dragon's ears are deep under the scale.

And she was in the flood too. That is the part I cannot un-know. She was being swept in the same water, drenched in the same shame, and somewhere in those days she was loving me enough to stand in that flood beside me and take it — and I never turned to look at her doing it. I was too busy being a dragon, breathing fire at the men, clinging to the wall, deafened by my own scale. She was showing me the largest thing she had, her willingness to be ruined in the water with me, and I did not even turn my head. You cannot see a dove's love when you have made yourself a dragon. The eyes do not work for that anymore.

But the punch was not the worst violence I did. The worst one had no sound.

It came later, in a quiet talk, and it was quieter and it killed more. She told me a true thing — that I only ever came to the university to take her into a tiled room, that I came for her mouth and not her hours, that she knew I hated sitting with her, hated the dates, hated the slow plain time of just being two people in a day. And she was right, and the worst of it is what rose in me when she said it, the thing I am most ashamed of in all of this: I felt, somewhere under the scale, that if she would not give me her body then I could not see what was so important about anything else she was giving. I had taken a whole person — her hours, her mind, her willingness to drown in my flood, her folded trembling dove — and I had decided that all of it was only worth the suffering if it came with the one thing the dragon eats. That is the dragon standing before the woman in the last book, waiting to devour the child the moment it is born. I stood in front of everything she was and I waited, mouth open, for the only part of her I had decided to call food. I devoured her self and I called the devouring love.

So she grew scales too. Of course she did. You cannot stay a dove in front of a dragon; the fire teaches the skin to harden. She blamed me, and the blame was a dragon's blame, and now there were two of us, two great armored things breathing fire across a tiled room where two birds had once hidden, each one certain the other was the monster, each one right. We had become each other's serpent. And the terrible thing, the Girard of it, is that we were identical by the end — two mirrors of the same scale, fighting not over love anymore but over which of us was the dragon and which the wronged dove, when the truth was we were both, both at once, all the way down.

Here is the only thing I understand now that I did not understand then, and I will say it once and leave it.

Under my scale there was still a dove. Folded, half-smothered, frightened, the soft thing that had only ever wanted a warm corner in a cold building  it was still in there the whole time, behind the fire, behind the broken arm and the wall and the council and the cruel quiet sentence. The dragon was never the truth of me. It was the armor the dove built when the door opened, and the armor got so good at protecting the dove that it nearly killed it, the way a fist clenched long enough forgets it was ever a hand.

And I think  I cannot know, because dragons cannot see through each other's smoke I think under her scale there was a dove too. Just as frightened. Just as folded. Just as smothered by the very armor it grew to stay safe. Two doves, each sealed inside a dragon, breathing fire at the other's scale and never once seeing the small soft trembling thing it was built to hide. That is the whole messed-up story. Not a monster and a victim. Two birds who armored themselves against the cold and then could not find each other through the heat of their own protection, and burned, each inside the shell that was only ever trying to keep the soft part alive.
