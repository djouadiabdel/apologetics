# Passion

For years I thought love was a thing you were strong enough to do. I worked at it the way a man works at a wall he is building around a house, stone by stone, certain that if the wall got high enough and good enough no one inside it would ever be cold again. I wrote her poems at two in the morning. I read them to her down the phone and listened to her breathe. I turned away every other girl, would not even sit near them, kept myself clean for a thing I thought I was protecting. And under all of it I carried a single sentence like a stone in the chest: _to deserve her I have to be rich, I have to give her the world, and until I can, I am nothing._ I thought that stone was love. I thought the weight of it proved how much I cared. I did not know it was the heaviest part of the wall, and that the wall was not around her. It was around me.

I gave and I gave and I called the giving love, and at the end she said the truest and most unbearable thing anyone has ever said to me. She said I had given her nothing. And the madness of it, the thing that nearly killed me, was that I had given her everything I had — every poem, every refused girl, every year, the whole burden of a man trying to buy his way into being worth keeping. How could everything be nothing? I turned that over for months in the hollow room. _I gave you the world. You gave me nothing._ I had it exactly backwards and I could not see it, because the man inside the wall can never see the wall.

Here is what I could not see. Everything I gave, I gave to own. The poems were beautiful and they were also a ledger. The refused girls were faithful and they were also an invoice. I gave her the world so that I could be her world, so that one day I could stand over the pile of everything I had given and say the thing every man like me says when the woman starts to leave — _after everything I did for you._ That sentence is the whole lie in five words. A gift you can later present as a bill was never a gift. It was a purchase, and what I was trying to purchase was not her happiness. It was proof that I was real, that I was not nothing, that the curse I carried about myself was wrong. I was not loving her. I was using her as the bank where I deposited my terror of being worthless, and I called the interest love. And it failed, the way every transaction fails when the other person stops paying, because a love that needs a return is not strong. It is the most fragile thing there is. It shatters the second the beloved gives you nothing back.

So my love was not enough. That is the plain fact I had to lie down under. It could not save her and it could not save me, because it was never the saving kind. It was the buying kind, and the buying kind always runs out.

And then, I do not know how else to say it, I was shown the other kind.

It came as an image, the way the truest things come, sideways, when you have stopped fighting. A lamb, small, white, standing in a field, and beside it its mother. And the lamb was looking at another lamb, an innocent one, smaller than itself, and as it looked it began to grow — not bigger, but older, the way a face grows when it understands something it cannot put down. The mother watched her lamb the way mothers do, smiling, that soft and bottomless smile of a woman watching the thing she made simply exist and be good. I knew her. I will not pretend I did not know who they were.

Then her face changed. I watched it happen and I have not been the same since. The smile did not fade — it broke, slowly, from the inside, the way ice breaks, because she had seen what was coming for him. She saw the whole of it at once, the wood and the iron and the crowd and the long afternoon, and her face filled with a horror and a grief that no human face is built to hold, and she did not turn away. That was the thing. She could not save him. Her love, which was the largest love a creature has ever carried, could not lift one nail. And she stayed anyway. She stood in the full sight of his suffering with her face coming apart and she did not leave and she did not look away and she did not present anyone a bill. She just stayed and watched and loved him through it, into it, with nothing to gain and everything to lose and no return coming, ever.

And the son, on the wood, looked back at her. And he was smiling. Not the smile of a man who does not feel it — he felt all of it, more than she did. The smile of someone who has chosen this, freely, knowing exactly what it costs, for people who gave him nothing. And as he looked at her, words moved through the whole of me, not spoken, just true:

_This is what it feels like to love someone._

Not the wall. Not the poems-as-ledger, not the world given so the giver could be worshipped. This. To be poured out for someone who cannot pay you back and will not even thank you. To stay in the full horror of their suffering with no power to fix it and refuse to leave. To give everything and call it nothing, because the moment you could call it something you would be back at the bank, counting. Love is the thing that gives itself to the ones who gave it nothing, and it does not break when nothing comes back, because nothing coming back was never the point. _You gave me nothing,_ the whole world says to him, from the foot of every cross, and he looks down at the ones who gave him nothing and he loves them anyway, and that is the only love that has ever been strong, because it is the only one that does not need.

I understood, lying there, what I had been doing my whole life. I had been trying to extract from a girl's eyes the one thing no person can give another — the verdict that I was worth something, that I was real, that the curse was a lie. I had hunted it in her gaze and in the poems' reception and in being the provider and the sky, and it had never been there, and I had hated her for not giving me the thing she did not have. And the whole time, the worth I was clawing for had already been given, freely, from a place I had never once looked — paid for in full on a hill by someone smiling through the iron at the exact people who gave him nothing. It was not for sale. It had never been for sale. You cannot buy it with poems and you cannot earn it by being rich enough to deserve a girl, and you cannot lose it by being told you gave nothing. It was given. It was finished. It had been finished before I was born.

I did not get up from that healed. I want to be honest. The room was still hollow the next night and I still cried sideways into my ears. But something had moved that does not move back. I had spent my life believing love was a strength I had to be great enough to perform, and I had failed at it, and the failure had been the proof of my worthlessness. And now I knew that the love that actually saves is not a performance and not a wall and not a strength at all. It is a wound, freely opened, for someone who gave you nothing.

I had been trying to be the one who provides the world.

I had not understood, until I saw her face break and his face smile, that I was the innocent lamb being looked at. That I was the one who gave nothing and was loved through the iron anyway.

That is what it feels like to be loved by someone.

I am only now learning to stop refusing it.
