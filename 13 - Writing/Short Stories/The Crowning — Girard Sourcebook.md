# The Crowning — Girard Sourcebook

This is an **anthropological pressure-test** for the novella, not a second doctrinal system. Ratzinger supplies the primary ecclesial and eschatological frame. Girard helps expose where the narrator borrows desire, turns a model into a rival, makes himself a victim, or converts another person into the explanation of his life.

## Works used

- René Girard, *Things Hidden since the Foundation of the World*, with Jean-Michel Oughourlian and Guy Lefort, trans. Stephen Bann and Michael Metteer (Stanford University Press, 1987). Especially Book III, chapters 1–2 and 5, printed pp. 283–325 and 393–432.
- René Girard, *I See Satan Fall Like Lightning*, trans. James G. Williams (Orbis/Novalis/Gracewing, 2001). Especially chapters 1–3 and 9–12, printed pp. 7–48 and 103–160.
- René Girard, *Mensonge romantique et vérité romanesque* (Grasset, 1961). Especially chapters I and XII. This local electronic edition has no dependable printed pagination, so its references use the PDF page counter.

Quotations remain short. French fragments are followed by a working translation; do not paste either language into the novella merely to signal that Girard is present.

## The Catholic guardrail

Girard's account of mimetic desire, accusation, and the victim is immensely useful here. His explicitly “non-sacrificial” reading of the Passion is a theological proposal, not the whole Catholic doctrine of sacrifice, Eucharist, satisfaction, or atonement. For *The Fire*, use Girard to detect false sacrifice—especially the narrator's temptation to make suffering consecrate him—while retaining Ratzinger's Christological and ecclesial account of purification.

## Six Girardian instruments for this novella

### 1. Triangular desire

Desire does not travel along a clean line from subject to object. A mediator indicates what is desirable and often what kind of person the subject imagines he will become by obtaining it. In this book the mediators include the imagined future theologian, the crowned image of marriage, the rival receiving four seconds, and the narrator's own beautifully suffering self.

Seed: “Le médiateur est imaginaire, la médiation ne l’est pas.” — *Mensonge romantique*, chapter I, local PDF p. 9. Working translation: *The mediator is imaginary; the mediation is not.*

### 2. The model becomes the obstacle

The person who teaches the subject what to desire may possess it, refuse it, or merely appear to block it. Opposition then intensifies desire. The subject insists the obstacle interrupted an already independent love, because admitting imitation feels humiliating.

Seed: “Opposition exasperates desire.” — *I See Satan*, printed p. 10.

### 3. Metaphysical desire

The object gradually matters less than the being supposedly possessed by the mediator. The narrator does not only want a relationship; he wants proof that he is the sort of man who can be chosen, crowned, remembered, and finally listened to. Theology and romance can therefore become two costumes for the same desire to acquire being from another.

Seed: “Les hommes seront des dieux les uns pour les autres.” — title of *Mensonge romantique*, chapter II, local PDF p. 30. Working translation: *Human beings will be gods for one another.*

### 4. Mimetic doubles

Rivals experience themselves as utterly different while becoming alike in gesture, argument, and fixation. The seated teacher and the narrator oppose one another with the same correctness, the same crown, and the same refusal to stand. The narrator's hatred of the other man's condition is knowledge he has not yet applied to himself.

Seed: “Nothing distinguishes them.” — *Things Hidden*, printed p. 299, describing antagonistic doubles.

### 5. Scandal and accusation

The *skandalon* is a fascinating obstacle: the subject cannot pass it and cannot stop looking at it. Indignation then produces explanations of guilt. A group—or a solitary narrator replaying a group inside himself—can gain temporary order by assigning responsibility to a victim: the woman, Gregory, the wrong list, the rival, the body, or “the rotten self.”

Seed: “Judge not, that you be not judged.” — Matthew 7:1, quoted in *Things Hidden*, printed p. 427.

### 6. Conversion

Novelistic conversion does not prove that every desire was unreal. It reveals the mediator, ends the hero's claim to unique victimhood, and allows the beloved to become a neighbor rather than a divinity. This is painfully exact for the ending of *The Crowning*.

Seed: “Marcel doit renoncer ... à faire de l’être aimé une divinité monstrueuse.” — *Mensonge romantique*, chapter XII, local PDF p. 141. Working translation: *Marcel must stop making the beloved a monstrous divinity.*

## Chapter map

| Novella chapter | Girardian pressure | What it may reveal |
|---|---|---|
| I. The Warmth | Triangular desire | The crown and future theologian mediate what he thinks he wants spontaneously. |
| II. What Cannot Be Returned | Acquisition | A person is turned into an object whose desirability increases when another holds or withholds it. |
| III. The Warm House | Metaphysical desire | He asks gifts to prove the importance and solidity of his being. |
| IV. The Ledger | Reciprocal accusation | Measuring guilt permits him to remain judge, victim, and creditor simultaneously. |
| V. The Two Rooms | Scapegoating | A true historical distinction becomes a way to blame a dead classifier for the narrator's chosen shelter. |
| VI. The Name | Imagined mediator | The future theologian is an image the narrator constructed and would obey because it bears his desired prestige. |
| VII. Four Seconds | Internal mediation | The rival gives the touch its unbearable value; envy reveals the triangle bodily. |
| Interlude. The Stair | The model becomes the obstacle | He maintains the unanswered question, because while no answer exists her word keeps the power to settle what he is. |
| VIII. The Man Who Was Right | Mimetic doubles | Narrator and seated teacher become indistinguishable while insisting upon their difference. |
| IX. The Fire | False self-sacrifice | Suffering can become a strategy for consecrating the sufferer; the Lamb destroys that strategy. |
| X. The Crowning | Novelistic conversion | He must surrender both the beloved-as-divinity and himself-as-eternal-victim before he can ask or leave. |

## How to use this beside the Ratzinger cards

Read Ratzinger first and Girard second. Then ask two different questions:

1. **Ratzinger:** What truth of grace, freedom, communion, judgment, or Christ is this event approaching?
2. **Girard:** Which person or image taught the narrator to want this, and whom is he blaming for standing between him and it?

Close both sourcebooks. Write the event from the body. Never put the words *mimetic desire*, *mediator*, *scapegoat*, or *metaphysical desire* into the novella unless the narrator is using jargon as another hiding place.
