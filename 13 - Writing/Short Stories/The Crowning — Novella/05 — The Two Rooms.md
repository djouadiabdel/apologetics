# V. The Two Rooms

The fourth floor has one name over the door and two rooms behind it, and this is where I was destroyed.

Listen to me carefully now.

There was a man in the desert who sat still for years and watched his own mind as a physician watches a fever. He wrote down what came. He counted eight. Not seven. Eight.

Then, a long time afterwards and a long way west, in a chair, another man tidied the list. He had reasons. Seven agrees with the days and the gifts and the pillars. Two of the eight looked to him like one thing seen twice, so one was placed inside the other and the door was given a single name.

They were not one thing. They were the only two on the whole list that had to be kept apart.

So the door was given a single name, and the name was wrong, and behind it there are still two rooms.

In the first room they are grieving. That is all. Some sit against the wall with their knees drawn up. Some walk while they grieve, which is harder. One has been setting a place at a table and taking it away again. Nothing in that room asks them to stop.

The person at the table placed a cup, a plate, and a spoon. The cup was cracked from lip to base and leaked a dark line onto the cloth. Each time the line reached the table's edge, the place was cleared and begun again.

I asked who the place was for.

The person touched the cracked cup with two fingers and said nothing.

I said the repetition was preventing the wound from closing. This was before I had understood the room. I lifted the cup and carried it to a shelf where other broken things had been put beyond use.

The person did not protest. That encouraged me.

The next place was set without a cup. When the hand reached the empty space, it remained there, curved around what was missing. The whole body bent over the hand.

I returned the cup.

You had taken the chair across from the empty place. You did not touch the table or ask who should have occupied it. You remained while the cup leaked and the cloth was changed.

The person setting the table looked at you once. Neither of you spoke.

I stood near the door with the useful cup from the next place in my hands. Nobody had asked me to hold it. When I set it down, it made more noise than I intended.

I tell you this is not a sickness. It is the last working instrument a person has left. Show me what is grieved and I will show you what was still being held. The room should never have been on the list.

Then you spoke.

Weren't you grieving before too?

It was the first thing you had said in the whole descent. Your chin lowered before you began and your voice caught once on grieving, but you did not take it back.

I said yes, of course, but the distinction was precisely—

I heard you, you said.[^c5-r1]

I was already at the other door with my hand raised. You did not speak again.

In the second room nobody grieves and nobody rests. They check the light. They go to the window. They go out and return and read two lines and put them down and go out again. Every one is certain that in another room, in another city, among people who would be kinder, the thing would become bearable. They call it many names and none is the true one. They have ceased to care, and the ceasing has a direction, and the direction is back toward the nothing we were all made from.

They talk about the weather in there. They are very calm about it. And their hands are wet, all of them, every one, and none of them has mentioned it, and I have never once seen a man in that room dry his hands.

The first man I spoke to had a coat over one arm. He crossed from the door to the window while answering me and from the window to the door before I had finished the next question.

Where are you going?

Out.

Where did you come from?

Out.

He smiled at the symmetry. His hands left wet marks on the coat.

I told him to stay.

The word stopped him. I knew at once that I had found the cure because his body reacted before his argument did. He looked at the nearest chair, then at me, and sat.

I sat across from him. You remained at the first room's threshold, where both rooms were visible.

The man and I made an inventory of what was present. One chair under him. One under me. A coat. A window. A door. The sound of somebody crossing the corridor outside. His two hands, wet. My two hands, dry.

After each item I said, stay.

He repeated it. His breathing slowed. The coat slipped from his knees onto the floor and he did not pick it up.

Others gathered. One wrote the word on the back of a page and folded it into a square. Another asked whether it should be repeated before or after the desire to leave arrived. I said before, during, and after. A third asked how long a person must remain. I told him duration was the wrong measure. The act of remaining was complete in every instant.

They wrote that down too.

The man in front of me had become very still. I believed I had saved him.

Then you shifted in the doorway.

I turned toward you for only a moment. When I looked back, the chair was empty. The coat remained on the floor. Wet footprints crossed to the window, but the window had not opened.

I told the others he had misunderstood the instruction. They wrote that down beneath the first sentence.

Then I noticed that my own hands were wet.

I went to the window to see whether he was below.

There was nothing outside it.

I returned to the chair, read the two written lines, put them down, and went to the window again.

Now hear what the wrong count did to me.

I had one word for both rooms. I was sorrowful and I was indifferent and I did not know they were two, because nobody had left me a word to tell them apart with.

So when the second thing came on me I called it by the name of the first. I called my not-caring a sorrow. I nursed it. I sat with it in the evenings like a man sitting with a sick relative and admired my own constancy, and I thought myself a man of deep feeling because I could not get up, and I wrote about it, and people told me it was beautiful.

It was not beautiful. It was the other room. I was in the other room for eleven years.

I did not spend all eleven at the window. Some of them I spent remaining exactly where I had been left, giving before anything could be requested, finding new uses for myself, making it impossible for anybody to say I had abandoned the room. I called that staying. It was a way of leaving without moving. A request kept underground cannot be refused twice.

And the relief I felt when I found an administrative decision to blame. Not a devil, not a woman, not my father. A list made tidy in a room I had never seen, by a man who died centuries before my mother was born and meant nothing but good by it. For one whole landing I had a dead defendant and eleven years with no fingerprints of mine on them.[^c5-g1]

The category failed me. It did not walk those years for me.

The cure for the second room is one word, I told you, and the word is stay.

Nobody stays. I did not stay.

This is where I met him properly, and I will tell you how because it is the only funny thing that happened to me here.

I had gone to the window for the ninth time that hour and he was there already: an old man, small, in a white cassock that had no business on any of these floors, looking out at nothing with his hands behind his back like somebody waiting for a train he knew was coming.

I recognised the cassock before I recognised anything else. I had chosen it years before. Not that cassock, of course. The one I would wear when the two halves had tired of misunderstanding one another and required somebody able to speak across the wound. I had imagined the cloth falling exactly that way from older shoulders. I had imagined the hands behind the back so often that I had begun practising the posture while reading.[^c5-g2]

He was not my face made old. That would have frightened me less. He was the man I meant my face to prove I had become.

I asked him how long he had been standing there.

He said that was not a question this place could answer. Then he apologised for the answer, and I have thought about the apology more than the answer.

Then he turned and he spoke to you.

Not to me. He had his back half to me and said something to you that took about as long as a sentence takes. I did not hear one word of it. You did not tell me, and I did not ask.

I told myself this was because the sentence belonged to you.

The other explanation had already occurred to me and I had rejected it: that I was not the person in the room he had come to instruct.

When he left the window, he came with us. He walked on your side, half a step behind, his hands still joined at his back. I treated his company as permission to continue teaching.

Now the gate, and this is the one that opened me.

I put down what it asked for last, after everything else, because I did not believe it counted as a possession.

It is the thing I know about myself. That I am the worst of them. Not badly behaved, not weak, the worst, the bottom of the list, and that everything decent I have ever done was a performance run by something rotten for its own reasons. I have held that for so long that I stopped experiencing it as an opinion. It is the floor I stand on. It is how I know where down is.

And the gate would not open with it in my hands.

So I said, take it, and I meant it the way you mean a thing at the last moment.

And it did not go quietly, because there is another version and I do not hold it.

There is another version of me, one I cannot hold. It was built elsewhere out of things I did without arranging them as evidence, and in it I am kind. Not complicated. Kind. I cannot inspect it in full or get at it to correct it.[^c5-rg1]

And it is true.

The gate placed the two versions on either side of me.

They were not bodies. They were more like the spaces bodies leave when they stand in a room for a long time. In the one to my left, every decent act had its concealed machinery exposed: the wish to be necessary beneath the offer of help, the wish to be remembered beneath patience, the pleasure of moral advantage beneath forgiveness. Nothing good survived inspection because inspection could always descend one motive farther.

In the one to my right were the same acts without the machinery. Not purified acts. The same ones. A chair pulled out before I knew who would sit. Work completed when nobody had been told it was mine. A restraint forgotten immediately after it cost me something. Things too small to support the theory I had built about myself.

I moved toward the left version because it contained more evidence.

The right moved with me.

I stepped toward the right. The left came too. There was no position from which I could keep only one.

I asked the old man which version was the deception.

He said nothing.

I asked which was more fundamental.

He looked at my feet.

The floor beneath them had begun to separate along a line exactly between the two versions. I widened my stance to keep one foot on each side. The crack continued through my body. I felt it enter at the base of the spine and travel upward one joint at a time.

You came nearer. Your hand rose to the height of my shoulder and stopped without touching it.

I told you not to interfere. If the division was true, assistance would only conceal it.

Your hand went down.

When the crack reached the chest they both spoke. Not one and then the other. Both, with my mouth, one saying that refusing help proved the rot and the other no, no, it proved I was trying not to use you, and both arguments were complete, both had every premise, both—

I tried to say which one was mine.

There was not a which.

The sound in my chest went on after my mouth stopped. The gate remained where it was. One version of me was true in my hands and the other was true somewhere I could not reach, and there was no body I knew how to make that could—

It went on for a long time.

I will not tell you I came out clean. I do not know what came out. I am telling you what happened and not what it was for.

Something was taken off me on that floor. I have not been able to find it since. I still go looking, and it is never where I left it.

My hands were up for the whole of it. Both of them on my head, gripping. Afterwards there were two soft places where my thumbs had been. They did not come out. I pressed at them while we went down the stairs and then stopped, because the thing was warm, and warm was the only argument I could still make for it.

## Theological material — Ratzinger

Full context: [[The Crowning — Ratzinger Sourcebook#IV. The Theology of Death — printed pp. 69–103]].

**Ecclesial idea:** Ratzinger distinguishes mere continuation from life: life is communion, while death begins in absence of relation. Grief can remain warm because it still bears relation to one who is absent; acedia cools into disengagement. Yet he explicitly refuses a cult of pain: suffering should be reduced where possible and is never currency with which to purchase love.

**Short seed:** “All joy wills eternity.” — Friedrich Nietzsche, quoted by Ratzinger on printed p. 94.

**Do not turn it into:** *I suffered because I loved more, therefore my suffering glorifies the love or gives me a claim.* That is the narrator's invoice returning in devotional language.

**Try writing from:** Let the cracked cup remain on the table while she says, *Weren't you grieving before too?* Re-dump only his half-finished answer. Make him expose, accidentally, why he needs grief to count as proof about himself.

## Desire material — Girard

Full context: [[The Crowning — Girard Sourcebook#5. Scandal and accusation]].

**Girardian pressure:** The historical correction may be true and still become a scapegoat story. “A tidy man ruined eleven years” gives the narrator a single culprit whose removal would make his private history intelligible. The old classification harmed his language; it did not perform eleven years of choices for him.

**Short seed:** “A feverish desire to differentiate between the guilty and the innocent.” — *Things Hidden*, printed p. 426.

**Do not turn it into:** Excusing Gregory's alteration of the list, or denying that bad categories have consequences. The point is the narrator's pleasure when a complex life suddenly has one dead defendant.

**Try writing from:** Keep every correct historical fact. Remove one sentence of accusation and replace it with the bodily relief he feels when he says *administrative decision*. Let the relief indict him.

## Sentence footnotes — Ratzinger + Girard

[^c5-r1]: **Ratzinger — communion begins by receiving another voice as other.** She does not supply a doctrine; she reports that his words arrived and refuses the distinction he was preparing. This is the first small defeat of his solitary theology. Her sentence should remain ordinary.

[^c5-g1]: **Girard — a true cause is becoming a scapegoat.** Gregory's classification affected Western language, but “I was ruined by” transfers an eleven-year history onto one absent culprit and gives the narrator the relief of clean accusation. Preserve the historical truth and expose the relief physically.

[^c5-g2]: **Girard — the desired self is a mediator.** He has rehearsed the posture of the man whose authority he wants. The cassock does not merely symbolize vocation; it has already taught his body what prestige looks like. A tiny copied gesture elsewhere can make this plant fire before the Lamb reveal.

[^c5-rg1]: **Communion defeats both self-idealization and self-scapegoating.** Ratzinger allows the truth of a person to exist in relation and beyond private possession; Girard prevents the rotten-self theory from becoming another unanimous accusation. The right-hand version must remain inaccessible—not a comforting acquittal he can cite.
