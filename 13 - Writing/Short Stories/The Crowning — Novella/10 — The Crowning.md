# X. The Crowning

And now the part I will be laughed at for.

I woke up.

I have gone back and forth on whether to tell you at all. I woke, and my face was on stone, and it was cold, and it was the kind of cold that means a place where nothing has happened.

So it was a vision. Fine. I have had one and I am not going to argue with anybody about it.

You are in front of me.

There is a crown on you and it is made of light. It is not the one you put against the wall. It comes off you in every direction and has taken your face. That is the only way I can put it. The light is where the face should be and I cannot see through it.[^c10-rg1]

I have the rest of you. I have the colour of the skin and the shape of the arms and the way you stand with the weight on one side. I have all of it. The face is light.

Your body is turned toward me. One shoulder is lower than the other. I decide there is a smile inside the light because I have always been fast at deciding what a body means.

I started walking.

I am still walking. Do not ask how long. I have told you already that the question is badly made. I have been walking toward you since I woke. You have not moved. I have not arrived. The distance behaves exactly like the warmth behaved above the seventh gate: every route I take runs alongside it and feels like approach.

And on the way there are the others.

I did not expect them. They are off to the side, out where I do not go, and they are all me. Rooms and rooms of me, thousands, every one of them a life I could have had, running at the same time, and I can see into all of them at once and that is the worst thing this place has done to me yet.

In nearly all of them I am walking.

Not dying, not raging, not being punished. Walking, at a good pace, toward something warm, explaining. In one of them I have a house and five children and I am lying in the dark solving a heating problem. In one I am telling somebody the correct order of the seven floors and I have got it right and she has stopped listening and I have not noticed.

The house is easiest to mistake for arrival. I can see all five children asleep in separate arrangements of limbs. One has kicked away the blanket. One is holding a wooden animal against the face. The hall light has been left on because somebody is afraid of the dark and everybody has agreed not to say who.

At the end of the hall there are two cups beside a bed.

I am under the blanket with my eyes open, calculating why the far rooms receive less heat. I have measured the distance from the furnace, the width of the doors, the hours each room spends closed. Beside me, somebody is awake too.

She says my name.

I tell her the western wall is losing heat faster than the others.

She says there is something she needs to ask.

I tell her I am listening. I am listening so intensely that I begin constructing answers before she has drawn breath. I prepare gentleness, then boundaries, then the distinction between what she feels and what the evidence supports. She watches me prepare.

The question does not come.

In the morning I repair the wall. The room is warmer the next night. I consider the problem solved.

In another life the old cassock fits me.

I am standing between two altars in a church large enough that my voice returns a moment after I use it. Men who had refused the same table have entered through opposite doors. I speak first in one vocabulary and then in the other. I do not pretend the wound was verbal, but I find words that allow each side to recognise its blood in the other's account.

When I finish, nobody applauds. It is a church. They lower their heads instead, which is better.

Later there are books. Other people place my name on them larger than I would have chosen. Students quote the sentence that opened the final door. I correct them when they detach it from the paragraph that made it safe.

At night, in a room provided by the church, I read what has been written about the reconciliation. One historian says conditions had been ready for years and any sufficiently patient intermediary might have succeeded.

I write a correction in the margin.

I do not send it.

The restraint warms me until morning.

They are not endings. That took me a long time to see. Every one is a man still walking, and he goes on walking, and the light remains exactly where it is on his face.

There are a small number of them where I have stopped.

I looked for what is different in the small number where I stop. It is not that the man is better. At some point he turns, opens his mouth, and asks for something plainly, in his own voice, in a way that can be refused.

I cannot hear the questions. I can see the answers enter their bodies.

In one room the light comes closer.

The man in that room receives the answer he wanted. I see it because his whole body moves toward the person before him and she does not move away. Hands rise. Somebody other than either of them places what must be placed.

For a while the room is too bright to examine.

Then ordinary time begins. There are tired mornings, badly received sentences, an evening when one person needs silence and the other experiences the silence as a verdict. The man reaches for the old instruments. He counts who began the last conversation. He weighs the warmth in what was said against the word that was omitted. He turns a freely given yes into evidence that every later no is a contradiction.

The crown on him becomes difficult to see beneath the one he has drawn.

In that room, asking was not the last gate. It was only the first act performed after the fire.

In another room she reaches for him before he asks.

He asks anyway.

She says she wants him there. Then she says she does not want the name he has brought for what is between them. Both sentences are true.

She feels his fingers change around hers and takes her hand back.

He has not been refused. He has not been crowned.

In one, the person before him says something very short. The man bends as though struck. His mouth opens again with the beginning of an explanation. I know the explanation. It is a good one. It would prove that the question had been misunderstood and that the answer had therefore been given to something he did not ask.

He closes his mouth.[^c10-r1]

Then he goes.

Nothing follows him. No fire punishes him for leaving. No voice calls him back to complete the argument. He goes because he was told to go, and the obedience does not make him smaller, and the light remains where it was without becoming cruel.[^c10-r2]

That life was the one I could not stop watching.

When I looked back at you, you had angled away.

I stopped walking.

Then I looked down at my arm.

I have been carrying it this whole time. Since the first line. Since before the first floor.

It is paper.[^c10-g2]

It is a drawing of a crown. It is mine. I know the hand. I made it a long time ago on a night I remember exactly, and I made it well. I was always good at that. I carried it down seven floors, held it toward a gate that did not want it, put it back on my own head, and called the act restraint.

And it is warm.

It is warm the way it has been warm every hour since I put it on, and it is exactly as warm as the back of my own neck, and it has been sitting on my own head for the whole of this.

Nothing is warm on its own. I said that at the start. I said it about the fire and was right about the fire. I carried the other half on my head without once running the principle in the other direction.

The paper was not warm because something lived inside it.

It was warm because I did.

It came apart.

No, not like that. Nothing happened, that is the point, I opened my hand and the fold—there was no fold anymore. Dust between the fingers. I tried to keep the larger piece but it broke where I touched it and then that piece was the dust too and I closed my hand because some of it was still there, I could feel it, I could—

There is no piece left.

So I have—

I have nothing.

You are still angled away.

I do not close the distance.

After a while, you close one step of it.

Only one.

The light thins at the edge of your face. I can see your mouth now. It is not arranged for me.

The dust of the paper remains in the lines of my palms. I turn my hands over. It falls onto the stone between us.

You look down at it.

Then you look at my face.

I wait.

I am going to open my mouth now.[^c10-rg2]

## Theological material — Ratzinger

Full context: [[The Crowning — Ratzinger Sourcebook#III. Word and Reality in Contemporary Appreciation — printed pp. 46–66]] and [[The Crowning — Ratzinger Sourcebook#VII. Hell, Purgatory, Heaven — printed pp. 215–238]].

**Ecclesial idea:** What is divine is received rather than grasped. Heaven rests on freedom and is communion, not private possession. Ratzinger's “reward” is the fulfilment of a unique person in the whole body of Christ, not a spouse awarded for suffering. The paper crown exposes the narrator's attempt to place on himself what only another can freely place—and another is equally free never to place it.

**Short seed:** “A white stone, with a new name written on the stone.” — Revelation 2:17, quoted by Ratzinger on printed p. 235.

**Do not turn it into:** The woman as the glory of God made available to him, her yes as ecclesial coronation, or her no as a gate that grace must overrule. Her freedom is not scenery in his eschatology.

**Try writing from:** Read the life in which she says *go*. Close the chapter. Speak only the instant after his mouth begins the good explanation and then closes. What does obedience do to his body before he can call it holy? Keep that fact available when you return to the final *I am going to open my mouth now.*

## Desire material — Girard

Full context: [[The Crowning — Girard Sourcebook#6. Conversion]]. Read *Mensonge romantique*, chapter XII, local PDF pp. 139–147.

**Girardian pressure:** Girard's novelistic conversion asks for two renunciations at once: the beloved can no longer be a divinity whose choice supplies being, and the desirer can no longer be the uniquely innocent victim of her distance. The paper crown is both claims made visible. Its dust does not decide whether he loves her; it removes the theology that made her answer a judgment on his existence.

**Short seed:** “Faire de l’être aimé une divinité monstrueuse.” — *Mensonge romantique*, chapter XII, local PDF p. 141. *To make the beloved a monstrous divinity.*

**Do not turn it into:** A tidy Girardian reveal that he never loved her, or that every possible relationship is rivalry. The ending remains an actual question between free persons.

**Try writing from:** In the life where he is told *go*, remove every sentence that makes him nobly obedient. Give him one ugly second in which he wants to become the victim again. Then let him leave before he can build a crown from that pain.

## Sentence footnotes — Ratzinger + Girard

[^c10-rg1]: **This image is productively dangerous.** Ratzinger: glory and salvation belong in Christ and communion, not in possession of one human being. Girard: the beloved becomes a divinity whose radiance erases her face. Keep enough particular body and independent movement that the prose knows the light may be his projection.

[^c10-r1]: **Ratzinger — freedom becomes bodily obedience.** The conversion is not that he has found the perfect answer but that he can cease manufacturing one. Because the sentence is only four words, the surrounding body must establish its cost.

[^c10-r2]: **Ratzinger — refusal does not negate either person's dignity.** The answer opens the gate because it is received as another's free word. Obedience means accepting the real relation rather than redescribing it until it yields the desired one.

[^c10-g2]: **Girard — the mediator is exposed as made material.** The crown condensed marriage, recognition, vocation, suffering, and the imagined future self into one self-authored object. Its paper does not prove that love was unreal; it proves that this coronation came from his own hand.

[^c10-rg2]: **The unresolved question now has two tests.** Ratzinger: can he ask without making her answer the instrument of salvation? Girard: can he hear her without making her divine or himself her victim? Do not answer either test in narration. The open mouth is the event.
