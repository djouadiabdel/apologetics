title: "De Dolore Eius Qui Multos Intellegit et cum Nemine Ambulat"
aliases:
  - "The Stations"

*On the sorrow of one who understands many and walks with no one.*

Wednesday begins at one in the afternoon because I worked Sunday for it.

That is not an image. I took the Sunday nobody wanted and the rota gave me Wednesday back. There are four hours before the city starts returning from work and the shops fill and being outside alone becomes a thing nobody notices.

I have made rules for those hours.

Leave without an errand. Stay somewhere after the thing you ordered is finished. Speak first once. If somebody asks a personal question, answer the question they asked and not the more interesting one underneath it.

Ask one obvious question.

I have never managed that one. The obvious question is available to anybody, which is what it is for, and asking it means saying out loud that I do not already know. So I ask the interesting one instead, which sounds like attention and is insurance against looking stupid for the length of one sentence.

Do not take a book.

I put the book inside my coat.

It is Ratzinger, which makes this worse. A novel could be mistaken for company. A German cardinal under the left arm is equipment.

He became pope, after. That still stops me. He wrote this at a desk for the four hundred people who cared how the argument went, and then they put him on a balcony and the whole world had to look at him whether it had any interest in him or not. I think about that more than I think about the eschatology.

The cafe is twelve minutes away. I know because the first week I began doing this I timed the route, and because there is no form of becoming ordinary that I cannot make strange by measuring it.

The woman behind the counter knows me. Not my name. She knows the coffee and that I need the socket near the wall. Today somebody is sitting there, so I take the table in the middle where there are two chairs and put my coat on the other one.

Then I take the coat off it.

That counts as nothing. I write nothing down.

I put my phone face down beside the coffee.

I turn it until the bottom edge is parallel with the table. The cup is not.

I keep both hands around the cup until the man speaks. I do not drink coffee with both hands.

The man at the next table asks what I am reading before I have opened the book.

He is somewhere past fifty. The wedding ring is tight and he turns it with the thumb of the same hand while he waits for my answer. His phone is face down. There is a second coffee on the table, untouched, placed opposite him.

Eschatology, I tell him.

He asks whether that is psychology.

I say it depends how badly the psychology has gone.

He laughs. It is a decent laugh. He asks what I do, and I tell him medicine, psychiatry if things continue according to plan.

That is enough. People do not need much permission when the permission has the right job attached to it.

His daughter is twenty three. She calls her mother and does not call him. When he asks why, she says there is no reason. He says there must be a reason. He has asked three times, and on the third she became angry, which he takes as evidence that the reason exists and is being concealed.

He tells me all of this in seven minutes.

I ask what happens when she does call.

Nothing happens, he says. We talk.

About what?

Her work. Her mother. The car. Things she should be doing and is not doing. She has a degree and no seriousness. He tells her this because her mother will not.

The second coffee remains opposite him. Nobody has touched it.

I tell him she may not be avoiding him. She may be avoiding the report she becomes in his hands. A person can love the reader and still stop bringing him pages.

He stops turning the ring.

It is good. I know when something is good before the other person says so. The face loses the small labour it was doing to hold the old arrangement in place. The eyes go down. The mouth opens once and decides against it. I have seen it in patients, in friends, in people beside me on public transport who never knew they had been understood.

He asks if I have children.

No.

A wife?

No.

Someone?

There is where the rule was meant to help.

I say there is a woman I speak to, I think of, I go to and belong to.

He waits.

I tell him the distinction is not settled, it is settled maybe? It is, it is at least for me. 

That should have been the end of it. There is nowhere to go with a sentence like that and I built it so there would not be.

Then I keep talking.

I tell him that in the Latin rite the two people marry each other. The priest is a witness. The consent is the sacrament, nobody else can supply it, and a marriage can happen in a hospital corridor with two strangers watching as long as the consent is real.

I tell him that in the Orthodox rite there are no vows. The priest crowns them. It is done to them and over them, and the crowns are martyrs' crowns, and the two of them do not make the marriage, they receive it.

He asks which one I am.

I say I was raised in neither, which is not what he asked.

I tell him the difference matters because one of them requires me to already be sure.

Then I stop, because I can hear it.

He does not say anything about her. He asks whether the crowns are heavy.

The woman he was waiting for comes in from the street. His sister, I learn from the way he says her name. She takes the untouched coffee without asking whose it is. He stands and buttons the wrong button of his coat, then fixes it while she is looking away.

Before they leave he asks my name.

I give it to him. He repeats it correctly and says his daughter reads too. He will tell her about the book.

Not what I said. The book.

Then they go.

I stay because the coffee is not finished.

The phone is at an angle. I straighten it before I lift the cup.

I hate coffee. I have hated it the whole time. I drink it because a table needs a reason and coffee is what the reason looks like, and because I cannot yet order a bottle of water at a counter and then sit down for an hour in front of it. What I want is one of the cold cans with the metal taste in them. Or water. Or tea, if the tea is not going to become an event.

My father drank it all day and slept fine. I have his hands and none of his tolerance for anything. He was a serious man with one bad habit and I have the same habit plus a laminated afternoon, so between the two of us he is clearly the better animal.

After it is finished I stay because the rule says to. Twenty six minutes. I do not read. This is harder than speaking to the man and I am irritated that it counts for less.

Across the room two students share a charger. One has been speaking for most of the twenty six minutes. The other has not looked at her phone once. I decide they are in love and that the quiet one knows first.

When they stand, they put on identical pharmacy coats.

Sisters, perhaps. Classmates. Two women who own the same charger. I had given them a whole life and they leave none of it behind.

I take out the small notebook where I record the attempts.

Outside without errand. Fifty four minutes.

Conversation initiated by other person.

Personal question answered partially.

No follow-up available.

I put a line through the last item because it sounds like an accusation and write not applicable.

The bookstore is on the next street. Going there violates the first rule, but I am already outside and a man cannot be expected to remain without an errand indefinitely. That would be loitering, which is a different pathology and not one I have.

The woman at the register has red around both eyes. I have seen her three Wednesdays now. She usually reads behind the counter and hides the book below the desk when the owner comes through. Today there is no book.

I ask whether the exam went badly.

What exam?

The one you were studying for.

She looks at me long enough that I begin preparing the shorter explanation.

I changed my contact lenses, she says. They are shit.

Oh.

She points at the book inside my coat.

You brought your own one again.

I take it out as if this had been my intention. The cover has made a red mark along my shirt where it pressed while I walked.

She tells me they have no Ratzinger and I tell her I was not looking for Ratzinger. She asks what I was looking for.

I have no answer that does not expose the entire afternoon.

Something short, I say.

She goes to the shelf and comes back with a poem. Not a collection. One poem printed on its own as a small book, the kind bought as a present by somebody who does not know the person.

I do not read poems. I have said that to people in a tone that made it sound like a position.

It is the one about the highwayman. He rides to the inn at night for the landlord's daughter. The soldiers come and tie her upright with a musket under her breast, and she waits with her finger on it until he is close enough down the road to hear, and then she fires it into herself so the shot will turn him back. He gets away. Then he learns what the shot was, and he turns around and rides back down the same road in daylight and they take him off the horse. At the end, on the right kind of winter night, he is still riding and she is still at the window.

I say it is very famous.

She says her mother used to say it out loud.

I buy it.

Outside, I have two books and no reason to continue. I stand between the bookstore and the pharmacy while people pass around me with the slight corrections bodies make around an object that should be moving.

There are people I could call. I am not going to pretend there are not.

One is a man who would come if I said something had happened. Nothing has happened.

One sends me work and I send work back. We have been doing this for two years and have never once asked the other where he is standing while he reads it.

Another is a doctor who likes me and has children and a Thursday morning.

One would say yes to coffee and spend the coffee asking what I am building now, and I would answer, and both of us would leave satisfied, which is not the thing I want and is too good a thing to punish for not being it.

I take out the phone. The contacts open under my thumb. I run from A to Z too quickly to read a name, then from Z to A more slowly.

I lock it.

I have two books, four free hours, and enough people to make a list. I stand there as though something has failed to arrive.

I am not lonely. I want that kept exactly as I have said it. I like walking without negotiating the direction. I like owning the silence around a page. I have abandoned gatherings to go home and been happy before the door closed behind me. There are people who experience an empty afternoon as an injury and I am not one of them.

At the next crossing I turn left. Home is right. I continue to the following corner, cross there, and come back along the opposite pavement. It adds four minutes. I do not put them in the notebook.

I walk home.

At the first corner the lining of my coat pulls across my hip and I feel the phone vibrate. It has not. I know that before my hand reaches it and my hand reaches it anyway. I take the Ratzinger out instead and carry him where everybody can see.

Future psychiatrist, defeated by polyester. The training is going very well.

The walk is good. There are clouds but no rain. A boy drops an orange and three people try to stop it with their feet and make it travel farther. The fourth catches it under a shoe and raises both hands as if the street has applauded. I laugh. The boy does not. His mother does, after checking the skin.

I tell the orange story once under my breath.

By the time I reach home I have changed the order twice. In the first version the mother is gone. In the second she returns. She was there.

At the door I say the last version from the beginning. Forty three seconds.

My room has stations.

I put the phone face down between the French log and the rota. There is no station for it.

There is blue tape on the floor where the camera goes and another piece where my feet go. The second mark was moved nine centimetres last week because the shelf made my head look displaced. Beside the camera is the one-take rule. If I cannot say the idea cold, I do not understand it. I have passed it twice and failed it enough times that the rule can still be trusted.

The psalm calendar is over the desk. Dates, not moods. If the date says lament, I do not replace it with praise because I happen to be having a good day. A detector tuned to pass is not a detector.

The ribbon is one day ahead. I read from it. I notice the date after amen and leave the ribbon there.

The French log is open beneath it. Every error goes into one class. Articles. Prepositions. Connective machinery. The record shows which part of my French is failing and whether it failed less this week.

There is a word target for the manuscript. A separate target for scripts. A rota. Savings. The safe income. The other income that remains a possibility as long as I do not turn it on.

That one has no title.

I do the work.

Nineteen pages of the book. Six lines in the notes. The cold take stops after two minutes when I cannot find the next sentence. The camera records another nineteen seconds of me looking at the shelf. I do not make a second one. The manuscript gets six hundred and forty three words. The French paragraph produces four errors, three of them at the joints where I knew they would be.

The phone remains face down. Each time one task ends there is one clean second before the next begins, and in that second I look at it and do not turn it over.

When the French is finished, the phone lies beside my right hand. I do not remember moving it.

I put it back between them and enter three errors into their classes. The fourth could be a preposition or connective machinery. I circle it in the paragraph and close the log.

Inside the bottom drawer is an envelope from the government with the fold worn white. The matter is finished. I need that stated. Nothing is currently being demanded from me. The dates have passed, the amounts were paid, the offices have stopped producing new offices inside themselves.

I still know where the envelope is without looking.

On the screen a project has reached the point where somebody sensible would ask what it could earn. I know what it could earn. I have modelled three versions and kept the smallest because the smallest fits inside the arrangements that no longer frighten me.

It is not money I fear. Money is the easiest part. A number arrives and remains the number. What happens around it is made of men behind glass asking for a paper that exists only in the office that sent me to them. It is made of proving that a thing happened to somebody whose system says it did not. It is being correct in a chair that does not care.

I can work a Sunday. A Sunday enters the account cleanly.

So I work Sundays.

There is an apartment saved in the browser. Forty seven square metres. Third floor. The kitchen catches afternoon light and the bedroom does not face the street. I have never visited it. It may already belong to somebody else. That does not matter. I use it for measurements.

The main room can take the desk if the shelf moves beside the door. The camera needs two metres from the wall. Books take the long side. The table folds. Two chairs do not.

I have tried.

In the first version you sit beside the desk while I write. You are not watching me. I want that clear. You have your own thing open, something I cannot see, and every forty minutes one of us says a sentence that does not require the other to stop being a person.

In the second version I am reading and you are moving through the room. You lift the keys from the table and say where you are going. I nod without looking up. Two lines later I find that I have read the same line three times.

In the third we are doing nothing together. This is the hardest one to render. The program keeps asking for furniture.

I put a second chair beside mine.

Then I notice what I have done.

Every object in the room belongs to my life. My books. My camera. My desk. My calendar. My work. You have a chair.

I move it toward the window. That makes you decorative.

I turn it toward me. That makes you an audience.

I place it across from the table. That makes every evening a conversation, and even I would leave that house.

I do not know what you would bring into the room.

Not the objects. I could ask about objects. What part of the day would be yours. What you would protect from me. Whether you want somebody doing twelve things near you, or whether the twelve things are the exact life you are trying not to enter. Whether you want travel, noise, friends arriving without notice, one finished profession, a man whose Saturday does not become money because the future frightened him on Wednesday.

In one rendering the second chair is absent. The room is easier to solve.

I do not save that rendering.

I close the apartment. I open it.

The second chair is where I left it.

I take off one shoe. Before I take off the other one I start crying.

There is no good sentence for this. I cried with one shoe on. My nose closed and I made the stupid swallowing sound people make when they are trying to keep grief above the throat, as though the mouth is the part that would make it real. I sat on the edge of the bed with the tied shoe still on the left foot and the loose one on the floor and cried until the position hurt my hip.

Every time I come here I say this is the last time. I say it into my shirt. Please let this be the last time I sit with one shoe on and make an afternoon into this, the last time I know exactly what I am doing while I do it anyway.

If I saw another man like this I would flip. I would tell him stand up. Have some fucking shame. The apartment is forty seven square metres and imaginary. The chair is for somebody who has never seen it. I know this. I enter the same pathetic place like it has been kept for me.

The pressure starts behind the breastbone. Oil moving like water, that is what it feels like, something thick building where breath is supposed to go and the chest pressing back because there is nowhere left to put it. Pneumothorax, the mind says, which is stupid, I know what a pneumothorax is, I know air and pleura and the clean black edge on the film, and knowing does not move this one. It has no edge. It does not leave.

I have felt it before. I hate that more than the pain.

The body has been here before me. It tightens in the old places and waits for the rest of the room to return.

Nobody knows that I do this.

Not my mother. Not the men whose problems I can tell back to them in a form they can carry.

Once every few months I tell somebody I had a difficult night. By then it is a case summary. The crying has been converted into an observation and the observation is calm enough to share. People say I am self-aware.

I am.

It has done nothing about the shoe.

When it stops, I wash my face. The red goes before the swelling. I put the second shoe back on because walking through the room with one shoe feels worse than crying did.

The notebook is still inside my coat.

Outside without errand.

One hour forty eight.

Two conversations.

One interpretation useful.

One interpretation wrong.

Personal disclosure partial.

I could write crying there. The category would be exposure, because the feeling occurred and nothing terrible followed. That is true and disgusting.

I leave the line blank.

I take the phone from between the French log and the rota and open the recorder.

I record the orange first. The mother stays. Forty three seconds.

Then I begin another voice note.

I start by saying I am in love. It takes four seconds and is the easiest thing I will say all day.

Then I keep going, because it turns out that was the door.

I say I have spoken a name into an empty room to find out what it does to the room.

I say I have imagined marriage. Not the day. I have not spent money on flowers in my head. I have imagined the Wednesday after, and the one after that, and another person in a room while I am reading, and me learning not to make a study out of the way she sits. I say the apartment is forty seven square metres and I have never seen it and I know where the second chair goes, which I do not, but it is close enough to true at nine at night.

Then I reach the other part, and I do not say it.

I say there are circumstances. I say it is the way these things are arranged. I say it is nobody's fault and simply how it works, which is a sentence with no subject anywhere in it, and I leave it standing there.

The thing I mean has a name and a year and a signature under it. There is an office where the same qualifications get somebody else through in an afternoon because of where he happened to be born, and where the identical afternoon takes four years of my sanity and a committee that may not sign. I know the numbers. I have read the numbers at two in the morning more often than I have read anything else this year.

I say it is like waiting for a season to turn.

Then I run.

I go to the fifth canto, the two of them in the wind, held together forever as the punishment. Francesca says they were reading a book, and they got as far as the kiss, and that day they read no further. She blames the book. She calls it the go-between. There have been books.

I go to the wall of fire he has to cross to reach the other side and will not. He stands in front of it and refuses. The old man with him says her name is on the other side. Then he goes in. He does not go in because it is the correct thing to do. He goes in because somebody said her name.

I go to the man in my coat, who, when they finally gave him the whole church and put him on the balcony, did not write first about doctrine. He wrote about eros. He said the two loves are not enemies, that wanting somebody is where the whole thing starts, and that it has to be turned into a gift or it will eat itself. He managed that at seventy eight with the world watching. I am doing this into a phone with the door shut.

I go to Girard, which is where I am least safe. He says wanting is borrowed. A person does not want the thing, he wants it because somebody wanted it first and he picked up desire off them the way he picks up an accent. I have used that on people for two years. I could use it on this in an afternoon and I would be good at it. I am not going to. I would rather want badly and not know why than take it apart and be right.

I go to the book in the middle of the Bible where nobody mentions God once, and it is two people describing each other's bodies, and they argued about whether to keep it and they kept it, then Akiva said the whole world was not worth the day it was given. They decided the most it could mean was God. They never decided it meant less than what it says. It says love is as strong as death and that many waters cannot quench it, and it is talking about two people in a bed, and it does not rank that below anything else in the book.

The note is eleven minutes long.

I listen to the first twelve seconds. That part is fine. I sound like somebody in love who does not mind who hears it.

Then I listen to the rest.

I have explained how I love a woman using a Florentine, a German, a Frenchman and a rabbi. Four men and a wall of fire, and not one plain sentence with nobody in it but her.

And at the place where the true thing was, the one with the name and the year on it, I said weather.

I delete it.

The orange remains in the recorder. Forty three seconds. The mother stays.

At six the rota asks who can take Saturday.

Saturday is worth enough that I open the apartment. I do not move the chair. I calculate what the shift does to the safe number and then calculate it a second time because the first answer is smaller than the feeling attached to it.

The shift does not buy a room. It buys part of a month in the room on the screen. The second chair remains exactly where I put it.

I write available.

The rota turns green.

I turn the phone face down.

I put it between the French log and the rota.

There is no station for it.
