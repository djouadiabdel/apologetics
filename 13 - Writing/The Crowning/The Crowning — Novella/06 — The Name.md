# VI. The Name

The fifth is where they say your name.

Not call it. Say it.

The floor is almost empty. The people on it keep far enough apart that no two shadows touch. They could shout without being overheard, but nobody raises a voice. The loud kind burns itself out. This kind is fed daily, kept warm, and turned in the night. A person can carry it for forty years, call it dignity, and be buried in it.

We passed the first man and a name was spoken somewhere behind him.

His shoulders moved. Nothing else did.

The name came again. He closed his eyes until the sound was finished and then opened them. The skin around his mouth had gone pale. He had refused so often that the refusal no longer required a decision.

At the far wall a woman had become difficult to see. The outline of her dress was sharp, but the hands inside its sleeves were thin as smoke. Her name was said and for one instant the hands returned. She tucked them under her arms and waited for them to disappear again.

Near the stairs, another name was spoken.

A man carrying his shoes stopped. He had worn through the soles and tied them together by their laces. The name was said only once.

Here, he answered.

It was not a brave answer. The word came out tired and slightly irritated, the response of somebody interrupted while travelling. Nothing opened above him. No light struck. He remained a man holding ruined shoes.

Then his feet began to bleed.

They had been grey when he entered. Colour travelled into them from the ankles and found every cut the stone had made. He sat down hard, dropped the shoes, and put both hands around one foot.

He shouted that answering had injured him.

The voice said his name again.

He answered with the shout still in his mouth.

The other foot began bleeding. He rocked on the stair, swearing, alive in every place the floor touched him.

Nobody congratulated him. The gate above the stairs opened and waited while he gathered the shoes. He stood badly, placed one foot down, and felt all of it.

He went through.

You watched until the gate closed. I explained that pain was not evidence of error, a principle I would later develop more fully when I reached the fire.

The old man looked at me as though the principle had arrived from somewhere else.

Nobody is chained on that floor. Nobody is prevented from answering.

An address can be refused. If it could not be, none of this would be love. It would be machinery and you would be a lever inside it. Everything here rests on that permission, and this is the floor where the permission is used.[^c6-r1]

I went back to the first man after his name had been said twice. He was still facing forward with the colour slowly returning around his mouth.

I told him the correct response was simple.

He said simplicity was not evidence.

I said an answer did not certify the speaker. It only acknowledged that the address had arrived.

He asked why acknowledgement was owed.

I had three answers. I gave the shortest. Because the one speaking had held him in existence long enough to refuse.

He said that was coercion dressed as gratitude.

The old man had followed us into the room. He stood beside you, listening. I wanted him to hear how quickly I could separate freedom from obligation.

I asked the seated man whether I might answer for him.

He laughed. It was the first loud sound on the floor.

You may answer anything you like, he said.

I stood behind him and spoke his name into the place where the voice had been. I said yes in the tone I believed he would have used before refusal became his character.

Nothing happened.

The man's shoulders did not return. No colour entered him. My answer occupied only my own mouth.

The old man asked what I had expected.

I said substitution was an established mechanism.

For what?

I began to answer and found that every example I possessed involved somebody offering himself, not somebody volunteering the mouth of the person who had refused.

The seated man thanked me for demonstrating his point.

I moved away before the old man could reply.

Near the far wall, the woman with the empty sleeves was trying to lift one hand toward the place from which her name had come. The hand appeared to the wrist, vanished, appeared again. You moved nearer but did not touch her. When she saw you, she tucked the sleeve under her arm.

Something was said then that I could not hear. You lowered your head. The old man did the same.

I waited for you to answer.

You did not.

I assumed the address had not been yours.

At the window I had asked the old man what held me in existence, since everything in me seemed destructible and I had been reduced to whatever could walk these stairs.

I expected him to say the soul. Everybody says the soul. I had the objection ready.

He said there was no piece of me that could not be destroyed. I had been made from nothing and possessed nothing that argued against returning to it. I remained because I was being addressed and the one addressing me did not stop. That was the whole mechanism. There was no other.[^c6-r2]

I asked whether that meant the address could never fail.

He looked past me toward you.

That was the question he refused.

Then my name was said.

I heard it. It was not far away or muffled. It was mine, all of it, the whole name, pronounced the way it sounds when somebody has known it longer than you have.

My mouth opened enough for the cold to touch my teeth.

The sound entered the body in order. The skin between my shoulders tightened first. Then the throat. Then the hands, each finger drawing inward as though around a railing. My knees unlocked.

I had believed hearing one's true name would feel like recognition. It felt like being located.

I did not answer.

Answering would have meant that I had not controlled the exchange. I also wanted to hear the name again, and I thought that if I answered the first call there might not be a second.[^c6-g1]

There was a second.

This time you turned fully toward the sound.

I watched you turn. My mouth was open. I had only to—

My teeth came down on the inside of one cheek and stayed there. My tongue went to the roof of my mouth and pressed and let go and pressed again.

But if I answered now, before it finished, I would never know whether there had been a third.

The last sound of my name passed me. I let it. I stood there with the answer ready and let it go past.

There was not a third.

I waited longer.

The old man went toward the stairs. You followed him. I remained where the second call had found me, keeping the same position so that nothing in the arrangement could suggest I had left.

I cleared my throat.

The sound was mine and therefore proved nothing.

I moved one step toward the place from which the name had come. No response. One step back. No response.

I said yes too quietly for you to hear.

Nothing changed.

I said it louder. The word travelled across the floor and several attenuated people looked toward me. It did not attach itself to the name because the name was no longer being offered.

The seated man I had tried to answer for smiled.

Simple, he said.

I asked the old man to say my name once, only to establish whether the mechanism depended on the speaker or the address.

He stood at the head of the stairs with you and did not turn.

I knew then that the silence was not a tactic for producing my answer. If it had been, the third call would have been the efficient choice. The absence of coercion was not a gentleness added to the mechanism. It was the mechanism, even when gentleness allowed me to disappear.

When I crossed the floor to join you, my footsteps sounded farther away than my body felt. I looked down twice to confirm that the legs were still there.

The silence after it was exact. It did not punish or threaten. Nothing approached to collect an answer. The hands of the woman beside the wall disappeared completely and her empty sleeves remained crossed over one another.

The fifth gate did not tear the name off me. It ceased using it. The door opened, and there was a great deal more room inside me than before. It did not feel like loss at first. It felt like quiet. You have to have lived in a quiet like that to know the difference.

At the bottom of the next flight you stopped.

You lifted your crown with both hands. Nothing resisted you. Before it cleared your head your elbows lowered once, and for a moment you held it there without wearing it and without taking it off. Then you raised it the remaining distance, set it against the wall carefully, as though it could still be damaged, and walked on without explaining and without looking back.

I noted when you did it. Fifth flight. Bottom stair. Both hands. No hesitation I could measure.

The crown remained against the wall as we descended. At the turn I could still see its upper edge. One more step and it would be out of sight.

I went back.

I told myself an abandoned thing should not be left where anybody might take it. I would carry it only until you chose a safer place. This was stewardship, not possession, and the distinction was obvious because I intended to return it.

When I reached for it, the old man was standing on the stair above.

He did not block me. He did not move his hands from behind his back. He looked from my hand to the crown with the same patience he had given my arguments.

I asked whether leaving a sacred thing against a wall was proper.

He asked who had called it sacred.

I said that was apparent from what it was.

Behind him, you continued down.

The crown against the wall had already gone cold. I could feel the absence of warmth before touching it.

I left it there and hurried after you, carrying the satisfaction of having chosen correctly.

You had stopped at the next turn. When I reached you, you put your hand around two of my fingers and started down. You kept them until the landing and then took your hand back. You did not look behind us at the crown.

Then I put one hand up to mine.

It was warm. It is always warm. It was the only thing left down there still behaving as though I were somebody.[^c6-g2]

## Theological material — Ratzinger

Full context: [[The Crowning — Ratzinger Sourcebook#V. The Immortality of the Soul and the Resurrection of the Dead — printed pp. 104–162]].

**Ecclesial idea:** Eternal life is dialogical. The person persists because God calls him by name, not because he possesses a private indestructible core. But the address preserves freedom: no substitute can speak another person's answer. This supports the floor's mechanism and also limits it—the woman is not the divine voice that keeps him alive.

**Short seed:** “Lord Jesus, receive my spirit.” — Acts 7:59, quoted by Ratzinger on printed p. 125.

**Do not turn it into:** Her refusal being a refusal of God, or his answer entitling him to hers. Two persons may both be addressed without being assigned to each other.

**Try writing from:** Hide everything from the first call through *There was not a third.* Say it aloud from memory, keeping attention in teeth, throat, knees, and hands. Let his calculation interrupt the body's almost-answer. Explain nothing until the call has passed.

## Desire material — Girard

Full context: [[The Crowning — Girard Sourcebook#1. Triangular desire]] and [[The Crowning — Girard Sourcebook#2. The model becomes the obstacle]].

**Girardian pressure:** The white-cassocked future theologian is a human mediator constructed out of prestige, patience, office, and the fantasy of finally being heard. The narrator would obey this image because it appears to possess the being he lacks. The Lamb later inhabits the shape without validating that fantasy.

**Short seed:** “Imitate me; and as rival, do not imitate me.” — *Things Hidden*, printed p. 291.

**Do not turn it into:** Christ being merely the narrator's projection. The imagined shape is his; the One who answers through and exceeds it is genuinely other.

**Try writing from:** At the window, show one gesture by which the narrator unconsciously copies the old man's posture. Do not let him notice the imitation until much later, if ever.

## Sentence footnotes — Ratzinger + Girard

[^c6-r1]: **Ratzinger — freedom belongs inside love, not beside it as a concession.** This sentence governs both the divine address and the eventual human question. Be careful, however: the woman's refusal and God's address are analogous only at the level of freedom; she is not cast as God.

[^c6-r2]: **Ratzinger — dialogical immortality.** Printed pp. 157–160 argue that the creature lives because God calls him by name and relation endures. This is one of the book's most exact theological embodiments. Let the old man state it, but keep the narrator's prepared objection nearby so the scene remains conflict rather than catechism.

[^c6-g1]: **Girard — obstruction intensifies desire.** The possible end of the calling makes the second call more desirable than the answer itself. He begins converting address into scarcity, then scarcity into value. The bodily almost-answer must precede this excellent diagnosis.

[^c6-g2]: **Girard — the crown now supplies being.** Having declined the uncontrolled address, he touches a self-made object that behaves “as though” he is somebody. This is metaphysical desire condensed into paper physics: he prefers an identity warmed by himself to one received without control.
