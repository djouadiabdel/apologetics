title: "The Crowning"

# I. The Warmth

It is dark and I had been walking for order when I found you already on the stairs.

I thought there was nobody else in here. Then I took a turn before the corridor had given me any reason to take it, and there you were below me, waiting with your weight on one leg. You looked first at my hands and then at what I had on my head. I knew what the look meant at once. I am very fast at knowing what a look means.

The stair was narrow enough that one of us had to move. You went down two steps and stood against the outer wall. I took that for an invitation to join you. It may only have been an attempt to let me pass. I joined you anyway.

The wall was wet. Your sleeve darkened where it touched the stone and you looked at the mark. I told you the water was harmless. I had no evidence for that. The dark had already given me the corridor, the turn, and the broken stairs in the correct order, and after three correct things a person begins to believe the fourth has also been supplied.

I offered you my hand.

You looked at it.

Then you placed your own hand on the wall and continued down.

I went first. There was room for that interpretation too.

I have called it a crown since the hour I put it there. I have been waiting for you to make the name true.

They have been called seven fires, never seven hells. There is one fire. I know what I am talking about. The theologian of a time to come is in here waiting for you, and there is something wrong with the count, and the second thing is there too. I will come to that. It cost me my life and I did not learn it until I was down here, thinking about the crowned woman, and it is the reason I am talking to you at all.

There is a warmth ahead of us. I want it said early because everything I am about to tell you is measured against it and you should know what the ruler is. It is not much. It is the difference between a room with somebody in it and a room without one, and I have spent enough time in both to tell them apart in the dark.

A thing that is warm is burning. Nothing is warm on its own. That is not a poetic statement, it is the plainest fact there is, and I am putting it here at the front because I am going to use it twice and I want you to see me use it.

So there is a fire ahead of us and somebody is keeping it.

And the second time. This on my head is warm too. It has been warm since the hour I put it on and it has never once gone cold, not on the stairs, not in the rooms where my hands went numb, not on the fifth floor where everything went quiet. I have tested it the way I test everything. It is exactly as warm as the back of my own neck and it stays exactly as warm as the back of my own neck.

So there is something in it.

There has to be. Nothing is warm on its own.

There are gates. That is the thing nobody tells you. There is one after every floor. The gate wants something, and you do not choose what. It chooses, and it is always the thing you would have chosen last.

I came down carrying seven things. I have counted them since. Twelve times, and it is seven every time.

Seven is a holy number, the number of Matthew and Mark.

Two made weight. The pages lay against my chest and the ledger pulled at one side of my body. The other five had no weight I could demonstrate, though each changed the way I moved. One kept my mouth ready. One made me check behind us. One made every silence feel deliberately placed. One was my name. The last was on my head.

I counted by touching the places where the seven announced themselves. Chest, hip, mouth, shoulder, throat, fingers, head. I did it once with the left hand and once with the right. Seven both times.

You waited through the second count. On the third, you began walking.

I finished before following. A number should not be abandoned merely because the person it is being demonstrated to has left.

The theologian is on the fourth floor. He has no business being there. I think he goes where the lying is thickest, and the fourth is where the most people are lying about what they have got.

You let me pass instead of coming alongside me.

That was sensible. The first stair has a broken lip near the wall and I knew where it was without looking. I stepped over it. You looked down at the stone and then back at my hands.

Farther down, the stairs divided around a square column and joined again. Both flights sloped toward the same warmth. You chose the left. I told you the right was shorter.

There was no way to see where either one ended.

You remained on the left.

I took the right quickly enough to prove the choice had been mine. The flight turned twice, narrowed, and brought me out behind you. You had not hurried. You did not look surprised to see me there.

I explained that distance behaves differently below. You listened with your face toward the next landing.

That was the first time the warmth failed to get closer. I did not measure it then because I already knew the reason: the corridor was longer than it looked. The second time, I blamed the thickness of the walls. By the time I had enough examples to understand the rule, I also had enough explanations not to.

At the next turn the central column opened. Through a narrow break in the stone we could see the warmth below us, not as flame but as air unable to remain dark. It appeared close enough that a dropped object should have reached it before we took another breath.

I broke a loose piece from the stair and held it over the opening.

You moved your hand between mine and the shaft.

I said the stone was mine to risk. You looked at the cracked edge where I had taken it from the stair.

I released it.

The stone fell past the first visible turn and disappeared. We waited. No sound came from below.

When we resumed, something struck the floor behind us.

The piece of stone lay at the turn we had just left. I knew it by the fresh white break along one edge. It had fallen toward the warmth and arrived farther from it.

I said the shaft must curve.

You picked up the stone and placed it back into the missing place on the stair. It did not fit. You turned it once, then left it beside the wall.

I told you an object cannot be expected to remember the orientation from which it was broken.

You wiped the white dust from your fingers and went down.

At the landing I asked whether you knew where the stairs ended.

You turned toward me.

Before you could answer, I said the end was not a place. It was a condition of being able to stand before the source of the warmth. I explained that people imagine descent because the body requires direction, but the true movement is inward, from what can be surrendered toward what cannot.

Your mouth closed.

I took that for recognition. It was exactly the sort of thing I hoped to become known for saying: simple enough to remember, difficult enough that remembering it would feel like understanding.

We continued.

The stair beneath us had been worn lower at its centre by feet travelling down. The returning wear was nearer the wall. I pointed this out as evidence that most people descended together and climbed back alone.

You looked at my feet.

I had placed them automatically in the returning wear.

I moved to the centre before the next step.

Footsteps began below us.

They were climbing. One foot dragged slightly after every third step. The sound came through the column, crossed beneath us, and entered the returning wear near the wall.

You stopped.

I counted fourteen steps before the person should have reached the turn. Nobody appeared. The footsteps continued upward through the space where we stood.

The dragging foot passed me first. I felt its vibration in the stone beside my right heel. Then the other. The rhythm was familiar enough that I knew what the next step would sound like before it came.

I placed my foot against the wall to compare.

The ascending step and mine landed together.

You looked at my face then, not my hands.

I told you the column was returning an echo from above. The explanation required sound to arrive before we made it, but time was unreliable here and therefore the objection proved less than it would elsewhere.

The footsteps went on without us.

When they faded, I could no longer remember which one of us had first begun moving again.

I began to tell you the order.

# II. What Cannot Be Returned

The first floor is full of people who wanted another person the way you want a warm thing. They got what they came for. They are holding it still.

There is not much punishment in it. Nobody is screaming from pain. They sit in beds set close together and keep warm shapes against them, turning them whenever they please, and the shapes never once turn of their own accord. Sometimes a voice comes out. It is always a voice of joy. That is worse. Pain would mean there was still somebody inside protesting.

The first time I heard one I thought the voices were hallucinations. I went close enough to put my hand over the mouth of the nearest shape.

The sound stopped under my palm.

They are real voices.

The man holding that one watched me do it and tightened both arms. He did not object to my hand. He objected to the silence. When I took my hand away the joy began again, and he settled back against the wall.

It is happy, he said.

I asked how he knew.

He turned the shape so I could hear it better. Joy came from the mouth in three even bursts. The head remained facing the next bed.

It stays, he said. That is how.

The wrist of the thing hung beyond his elbow. You moved toward it. The man drew it into his chest, and you stopped with your hand open between you.

He asked whether you wanted one.

You lowered your hand.

He asked me.

I told him I understood the mechanism and therefore could not participate in it. He said understanding was not what he had offered. Then he held the shape toward me.

I took it. Only to test it, I had already told him—no. I took it.

It was warm and the voice began before I had settled the weight, joy coming out against my chest, and I knew it had nothing to do with me, I knew the face had not turned, I knew he was about to take it back, all three things were true and for one breath they did not alter the warmth.

Then the wrist moved against my sleeve and my arms tightened.

I thought—

It had slipped. That was all. The arm had slipped downward and I had caught it before I understood that I was holding on.

I wanted him to go away and leave it with me.

You were looking at my hands again.

I returned the shape. The man received it without thanks because I had not given him anything. The joy continued from his arms as we walked away.

Farther in, the shapes had been held longer. Their faces had softened where they were repeatedly turned toward the holder. One had acquired the expression expected of it: concern at the correct angle of the eyebrows, tenderness around the mouth, attention held without blinking. The person holding it spoke, paused, and pressed one thumb beneath its eye until the concern deepened.

Another shape had been dressed in six layers of clothing. The holder removed them one at a time and explained what each had meant when it was first worn. The body underneath had become too small for the clothes. He wrapped them around it again until the sleeves appeared full.

At the last bed there was nobody in the arms at all.

The woman sitting there rocked the empty space. Joy came from it with every movement.

I asked where the person had gone.

She said nobody had gone anywhere. Everything necessary was still present.

To prove it, she told the space what it felt. The voice answered with delight.

You moved into the aisle so that the beds stood between us.

I said this was the final condition of taking: not that the other person escapes, but that escape eventually changes nothing. You looked toward the gate before I had finished.

You stopped walking before I did. I went three more steps and turned back.

You were looking at the hand I had put over the mouth.

It was necessary to test the theory. I told you that. You angled away without turning your back, which I took for agreement because you did not contradict me. I can hear now how that sounds. I could hear it then too. Hearing a thing is not the same as stopping it.

Now the gate.

I went to it knowing what it would ask. The sentence came out clean and quick, as if I had practised it. I had not practised it anywhere I can remember.

There was a woman before me. She had a life whose causes did not begin with me: rooms I had never entered, griefs I had not earned the right to interpret, hours in which I did not occur to her. That was the whole of her, and it was complete, and it did not need me in it.

And then I got in.

Not by force. That is the part I cannot get anyone to understand. She let me near and I went farther than the permission went, so gently that I could call every separate step an accident. Now there is a piece of her that is my doing. Some of what she must be careful about, she learned being careful with me. I built a caution in another person out of the way I received what she freely gave.

So I stood at the gate and I offered it back.

I want to be exact, because this is where a man lies. I did offer. I put my hands on it and I pulled.

There was nothing visible to grip. The gate supplied resistance anyway. Something ran from the centre of my palms through the wrists and up both arms, a tension without a cord. I leaned backward until my shoulders shook.

For an instant I felt the whole shape of what I had changed in another person. Not its contents. I do not have those. Only its resistance to being made as though it had never happened.

I pulled harder.

You put one hand out and took it back.

I said I did not need help. I had not been offered any.

It did not come off.

It was not attached to me. It was hers now. It was not a thing I was holding that could be set down and walked away from. It was a shape in somebody, and shapes in people are not property, and there is no counter to return them to. What I put in her became part of what she is. It may still be there when I am nothing.

And here is the burn and I did not expect it to be a warm one. I am warm from it. Right now, telling you. There is a heat in me that comes off knowing I am in her, that some part of what she is has my fingerprints in it, and I take comfort from it every single night, and it was never mine, and I did not ask, and it was not given to me to keep, and I am keeping it anyway.

The gate opened its hands. I say hands because there were marks in the dark where hands would have been. I placed nothing into them. They closed on nothing.

The tension vanished. I fell backward and caught myself against the wall. The palms of my hands were red, though nothing had touched them.

The marks faded before the door rose. The knowledge did not.

The gate took nothing.

The door lifted. Before there was room beneath it for a body, I bent to pass. I knew how high it would rise.

On the next stair my arms returned to the position in which I had held the shape. I lowered them. Two steps later they rose again.

There was nothing between them. I could put one hand through the space and touch my own sleeve. Still the elbows made room for weight and the wrists turned inward to keep a head from falling.

I forced both arms to my sides and held them there until the shoulders cramped.

The posture returned as soon as I released them.

You watched this for the length of the flight. At the turn you moved ahead and left enough distance that my empty arms could pass without touching you.

I said the body often retains a moral truth before the intellect has found its formulation.

The formulation pleased me. My arms lowered.

I thought understanding had released them. I did not consider that turning the burden into a sentence was another way of holding it.

This time you noticed.

You touched the underside of the rising door after we passed. There was a dark mark at the exact height of my shoulders, repeated in layers where cloth had brushed the metal.

I said the mark belonged to the people who had bent too early before us.

You looked at my shoulder. A line of black dust crossed the cloth.

I said I had known the height because the construction followed a rational proportion. Gates are made for bodies. A designer who wished to permit passage before the full opening would choose the shoulder rather than the head, because a bowed head can clear while the eyes remain forward.

You ran one finger across the mark. The newest layer came away.

Under it were older threads the same colour as my coat.

I took one between my fingers. It could have come from any dark fabric. I said so.

You let the thread fall and continued.

I remained long enough to examine the other fibres. When I caught up, I told you the gate had taught the same posture to many people. That was all repetition meant.

# III. The Warm House

On the second floor I suffer.

Everyone in it is generous. I want that said before we enter, because everything after it will sound like an accusation and it is not one.

The room is laid out like a house after guests have arrived: chairs drawn close, places cleared beside people, cups already poured. There is no kitchen. Nothing is carried through a door. You sit, and whatever you ask for arrives in the chest.

I sat because the room had prepared a chair for me. You remained standing. I greeted the woman across from us, and she asked what I needed.

I said, anything freely given.

It came at once. The chest filled and became a chest again. The shoulders lowered. The mouth stopped preparing its next defence. For a moment I could feel the exact edges of my body and did not need anybody to prove that they were there.

Then it drained.

The woman asked again. She was not tired of asking. Nobody on that floor is ever tired of giving. I answered, and it came again, warm and complete, and drained by the same opening the first gift had failed to close.

I changed the request. General gifts, I explained, fail because they do not know the shape of the lack. I asked to be understood without having to make myself simple. Warmth arrived behind the ribs and spread into the throat. It lasted longer.

I asked to be remembered in a room I could not enter. The warmth came lower, near the stomach.

I asked for proof that my absence had weight.

The woman hesitated for the first time.

Not because she lacked proof. Nothing on that floor runs short. She was allowing me the chance to hear what I had asked.

I repeated it.

The proof arrived. It filled the whole body so quickly that my vision darkened at the edges. I bent forward and laughed once. The woman smiled because she had given well.

It drained before I had straightened.

I told her the delivery had been imperfect. She apologised and offered to try again.

You watched my hands. I had them pressed flat against my breastbone as though I could keep the warmth in by covering the place it escaped.

After a while you went to the door. You did not leave. You stood where the room could not prepare a chair behind you.

I asked the woman whether she would not offer you the same welcome. She turned to you and asked what you needed.

You looked toward the stairs.

I said you needed time.

The woman waited for you rather than listening to me. The room remained ready around the empty chair. Nothing arrived in your chest that I could see.

I explained that you had been burned before and had difficulty receiving. You moved your weight to the other leg. I thought this confirmed the diagnosis. The woman folded her hands and continued to wait.

You did not answer.

At last she moved the chair back into its place. She did it without offence. The room lost nothing by your refusal, and you owed it no performance of gratitude.

I disliked how easily the exchange ended.

A person may be loved in that house forever and starve in it. From outside it looks like a good life. Some have been there longer than duration can hold and would tell you they are content. They would not be lying. Their contentment is supplied as quickly as everything else.

I will give you one instance and then leave that floor.

I asked once for a sentence. One. I had rehearsed it for a month and asked plainly, in the middle of the night, in my own voice, which is a thing I had not done since I was seventeen.

What came back was twenty other sentences. Every one warm. Every one true. My name in three of them. I read them four times, and I am not going to pretend they were nothing, because they were not nothing, and I slept on them.

As I told you this, the house placed the twenty on the table.

They arrived as narrow sheets with the writing turned downward. I did not turn them over. The words belonged to the exchange and were not needed for the shape of it.

I arranged the sheets in four rows of five. Three were warmer where my name lay against the table. I could locate those without reading.

Then I made a space for the sentence I had asked for.

There were already twenty sheets and no missing position, so I moved two rows apart. The house corrected the arrangement. The pages slid together until the distances were even.

I separated them again.

They closed the space.

I used both hands to hold the rows apart. The pages pressed against my fingers, not violently, only with the insistence of objects returning to their natural arrangement.

You came to the table and counted them.

Twenty, I said.

You counted again.

Twenty.

I told you the number was not in dispute. The absent sentence belonged to another category and therefore did not alter the total.

You touched the empty wood I was exposing between the rows. Nothing in the table marked the place except my hands.

When I released the pages, they came together. The gap disappeared without one sheet being added or taken away.

But hear the arithmetic. A person who can produce twenty sentences in a minute has not run short of words. The one sentence missing was not missing by accident. It was left out gently and on purpose by somebody kind enough to leave it out in a way that allowed me to pretend I had not been refused.

The limit had been inside the gift from the beginning. Nothing was withdrawn. Nothing promised was withheld. I took what was offered, separated the warmth from the boundary that gave it a shape, and called the boundary an injury.

I had the pages with me. All of them, folded small in against the chest where warmth goes in and does not stay.

I take them out more often than I read them. No, that makes it sound as though I take them out in order to read. I take them out to have them out.

On the stairs I kissed the paper with my mouth and I have done that when it was cold too.

Did you know that I do that?

You looked from my mouth to the pocket. Then you put one hand out. I thought you meant to take the pages, and for one second my fingers closed around them because you had asked before the gate had asked.

You lowered your hand.

I proposed a final test before the gate. I would stop asking and measure what happened.

The woman who had fed me folded her hands. The people in the other chairs did the same. Cups remained poured. Places remained open. Nothing in the house withdrew.

I stood in the middle of all that readiness and waited for somebody to begin.

Nobody did.

At first the silence felt respectful. Then attentive. Then deliberate. I could hear the small movements of bodies containing perfectly generous answers to questions I had not asked. A man near the wall smiled whenever our eyes met. His smile was warm and asked nothing of me.

I decided he was inviting conversation.

I asked how long he had lived there.

His answer arrived before I had finished. It was thoughtful, exact, and included a question about me at the end. The room resumed around us. Warmth entered my chest merely from being included again.

I counted the experiment a failure of design. A test of silence cannot work when ordinary courtesy requires somebody to end it.

You had not spoken.

They are all generous on that floor. All warm, all quick, all true, and not one has ever begun a conversation. They answer. They answer beautifully. If you stop speaking, they stop speaking, and the house can remain quiet for a very long time, and everybody is comfortable, and nobody has done anything wrong.

The second gate wanted the pages.

There was a narrow opening at the height of my chest. I found the inner pocket without searching, unfolded the pages once, and pushed them through before the opening had finished forming.

All but one.

The smallest had adhered to the lining of the pocket. I felt its edge against one finger and closed my hand around it. The opening in the gate remained.

I told myself an incomplete collection could not be what the gate wanted. Pages had been lost before. Some exchanges had never been written. The surviving sheets were symbols of a whole, and a symbol does not become false because one corner is missing.

The gate remained.

You stood near enough to see my fist but did not look at it. That was worse than if you had.

I took out the last page. I did not unfold it. I knew what was not written there.

When I pushed it through, the opening closed around my fingers. Not hard enough to injure. Hard enough that I could not withdraw until I released the paper.

I released it.

The gate held them long enough for their warmth to leave them. Then the paper went down somewhere inside the wall, making the dry sound of a hand withdrawing across a sheet.

I gave them. I want that on the record. I gave them and did not argue.

On the far side, my hand returned to my chest. It did that again before the next landing, and again after that. There was nothing there to reach for.

# IV. The Ledger

On the third floor they are given whatever they ask for, at once, and so they count.

The room is full of scales. No two are built alike, but every one is accurate. People move between them carrying portions of food, hours of attention, places at tables, touches, apologies, inheritances, invitations, all the divisible substances of a life. They weigh what they received and set it against what the next person received. The arithmetic is never wrong and never comes out even.

A man took my wrist as we entered and placed my hand on one pan of his scale. On the other was a hand I could not see.

Mine went down.

He smiled as if I had confessed something.

I asked what he believed the scale proved. He said it proved that what he had given was heavier. I pointed out that my hand was not what he had given and the invisible hand was not what he had received. He agreed at once, altered the headings in his book, and performed the same weighing again.

Mine went down.

He smiled in exactly the same way.

They will explain at length and with documents that they are owed. The documents are excellent. The figures can be checked. The falsity is not in a number. It is in the belief that somewhere a final column exists in which a person can be made even with another person and walk away unchanged.

I had brought my own ledger. It was bound too well and heavy enough that I carried it against my hip. I had recorded what I gave, what was asked, what arrived unasked, what I had been willing to do, and what willingness ought to count for when the act itself could not be completed. I had separate columns for intention and result. This was fairer, I thought, because results are frequently outside a man's control.

The people nearest us saw the book and assumed I held office there.

Two came forward with a scale between them. One pan held years. The other held a single night. The years were bundled like reeds, each cut to the same length and tied with black thread. The night lay in a glass vessel and moved when nobody touched it.

The first person said he had remained for all the years.

The second said that on the night in the vessel, remaining had become necessary and he had not.

They wanted to know which was heavier.

I told them duration and necessity were different measures and should not share a scale. The first agreed immediately. The second asked whether a thing ceased to be owed merely because it could not be converted.

I said nothing was owed. This floor's entire error was the language of owing.

Then what are you carrying, the second asked.

The ledger had become heavier against my hip. I placed it on a nearby desk and opened to the section on presence. There were subdivisions for willingness, capacity, warning, cost, and whether the person receiving the act had understood the cost correctly. The people gathered close enough that their shoulders touched.

I worked carefully. I removed from the years everything done from habit, everything done while expecting recognition, and everything later used as evidence. The bundle became smaller but did not vanish. From the night I removed fear, confusion, and every consequence the absent person could not reasonably have predicted. The night became lighter too.

We returned both to the scale.

The years went down.

The first person began to cry. The second looked unsurprised.

I explained that the result did not declare anybody good. It established only which contribution carried greater weight under the categories available. The first thanked me. The second picked up the glass vessel.

I did not ask for the years, he said.

He poured the night over them.

The reeds blackened and bent. The scale struck the floor hard enough to break one of its legs.

The people around me began speaking at once. Some said the new fact required a new column. Others said a gift could not be charged against the recipient merely because it had been costly to give. A woman tore a page from my ledger and wrote the sentence down before I could decide whether it was correct.

More scales arrived.

For a while I judged them.

I was good at it. I could find the concealed premise in a complaint before the speaker reached it. I could tell which quantity had been counted twice and which had been left out because including it would ruin the desired sum. People wrote my distinctions in the margins of their books. The line before me reached the far wall.

You stood outside it.

When I finally looked toward you, you had your body turned toward the gate. The line continued moving and I had already raised one hand to call the next case.

I lowered it.

You let me pass between the scales instead of walking beside me. When the aisle narrowed, you stopped altogether and waited for me to notice.

I did notice. I explained that the ledger was wide.

At the gate an old hand came through the bars and rested on the cover. The sleeve was white. I could not see the man attached to it.

He asked me what I thought was being paid off and to whom.

I said I did not know. That was rather the point. Somebody must be keeping the account.

The hand opened the ledger near the middle. I disliked that. The argument began at the first page and was cumulative, and a person entering halfway could make it say anything.

He read one line.

Then he asked what payment would turn that line into a man who had not written it.

I said payment was not meant to change the past.

What is it meant to change?

The balance.

Where is the balance kept?

I had answered this already. I began again, more carefully. The speech came with its examples in the correct order and the objection already answered at the end of every paragraph. The hand remained on the page. You watched the hand and not me.

When I finished, the man behind the bars said that if a debt could be settled here, a person could leave unchanged with a receipt, and the receipt would be worth exactly what he was, which was nothing yet.

You do not settle up. You are changed, or you are not.

I wish that sentence were mine.

I asked the man behind the bars whether it was his.

No.

Whose, then?

He removed his hand from the ledger. The white sleeve remained visible through the bars.

I said attribution mattered. A sentence detached from its speaker can be made to serve anything, and a person ought to know what life had authorised the words.

Would the sentence become less true in a worse mouth?

No, I said. It would become more dangerous.

Would it become more true in a better one?

No.

Then you are not asking about the sentence.

I was asking who had the right to teach it.

The sleeve shifted. For a moment I saw the outline of a shoulder behind the bars and the fall of white cloth from it. I imagined the face above it before the dark supplied one: old, tired correctly, patient with the errors of people who had not yet read enough.

The man asked whether a right to teach could be entered into my ledger.

I said the question confused authority with quantity.

He said that was the first true thing I had told him.

The third gate took the ledger.

It did not take the pages people had torn out.

Behind us, the line continued. The woman who had copied the sentence about costly gifts was using it to decide another case. She quoted me accurately. The people around her wrote it down beneath their own headings.

I told the old hand that the payment was incomplete while the teaching remained in circulation.

The voice behind the bars asked whether I wanted the sentence removed from them.

I said I wanted no ownership of it.

That was not the question.

The woman began explaining the distinction to somebody who had not heard me. She had improved the example.

I could have gone back and demanded the page. I could have corrected the use, clarified that the sentence described a limit and was not itself a method of judgment. The line made space for me when I turned.

You were already on the far side of the gate.

I said the truth did not become mine merely because I had expressed it first.

The hand withdrew with the ledger.

It opened inward. I stepped back sharply because I had expected it to rise.

There was no reason to expect that. The first had risen. The second had opened inside the wall. I resented the needless inconsistency of the design for several steps.

Without the book, my hands kept closing on nothing. I checked my pockets eleven times on one flight of stairs, and I know it was eleven.

The only thing still on me was on my head. I put my hand up in the dark. It gave a little under the fingers and came back to where it was, and I decided that was the heat.

# V. The Two Rooms

The fourth floor has one name over the door and two rooms behind it, and this is where I was destroyed.

Listen to me carefully now.

There was a man in the desert who sat still for years and watched his own mind as a physician watches a fever. He wrote down what came. He counted eight. Not seven. Eight.

Then, a long time afterwards and a long way west, in a chair, another man tidied the list. He had reasons. Seven agrees with the days and the gifts and the pillars. Two of the eight looked to him like one thing seen twice, so one was placed inside the other and the door was given a single name.

They were not one thing. They were the only two on the whole list that had to be kept apart.

So the door was given a single name, and the name was wrong, and behind it there are still two rooms.

In the first room they are grieving. That is all. Some sit against the wall with their knees drawn up. Some walk while they grieve, which is harder. One has been setting a place at a table and taking it away again. Nothing in that room asks them to stop.

The person at the table placed a cup, a plate, and a spoon. The cup was cracked from lip to base and leaked a dark line onto the cloth. Each time the line reached the table's edge, the place was cleared and begun again.

I asked who the place was for.

The person touched the cracked cup with two fingers and said nothing.

I said the repetition was preventing the wound from closing. This was before I had understood the room. I lifted the cup and carried it to a shelf where other broken things had been put beyond use.

The person did not protest. That encouraged me.

The next place was set without a cup. When the hand reached the empty space, it remained there, curved around what was missing. The whole body bent over the hand.

I returned the cup.

You had taken the chair across from the empty place. You did not touch the table or ask who should have occupied it. You remained while the cup leaked and the cloth was changed.

The person setting the table looked at you once. Neither of you spoke.

I stood near the door with the useful cup from the next place in my hands. Nobody had asked me to hold it. When I set it down, it made more noise than I intended.

I tell you this is not a sickness. It is the last working instrument a person has left. Show me what is grieved and I will show you what was still being held. The room should never have been on the list.

Then you spoke.

Weren't you grieving before too?

It was the first thing you had said in the whole descent. Your chin lowered before you began and your voice caught once on grieving, but you did not take it back.

I said yes, of course, but the distinction was precisely—

I heard you, you said.

I was already at the other door with my hand raised. You did not speak again.

In the second room nobody grieves and nobody rests. They check the light. They go to the window. They go out and return and read two lines and put them down and go out again. Every one is certain that in another room, in another city, among people who would be kinder, the thing would become bearable. They call it many names and none is the true one. They have ceased to care, and the ceasing has a direction, and the direction is back toward the nothing we were all made from.

They talk about the weather in there. They are very calm about it. And their hands are wet, all of them, every one, and none of them has mentioned it, and I have never once seen a man in that room dry his hands.

The first man I spoke to had a coat over one arm. He crossed from the door to the window while answering me and from the window to the door before I had finished the next question.

Where are you going?

Out.

Where did you come from?

Out.

He smiled at the symmetry. His hands left wet marks on the coat.

I told him to stay.

The word stopped him. I knew at once that I had found the cure because his body reacted before his argument did. He looked at the nearest chair, then at me, and sat.

I sat across from him. You remained at the first room's threshold, where both rooms were visible.

The man and I made an inventory of what was present. One chair under him. One under me. A coat. A window. A door. The sound of somebody crossing the corridor outside. His two hands, wet. My two hands, dry.

After each item I said, stay.

He repeated it. His breathing slowed. The coat slipped from his knees onto the floor and he did not pick it up.

Others gathered. One wrote the word on the back of a page and folded it into a square. Another asked whether it should be repeated before or after the desire to leave arrived. I said before, during, and after. A third asked how long a person must remain. I told him duration was the wrong measure. The act of remaining was complete in every instant.

They wrote that down too.

The man in front of me had become very still. I believed I had saved him.

Then you shifted in the doorway.

I turned toward you for only a moment. When I looked back, the chair was empty. The coat remained on the floor. Wet footprints crossed to the window, but the window had not opened.

I told the others he had misunderstood the instruction. They wrote that down beneath the first sentence.

Then I noticed that my own hands were wet.

I went to the window to see whether he was below.

There was nothing outside it.

I returned to the chair, read the two written lines, put them down, and went to the window again.

Now hear what the wrong count did to me.

I had one word for both rooms. I was sorrowful and I was indifferent and I did not know they were two, because nobody had left me a word to tell them apart with.

So when the second thing came on me I called it by the name of the first. I called my not-caring a sorrow. I nursed it. I sat with it in the evenings like a man sitting with a sick relative and admired my own constancy, and I thought myself a man of deep feeling because I could not get up, and I wrote about it, and people told me it was beautiful.

It was not beautiful. It was the other room. I was in the other room for eleven years.

And I want you to understand that I was ruined by an administrative decision. Not by a devil, not by a woman, not by my father. By a list being made tidy, in a room I have never seen, by a man who died centuries before my mother was born, and who meant nothing but good by it.

The cure for the second room is one word, I told you, and the word is stay.

Nobody stays. I did not stay.

This is where I met him properly, and I will tell you how because it is the only funny thing that happened to me here.

I had gone to the window for the ninth time that hour and he was there already: an old man, small, in a white cassock that had no business on any of these floors, looking out at nothing with his hands behind his back like somebody waiting for a train he knew was coming.

I recognised the cassock before I recognised anything else. I had chosen it years before. Not that cassock, of course. The one I would wear when the two halves had tired of misunderstanding one another and required somebody able to speak across the wound. I had imagined the cloth falling exactly that way from older shoulders. I had imagined the hands behind the back so often that I had begun practising the posture while reading.

He was not my face made old. That would have frightened me less. He was the man I meant my face to prove I had become.

I asked him how long he had been standing there.

He said that was not a question this place could answer. Then he apologised for the answer, and I have thought about the apology more than the answer.

Then he turned and he spoke to you.

Not to me. He had his back half to me and said something to you that took about as long as a sentence takes. I did not hear one word of it. You did not tell me, and I did not ask.

I told myself this was because the sentence belonged to you.

The other explanation had already occurred to me and I had rejected it: that I was not the person in the room he had come to instruct.

When he left the window, he came with us. He walked on your side, half a step behind, his hands still joined at his back. I treated his company as permission to continue teaching.

Now the gate, and this is the one that opened me.

I put down what it asked for last, after everything else, because I did not believe it counted as a possession.

It is the thing I know about myself. That I am the worst of them. Not badly behaved, not weak, the worst, the bottom of the list, and that everything decent I have ever done was a performance run by something rotten for its own reasons. I have held that for so long that I stopped experiencing it as an opinion. It is the floor I stand on. It is how I know where down is.

And the gate would not open with it in my hands.

So I said, take it, and I meant it the way you mean a thing at the last moment.

And it did not go quietly, because there is another version and I do not hold it.

There is another version of me, one I cannot hold. It was built elsewhere out of things I did without arranging them as evidence, and in it I am kind. Not complicated. Kind. I cannot inspect it in full or get at it to correct it.

And it is true.

The gate placed the two versions on either side of me.

They were not bodies. They were more like the spaces bodies leave when they stand in a room for a long time. In the one to my left, every decent act had its concealed machinery exposed: the wish to be necessary beneath the offer of help, the wish to be remembered beneath patience, the pleasure of moral advantage beneath forgiveness. Nothing good survived inspection because inspection could always descend one motive farther.

In the one to my right were the same acts without the machinery. Not purified acts. The same ones. A chair pulled out before I knew who would sit. Work completed when nobody had been told it was mine. A restraint forgotten immediately after it cost me something. Things too small to support the theory I had built about myself.

I moved toward the left version because it contained more evidence.

The right moved with me.

I stepped toward the right. The left came too. There was no position from which I could keep only one.

I asked the old man which version was the deception.

He said nothing.

I asked which was more fundamental.

He looked at my feet.

The floor beneath them had begun to separate along a line exactly between the two versions. I widened my stance to keep one foot on each side. The crack continued through my body. I felt it enter at the base of the spine and travel upward one joint at a time.

You came nearer. Your hand rose to the height of my shoulder and stopped without touching it.

I told you not to interfere. If the division was true, assistance would only conceal it.

Your hand went down.

When the crack reached the chest they both spoke. Not one and then the other. Both, with my mouth, one saying that refusing help proved the rot and the other no, no, it proved I was trying not to use you, and both arguments were complete, both had every premise, both—

I tried to say which one was mine.

There was not a which.

The sound in my chest went on after my mouth stopped. The gate remained where it was. One version of me was true in my hands and the other was true somewhere I could not reach, and there was no body I knew how to make that could—

It went on for a long time.

I will not tell you I came out clean. I do not know what came out. I am telling you what happened and not what it was for.

Something was taken off me on that floor. I have not been able to find it since. I still go looking, and it is never where I left it.

My hands were up for the whole of it. Both of them on my head, gripping. Afterwards there were two soft places where my thumbs had been. They did not come out. I pressed at them while we went down the stairs and then stopped, because the thing was warm, and warm was the only argument I could still make for it.

# VI. The Name

The fifth is where they say your name.

Not call it. Say it.

The floor is almost empty. The people on it keep far enough apart that no two shadows touch. They could shout without being overheard, but nobody raises a voice. The loud kind burns itself out. This kind is fed daily, kept warm, and turned in the night. A person can carry it for forty years, call it dignity, and be buried in it.

We passed the first man and a name was spoken somewhere behind him.

His shoulders moved. Nothing else did.

The name came again. He closed his eyes until the sound was finished and then opened them. The skin around his mouth had gone pale. He had refused so often that the refusal no longer required a decision.

At the far wall a woman had become difficult to see. The outline of her dress was sharp, but the hands inside its sleeves were thin as smoke. Her name was said and for one instant the hands returned. She tucked them under her arms and waited for them to disappear again.

Near the stairs, another name was spoken.

A man carrying his shoes stopped. He had worn through the soles and tied them together by their laces. The name was said only once.

Here, he answered.

It was not a brave answer. The word came out tired and slightly irritated, the response of somebody interrupted while travelling. Nothing opened above him. No light struck. He remained a man holding ruined shoes.

Then his feet began to bleed.

They had been grey when he entered. Colour travelled into them from the ankles and found every cut the stone had made. He sat down hard, dropped the shoes, and put both hands around one foot.

He shouted that answering had injured him.

The voice said his name again.

He answered with the shout still in his mouth.

The other foot began bleeding. He rocked on the stair, swearing, alive in every place the floor touched him.

Nobody congratulated him. The gate above the stairs opened and waited while he gathered the shoes. He stood badly, placed one foot down, and felt all of it.

He went through.

You watched until the gate closed. I explained that pain was not evidence of error, a principle I would later develop more fully when I reached the fire.

The old man looked at me as though the principle had arrived from somewhere else.

Nobody is chained on that floor. Nobody is prevented from answering.

An address can be refused. If it could not be, none of this would be love. It would be machinery and you would be a lever inside it. Everything here rests on that permission, and this is the floor where the permission is used.

I went back to the first man after his name had been said twice. He was still facing forward with the colour slowly returning around his mouth.

I told him the correct response was simple.

He said simplicity was not evidence.

I said an answer did not certify the speaker. It only acknowledged that the address had arrived.

He asked why acknowledgement was owed.

I had three answers. I gave the shortest. Because the one speaking had held him in existence long enough to refuse.

He said that was coercion dressed as gratitude.

The old man had followed us into the room. He stood beside you, listening. I wanted him to hear how quickly I could separate freedom from obligation.

I asked the seated man whether I might answer for him.

He laughed. It was the first loud sound on the floor.

You may answer anything you like, he said.

I stood behind him and spoke his name into the place where the voice had been. I said yes in the tone I believed he would have used before refusal became his character.

Nothing happened.

The man's shoulders did not return. No colour entered him. My answer occupied only my own mouth.

The old man asked what I had expected.

I said substitution was an established mechanism.

For what?

I began to answer and found that every example I possessed involved somebody offering himself, not somebody volunteering the mouth of the person who had refused.

The seated man thanked me for demonstrating his point.

I moved away before the old man could reply.

Near the far wall, the woman with the empty sleeves was trying to lift one hand toward the place from which her name had come. The hand appeared to the wrist, vanished, appeared again. You moved nearer but did not touch her. When she saw you, she tucked the sleeve under her arm.

Something was said then that I could not hear. You lowered your head. The old man did the same.

I waited for you to answer.

You did not.

I assumed the address had not been yours.

At the window I had asked the old man what held me in existence, since everything in me seemed destructible and I had been reduced to whatever could walk these stairs.

I expected him to say the soul. Everybody says the soul. I had the objection ready.

He said there was no piece of me that could not be destroyed. I had been made from nothing and possessed nothing that argued against returning to it. I remained because I was being addressed and the one addressing me did not stop. That was the whole mechanism. There was no other.

I asked whether that meant the address could never fail.

He looked past me toward you.

That was the question he refused.

Then my name was said.

I heard it. I want that written down and written in my own hand. It was not far away or muffled. It was mine, all of it, the whole name, pronounced the way it sounds when somebody has known it longer than you have.

My mouth opened enough for the cold to touch my teeth.

The sound entered the body in order. The skin between my shoulders tightened first. Then the throat. Then the hands, each finger drawing inward as though around a railing. My knees unlocked.

I had believed hearing one's true name would feel like recognition. It felt like being located.

I did not answer.

Answering would have meant that I had not controlled the exchange. I also wanted to hear the name again, and I thought that if I answered the first call there might not be a second.

There was a second.

This time you turned fully toward the sound.

I watched you turn. My mouth was open. I had only to—

But if I answered now, before it finished, I would never know whether there had been a third.

The last sound of my name passed me. I let it. I stood there with the answer ready and let it go past.

There was not a third.

I waited longer.

The old man went toward the stairs. You followed him. I remained where the second call had found me, keeping the same position so that nothing in the arrangement could suggest I had left.

I cleared my throat.

The sound was mine and therefore proved nothing.

I moved one step toward the place from which the name had come. No response. One step back. No response. I considered answering the second call late, but a delayed answer would reveal that I had spent the silence calculating whether another call could be obtained.

I said yes too quietly for you to hear.

Nothing changed.

I said it louder. The word travelled across the floor and several attenuated people looked toward me. It did not attach itself to the name because the name was no longer being offered.

The seated man I had tried to answer for smiled.

Simple, he said.

I asked the old man to say my name once, only to establish whether the mechanism depended on the speaker or the address.

He stood at the head of the stairs with you and did not turn.

I knew then that the silence was not a tactic for producing my answer. If it had been, the third call would have been the efficient choice. The absence of coercion was not a gentleness added to the mechanism. It was the mechanism, even when gentleness allowed me to disappear.

When I crossed the floor to join you, my footsteps sounded farther away than my body felt. I looked down twice to confirm that the legs were still there.

The silence after it was exact. It did not punish or threaten. Nothing approached to collect an answer. The hands of the woman beside the wall disappeared completely and her empty sleeves remained crossed over one another.

The fifth gate did not tear the name off me. It ceased using it. The door opened, and there was a great deal more room inside me than before. It did not feel like loss at first. It felt like quiet. You have to have lived in a quiet like that to know the difference.

At the bottom of the next flight you stopped.

You lifted your crown with both hands. Nothing resisted you. You set it against the wall carefully, as though it could still be damaged, and walked on without explaining and without looking back.

I noted when you did it. Fifth flight. Bottom stair. Both hands. No hesitation I could measure.

The crown remained against the wall as we descended. At the turn I could still see its upper edge. One more step and it would be out of sight.

I went back.

I told myself an abandoned thing should not be left where anybody might take it. I would carry it only until you chose a safer place. This was stewardship, not possession, and the distinction was obvious because I intended to return it.

When I reached for it, the old man was standing on the stair above.

He did not block me. He did not move his hands from behind his back. He looked from my hand to the crown with the same patience he had given my arguments.

I asked whether leaving a sacred thing against a wall was proper.

He asked who had called it sacred.

I said that was apparent from what it was.

Behind him, you continued down.

The crown against the wall had already gone cold. I could feel the absence of warmth before touching it.

I left it there and hurried after you, carrying the satisfaction of having chosen correctly.

Then I put one hand up to mine.

It was warm. It is always warm. It was the only thing left down there still behaving as though I were somebody.

# VII. Four Seconds

The sixth is where you are seen.

Not judged. Seen and not turned away from, which is worse.

What shames you there is not cruelty. I had my speech about my cruelty prepared for years and was almost looking forward to giving it. What shames you is how much you wanted, how small the wanted things were, how long you waited, and that you counted them in seconds.

Four seconds. A hand on the back of the neck.

A person can live with being known as wicked. It is being known as hungry that finishes him.

At the entrance stood a narrow table and a woman with a bowl of water. She asked what I had brought to be seen.

I began with cruelty. I gave her the deliberate acts first, then the acts whose deliberation I had hidden from myself, then the things omitted because omission leaves cleaner hands. I did not minimise. I named motive where I knew it and refused motive where claiming knowledge would have made the confession more impressive than the act.

It was a good confession.

The woman listened to the end. Then she dipped one hand into the bowl and touched two wet fingers to the centre of my forehead.

Not that, she said.

I thought she meant the confession had been incomplete. I began with the earliest thing I had left out.

Not what you did.

What, then?

What you wanted while you did it.

I told her wanting was already contained in motive.

She moved the bowl aside. On the table underneath it were small objects I had not seen: a chair pulled close to another chair; a door left open after everybody else had gone; a hand no larger than a coin resting at the back of a neck; a page with no writing on it except my name; two cups, one still full because nobody had arrived to drink it.

I reached for the hand.

The woman covered it with her palm.

Not yet.

I asked whose objects they were.

She said they belonged to anybody who recognised them.

Behind us, people moved through the entrance carrying elaborate accounts of what they had done. Each account was returned. Some argued that the account was necessary context. Some added worse acts in the hope of finally becoming too wicked to be examined for anything smaller. The woman waited for their lists to end and uncovered the objects again.

You came to the table. Your eyes moved across the chair, the door, the page, the cups. You did not reach for anything.

I told the woman that some people had disciplined their wants so completely that no object applied.

She looked at you rather than answering me.

The old man lowered his head. I mistook the movement for agreement.

The woman returned the little hand to the table and pointed toward the low wall.

At the front of the floor is a low wall. Everybody is eventually brought to it. Beyond it, ordinary rooms open and close in the dark. Nothing performed there is meant for us, which is why every movement can be seen.

I put both hands on the wall. The stone had no warmth of its own.

You stood behind my right shoulder. When I shifted toward you, you stopped. You did not move backward. I am recording that exactly.

The first room opened.

A young man was reading from pages held too close to his face. His argument was confused. He had mistaken a word in one language for a similar word in another and built the final claim on the mistake. I saw it before he finished the paragraph.

The people listening did not.

When he reached the end, an old woman in the front put both hands together and thanked him. She said the argument had returned something to her that learning had taken away. The young man lowered his pages and could not answer.

I gave the correction aloud. The wall returned my voice to me and none of it entered the room.

The young man would discover the error later. He would be embarrassed and revise the pages. The old woman would retain what the mistaken argument had given her. No correction would remove it.

The room closed.

In the second, a man had come to apologise. He did it badly. He began with intention, included circumstances that reduced his freedom, and said if more often than he said I. I marked each failure.

The person before him listened. Before the apology had improved, she crossed the room and held him.

He stopped speaking into her shoulder.

I said she had interrupted the necessary part. Forgiveness before an accurate account leaves the structure of the harm intact. The old man beside you heard me and did not answer.

The room closed.

In the third, somebody was chosen for work I could have done better. In the fourth, a letter arrived for a person who had not spent the morning checking whether it would. In the fifth, a hand reached across a table and took another hand without either person having to explain what the movement meant.

Each room supplied a reason. I found it. The young man had spoken from need rather than scholarship. The poor apology was received because of a history I could not see. The chosen worker possessed some ordinary quality the task required. The letter had been earned in ways unavailable to inspection. The hands belonged to people whose earlier asking had made the gesture possible.

The reasons were sound.

They changed nothing in my body.

Then the nearest room opened.

In the nearest room there was a man and somebody with him.

He was not a better man than me. I looked. I looked hard and for a long time. I want to say I was searching for a reason, and there was not one. I have gone over it since. There is still not one.

She came behind him while he worked at something in his hands, a strap or a buckle. She put her palm flat against the back of his neck, under the hair, and left it there.

He did not stop what he was doing.

He did not stop.

He kept working at the strap. He said something over his shoulder that was not about her and laughed at the end. Her hand remained and then went away. He never looked up.

Four seconds.

Four full seconds. I counted them because I count every one I have been given. There have been eleven and I know the dates. He received four and did not look up and did not know and will not remember it tomorrow and it cost him nothing it cost him absolutely nothing he did not even have to be present for it he was somewhere else the whole time and she gave it to him anyway and will give him another tonight and he will not notice that one either and I stood in the dark and wanted it so badly that my mouth filled and my hands went over the wall and I would have taken it from him I would have taken it out of the air I would have entered the room and taken it and I knew in that second that I would have and something in me that had been standing for a very long time went out from under me

and I ran.

When I got to the floor there was spit hanging from my mouth.

There is nowhere on that floor to run away to. I ran along the wall with my hands over my ears, which did nothing because this is not a floor you hear. I struck one of the stone dividers with my shoulder, lost the next step, and went down on my knees at the far end.

The thing on my head moved when I ran. Not the way a weight moves. It went with my head and then went on going, half a beat late, lifting at the back the way a light thing lifts in disturbed air.

When I fell, my hand went to the back of my neck.

The thing was exactly as warm as the skin beneath it. Exactly the temperature of the place where her hand had rested on him.

I decided that proved the heat was spreading.

You came as far as the last divider and stopped. You looked at me the way you look at somebody carrying something too heavy for what it turns out to be. I know what I thought the look meant. I will not make you responsible for the thought.

The old man stood on the other side of the divider. He had been there while I watched. He looked down at me without taking his hands from behind his back.

Which part of that happened in you alone? he asked.

I said the grief was mine.

He looked through the opening toward the two people on the other side. I could not point to any part of it that had begun in me and ended there.

I told him I would rather do it alone. I had always done everything alone. I could be worked on privately and emerge without troubling anybody.

He said nobody comes out of anything alone. There was no private version and never had been. Wanting a private version belonged to one of the two rooms on the fourth floor, and I should know which.

I told him I did know. The desert man had known too. That was the point of restoring the eight.

The old man looked through the opening toward the room I had fled.

He did not write this one down, he said.

I thought I had misheard him.

The man in the desert, he said. There is no place for this in his eight.

I got to my feet. My knees had left two wet marks on the stone.

Then who put it here?

The man in the chair.

He said nothing else. He did not need to. The tidy man I had blamed for eleven years was the only one who had left me a word for the thing that had just taken my legs away. I had come down to repair his count, and the floor that destroyed me did not exist in mine.

Here is the thing about the sixth burn that I have not been able to put anywhere.

It is not the same fire.

Everything else burns the way a held thing burns. It is in your hands and hot and you keep hold of it. This one comes off. I felt it being taken from me the way a dressing comes off skin that has grown into it. It was worse than carrying it. When it came away, there was less of me and the less was not a loss.

Something has to be shed. Somebody bled for the shedding to be possible. It is the oldest transaction and the only one down here that is not a transaction, because nothing went the other way.

The sixth gate took the count.

It asked me to give the four seconds first.

I said one.

The first second left the scene. The hand at the back of the neck did not move, but the beginning of its duration became impossible to locate.

I said two. Then three.

At four, the hand lifted. I still remembered the touch. I remembered seeing it begin and end. I could no longer place a measured distance between those events.

Then the gate wanted the eleven.

I gave ten quickly. The dates remained in their order, each attached to a room, weather, clothing, what had happened immediately before and what I did after. The memories were complete. Only the small mark I had made beside each was gone.

I held the last number.

Without it, I said, the others could no longer be described as insufficient. They would simply have happened.

The gate waited.

I looked back at the wall where the hand had rested on somebody else. The room beyond it had closed. There was only stone.

Eleven, I said.

The number went.

Not the memories. The dates remain. The gate took the arithmetic. On the stairs I tried to add the events and they would not stay in a line. I could remember every one without being able to turn them into a sum.

I tried subtraction. That failed too.

You waited at the next turn while I counted on my fingers and arrived nowhere.

# VIII. The Man Who Was Right

The seventh has no heat at all. That is how you know you have arrived.

There are people on it who could stand up.

Nothing holds them. No chain, no ice, no lock at the door. They are still being addressed by somebody who has not stopped, and they hear perfectly well. They decided not to answer so long ago that they have forgotten the deciding. They have a word for the decision now. The word is honesty.

The floor is arranged as a school.

Low desks face a stone platform. Paper has been stacked in exact squares beside every desk. The people nearest the front copy what is said on the platform and pass the pages backward, where others correct the wording without changing the argument. Shelves cover the walls. Every book is accurate.

I took one down. It described the first gate better than I had described it to you. The distinction between taking and receiving occupied nine pages and admitted every objection I could form. Another volume explained why warmth cannot be kept by increasing its quantity. A third proved that no ledger can alter the person who wrote it.

Nobody on the seventh lacked understanding.

At the centre of the platform sat a man who could have stood. His legs were folded beneath him, not wasted or bound. A young woman at the nearest desk wrote down every word he said.

He was explaining the fire.

He said it was not punishment. He said pain was what happened when life entered a part that had organised itself around being numb. He used the example of blood returning to a limb. It was the example I had prepared for you.

He used it better.

When he finished, six people carried copies to the shelves. The man corrected one title before allowing it to be placed.

I asked what he was waiting for.

He said he would stand when he trusted the voice asking him to stand.

What would make the voice trustworthy?

Agreement with the truth.

And who would determine that?

He looked almost offended by the ease of the question.

I would.

Then he asked whether I had found an error in his teaching.

I said his account of the fire was sound but incomplete. He had described the operation without establishing why the old count concealed the condition that made the operation necessary.

The young woman at the desk looked up. Her pen remained against the page.

The man moved one hand toward the empty side of the platform.

I understood the invitation. I also understood that accepting it would allow me to correct the teaching in front of the people who had copied the error. Refusing would have been modest only in appearance. One must not protect humility at the expense of truth.

I went up.

The platform was warmer than the rest of the floor where the man had been sitting. That was the only warmth there and it came entirely from his body. He shifted to give me room without standing.

I told them about the desert and the chair in the West. I gave the number before and the number after. I explained the two rooms placed beneath one name and the eleven years a man could lose because an act of classification looked harmless to the classifier. I did not name either room. Naming had caused enough damage.

The pens moved.

I explained that a clean system can commit an unclean act by making experience answer to symmetry. I said the number seven had not done the violence; the desire to be finished had. I said a list becomes dangerous at the moment its elegance is treated as evidence.

Somebody at the back asked whether my restored eight was itself a list.

I said restoration and invention were different operations.

How?

One submits to what was there.

Who determines what was there?

The evidence.

Who orders the evidence?

I saw where the questions were going and admired their seriousness. I told the room that interpretation could not be avoided merely because it could be abused. A person must accept the burden of judging truly.

And who determines that he has?

The man beside me answered before I could.

He does.

The room laughed.

I raised my voice and gave the last premise again. When they were quiet, I completed the argument.

When I finished, the young woman brought me what she had written. My portion filled fourteen pages. I corrected the number in the first paragraph, replaced a weak verb on the sixth page, and removed the laughter from the account because it did not bear on the conclusion.

She asked what name should go above it.

I told her the work did not need my name.

She waited.

I gave it to her for purposes of attribution.

I spelled it for her. She had already written it correctly.

You had gone to the end of the shelves while I spoke. The old man remained below the platform, looking toward you. He had not taken down one word.

The seated man asked whether I meant to stay and teach the correction properly.

I said I was only passing through.

They all say that, he told me.

I stepped down from the platform.

He was right. That is the thing that cannot be borne. He had been right every hour since and being right had cost him everything there was. If you asked whether he regretted it, he would say no, and he would not be lying.

He wore a crown. It sat badly. There was nobody there to laugh at it.

As we left the platform, the young woman offered him the pages she had taken down. He read the first, changed a word, and handed it back without looking at her. She waited. He began the next explanation.

She put the pages on the shelf and sat down again.

You had stopped beside an empty place at the end of the shelves. It was narrow, no wider than one book.

I knew what you were thinking. There would be a book of mine there one day, the one that put the counts together and healed an old division by explaining it correctly. That was why you had stopped. You had seen the space waiting for me.

You were looking at the floor.

There is no lock on the seventh. I want that written down because it is the only thing about this place that frightens me. A locked door can be worked at. There is nothing to work at here. It is a solitude that could be entered at any moment and will not be entered, because the thing that would let love in has been made into character, and a person does not surrender character merely to get warm.

The seventh gate stood at the bottom of the floor. I knew the number of steps before it came into view. I knew where the stone changed beneath the left foot. I knew there would be no handle.

Beyond it was the fire. The warmth we had followed through every corridor ended at the door as cleanly as light at the edge of a shutter.

I went up to the gate and waited for it to show me what it wanted.

Nothing opened in the wall. No hands appeared. There was no slot at the height of the chest, no scale, no place to set a book. The door remained blank.

I took the crown off with both hands.

It came away easily.

I held it toward the gate. The gate did nothing.

The thing gave under my thumbs. It went soft and kept the two places where I pressed it. I told myself that was the heat of my hands acting on metal.

Then I understood what the gate wanted.

You were two steps behind me, bareheaded, watching my hands.

The gate did not want the crown. It wanted the asking.

I turned halfway.

You raised your eyes from my hands to my mouth.

There were many questions available. I could ask you to remove what I had placed myself, which would make you responsible for correcting an act you had not performed. I could ask whether you understood, which would be an examination. I could ask whether the journey had meant what I believed it meant, which would conceal the desired answer inside the account of the journey.

The plain question was smaller than all of those.

It did not contain the stairs, the pages, the count, the old division, or the fire. It would not prove I had suffered correctly. It offered no context that could make refusal appear unreasonable.

My mouth formed the first shape of it.

You waited.

I had it. There was the first word and after that only the saying of it, only your answer, and if you said no then—

No. That is not—

The whole building was above us. Every floor remained standing because I could still add one more sentence before the question.

I added one.

I put the crown back on my head.

Then I told the gate that another person's freedom could not be made into an instrument of my salvation. I said I would not place on you the responsibility for opening a gate built for me. I said the question was mine to bear until I could ask it without using your answer as payment.

All of that is true.

It is the best argument I have ever constructed.

The gate stayed shut.

I turned to explain the refusal to you. I was already explaining before I had finished turning. The examples came in their proper order. The last objection was answered before you could make it.

The speech was not merely ready.

Halfway through it, I knew which sentence came next.

I had said it here before.

Not once.

The earlier descents returned without arranging themselves as memories. A hand in the second gate releasing the final page. The ledger passing through the bars. My name entering silence. The seventh door, blank every time. My own voice improving against it.

I had used other sentences. The first refusal had been crude: I was not ready. Later I said readiness was a false category and no finite preparation could become proportionate to the gift. After that I argued that asking under conditions of unequal desire could not be free. Each explanation corrected the weakness in the one before it.

Each had been true.

After each, I had found the returning stair. At the top the pages came back warm against the chest. The ledger returned with new blank pages bound into it. My name closed around me. I counted seven things and treated their restoration as proof that the descent had been visionary rather than failed.

The knowledge remained.

That was why the speeches came cleanly. That was why my foot knew the worn stone. Refusal had not expelled me from any floor. It had educated me until I could identify the payment, make it, and carry the lesson farther down. At the seventh I had converted every education into a reason to remain.

I do not know how many times. Twelve is the number of counts I told you about, not the number of descents. I am exact even where exactness protects me.

This was the first descent on which I had found you already coming down.

You let me finish. When I stepped closer, you angled away and looked past me toward the closed gate.

# IX. The Fire

It is not a thing and it was never set for punishing. Nobody lit it and nobody is standing back from it.

It is what arrives when you are at last looked at by the one who has been saying your name, and it is called burning only on account of what you are, not on account of what it is. Blood comes back into a limb that has learned to live without it, and the limb screams, and the blood is the cure, and the screaming is the cure working.

There is no clock in it. Do not ask how long. It is not that kind of duration. The question is not difficult, it is badly made, in the same way the count of eight was badly made, and by the same sort of tidy men.

And what is being burned is not you. It is the enormous quantity of straw and cheap timber you piled over the one true decision you ever made, which is still down there under all of it, and which you have not been able to reach with your own hands for years. That is the whole operation. Not to destroy you. To make you into something that can stand in front of what is coming and not be killed by it.

I turned to give all of that to the old man, because I had it in order and it was good and I wanted it heard.

He was no longer beside you.

I looked back toward the window. He was not there either.

I went along the seventh calling the only name I had for him, which was not a name. The people at their desks did not look up. The man on the platform had begun again. Somewhere behind me, a pen kept pace with his voice.

The route back to the stairs was open. I knew what would happen if I took it. At the top the pages would be against my chest again. The ledger would pull at my shoulder. My name would fit around me. I would count seven things twelve times and begin with a better explanation.

You remained at the gate.

For the first time, you were facing something other than me.

Then there was light along the bottom of the door.

The gate did not open. I watched the line around it. No hinge moved, no latch withdrew, no space appeared between the door and its frame.

The light came through anyway.

It crossed the stone toward us, and there was a body standing in it on our side of the closed gate.

Behind me, the pen stopped.

The man on the platform said that every barrier is permeable to its proper object. He began explaining that the gate had therefore not been broken. His voice remained calm, but the people at the desks were no longer writing. One by one they turned toward the warmth.

He raised his voice.

The warmth reached the platform. His crown threw no reflection. He put both hands around it and continued speaking, faster now, establishing the distinction between an exception and a contradiction.

Nobody wrote the distinction down.

He called for the young woman by name.

She stood.

For one moment I thought she was answering him. Then she stepped away from the desk and faced the body in the light.

The man on the platform was still correct. The gate had not opened. Nothing in its construction had failed. The thing on our side had simply crossed from the direction no resident had been able to travel.

It was standing.

It had been killed. I want to be careful here because this is the one place in the whole account where I have no equipment. It was a small thing and it had been killed and it was standing up, and the killing had not been undone, it was still in it, it was on it, the throat, and it was standing.

And it had seven eyes.

I counted them. I have counted everything on the way down here and this is the only count I have ever made that came out right the first time. Seven. Not eight and not six and not a tidy man's seven that used to be eight. Seven, in a body, looking at me out of all of them at once.

One eye held the colour of the old man's eyes at the window. Another held the slight delay before his apology. I do not know how an eye can contain a delay. I am reporting what I saw.

The white of the cassock remained around the small body for a moment, not as cloth but as the memory of a shape no longer needed. The hands behind the back were the last part to go. They opened at his sides.

I had spent years constructing the person I would obey. I had selected age, office, patience, learning, the visible proof of a life whose arguments had been received. I had made him small so that power would appear disciplined and dressed him in white so that ambition could pass for service. I had prepared every inch of him as carefully as I prepared a sentence.

Another had accepted the shape without accepting one claim I had made for it.

Every floor I have described to you, it had already been on. Every one. The old hand at the ledger was it. The man at the window was it. The voice on the fifth saying my name twice and not a third time was it.

I had built the shape of the theologian myself: the cassock, the hands, the patient posture, the old age in which the world had finally listened. But the one standing inside the shape had never been me. I had been accompanied by another through the whole descent while explaining to you that I was the guide.

I got down. Not out of reverence. There was no decision in it, the legs went.

The crown came off my head as I went down. It fell onto the stone beside me and moved. I could hear it against the floor. It was shaking the way a light thing shakes when something very large is coming up beneath it.

Then the blood.

I am going to do this properly because it is the only part of the whole descent that was done to me rather than by me.

It came onto the back of my hands first and it was warm and that was all it was for a moment, warm, like being touched by somebody who has been out in the sun. Then it went in. Not through a cut. In, the way heat goes into a hand that has been out in the cold too long and has stopped reporting, and the first thing that happens is not pain, the first thing that happens is that the hand starts existing again, and you find out how much of it had gone quiet without telling you.

Then it hurt.

No, first the hands came back. Under the nails, then every fingertip separately, the stone in every line of the palms and I tried to lift them and could not because there was too much hand, every joint there at once, every small bone moving against the next, the wrists beating where the body had been trying the doors from inside.

Then the right elbow. A cold place in it I had carried so long I thought that was simply where an arm ended. The heat entered and the arm went out from under me and I was on one shoulder and looking at you through my fingers.

Not nearer.

Not gone.

I tried to decide which one meant—

The blood reached my throat.

The first sound was not a word. The next almost had your name in it, though I do not know your name down here, though I have not written it, though— I bit it apart.

Then the jaw opened. Every explanation was in there. I had been holding all of them with my teeth. They struck together and I tasted blood from the tongue and could not tell which blood was mine, which had come in through the hands, whether that distinction still—

I pissed myself and only knew because it went cold under my hip.

It went up the arms and into the chest and it found every place I had shut and it opened them in order, and each one screamed as it came back on, and the screaming was not the injury, I understood that at the time and I am not saying it now because it sounds well, I understood it while it was happening. The blood was the cure. The screaming was the cure working. I was not being punished. I was being reperfused.

The chest was worse because it had continued functioning while parts of it disappeared. The heart had learned a shortened route. It beat around certain rooms, sending only enough blood through them to keep their doors from being declared closed. When the full circulation returned, those rooms expanded against the ribs.

The second floor opened first. Every gift I had received arrived again without the stories I had attached to its limits. Warmth struck the chest and left, struck and left, and every departure was clean. Nothing had been stolen on the way out. I had been making a wound out of the fact that a gift ends.

The ledger followed. I felt its absent weight along my hip and all the quantities I had lost at the sixth gate tried to return. They could not become numbers. They entered as people instead. Every column acquired a face I had claimed not to be judging.

Then my name entered, the whole of it, not spoken from outside now but carried in the blood to every place that had refused it. The body answered before the mouth. The back arched. The hands opened.

And the whole of it, the entire enormous thing, was longing.

That is what it is made of. Not fire. Wanting. Every want I had put down and pretended out of existence returned with the circulation: the four seconds, the pages, the sentence rehearsed for a month, the hand at the back of the neck, every answer I had converted into a promise and every boundary I had renamed as delay, all of it alive at the same time, at full strength, in a body, and there was no position I could lie in.

There was also the wanting to be the old man.

It had hidden better than the others because it wore usefulness. I wanted the two halves brought together. I wanted old wounds closed. I wanted people to love what was true because somebody had made it beautiful enough to approach.

And I wanted the somebody to have my name.

The fire did not separate those wants for me. It turned both to full strength and left them touching. Vocation and the wish to be seen having a vocation occupied the same piece of flesh. I could not burn one without feeling the other burn beside it.

On the platform, the seated man was still explaining. His voice broke on a word and began the sentence again. The warmth had reached his folded legs. He could have stood.

I did not see whether he did.

The thing on the stone beside me had gone dark along one edge and was curling in toward the middle, slowly, the way something does when the heat has got properly into it. I watched it do that with my face on the ground. I thought, that is what fire does to metal.

I have never in my life seen what fire does to metal.

And it went on.

And I want you to hear the next part because it is the only good news I have.

And while it was at full strength I went looking, because I am the way I am and I do not stop being the way I am even for that.

I went through myself for the second room. The not-caring. The going to the window, the two lines and putting it down, the other city and the kinder people. I went through myself for it the way you go through a coat you are sure you left something in.

I searched the ordinary places first. The wish to sleep after waking. The moment after reading two lines when the hand puts the page down before the mind has refused it. The belief that work would become possible in a cleaner room. The calm voice used to describe a life already being abandoned.

I found memories of all of them. A memory is not a room. I entered each and struck the walls. Grief answered from several. Shame answered from more. Wanting answered from all of them.

The wet-handed man was not there.

The window was not there.

The coat had no other city in its pockets.

It is not in me.

I could not find it anywhere and I looked properly and I had the whole of myself open at the time, every room of it, nothing shut, and it was not there.

The other one was still in me. The grieving. It was exactly where it has always been and nothing was happening to it. It was not being burned. It was not on the list of what was being burned. I lay there and grieved the entire time and the fire went round it the way water goes round a stone.

Two rooms. I had it right. I had been right from the fourth floor and was right now, and being right had done nothing for me in eleven years. Knowing there are two rooms is not the same as having only one of them left. This did while I lay on the stone what correctness could not do in a decade.

And I will tell you the mechanism because I worked it out lying down and it is the only piece of this I have that nobody handed me.

You cannot not-care while you are burning like that.

That is all it is. The second room needs a low flame. It grows in a man who has gone slightly cold and has not noticed, and the whole of its power is that it feels like sorrow, and sorrow is warm, and the moment something turns the wanting all the way up the not-caring has nowhere to stand. It is not driven out. There is simply no room for it in a body where everything is on.

So the cure for the second room was never the word stay. Stay is what you do while waiting for this. I have been telling people the wrong thing for years, and I was very good at it, and they wrote it down.

It went on until it stopped being able to burn me.

Not because it went out. It did not go out, it is still going, it is what the warm at the front of this account has been the whole time. It stopped because there stopped being anything left in me that had a temperature. Everything in me that could be burned had been. What was left was the thing under the straw, the one decision, and it does not burn, and I lay on the stone in the middle of the fire and it did not hurt anymore and I have never been so awake.

# X. The Crowning

And now the part I will be laughed at for.

I woke up.

I am putting it plainly because I have gone back and forth on whether to tell you at all. I woke, and my face was on stone, and it was cold, and it was the kind of cold that means a place where nothing has happened.

So it was a vision. Fine. I have had one and I am not going to argue with anybody about it.

You are in front of me.

There is a crown on you and it is made of light. It is not the one you put against the wall. It comes off you in every direction and has taken your face. That is the only way I can put it. The light is where the face should be and I cannot see through it.

I have the rest of you. I have the colour of the skin and the shape of the arms and the way you stand with the weight on one side. I have all of it. The face is light.

Your body is turned toward me. One shoulder is lower than the other. I decide there is a smile inside the light because I have always been fast at deciding what a body means.

I started walking.

I am still walking. Do not ask how long. I have told you already that the question is badly made. I have been walking toward you since I woke. You have not moved. I have not arrived. The distance behaves exactly like the warmth behaved above the seventh gate: every route I take runs alongside it and feels like approach.

And on the way there are the others.

I did not expect them. They are off to the side, out where I do not go, and they are all me. Rooms and rooms of me, thousands, every one of them a life I could have had, running at the same time, and I can see into all of them at once and that is the worst thing this place has done to me yet.

In nearly all of them I am walking.

That is what I want you to know. Not dying, not raging, not being punished. Walking, at a good pace, toward something warm, explaining. In one of them I have a house and five children and I am lying in the dark solving a heating problem. In one I am thirty four and very calm. In one I am telling somebody the correct order of the seven floors and I have got it right and she has stopped listening and I have not noticed.

The house is easiest to mistake for arrival. I can see all five children asleep in separate arrangements of limbs. One has kicked away the blanket. One is holding a wooden animal against the face. The hall light has been left on because somebody is afraid of the dark and everybody has agreed not to say who.

At the end of the hall there are two cups beside a bed.

I am under the blanket with my eyes open, calculating why the far rooms receive less heat. I have measured the distance from the furnace, the width of the doors, the hours each room spends closed. Beside me, somebody is awake too.

She says my name.

I tell her the western wall is losing heat faster than the others.

She says there is something she needs to ask.

I tell her I am listening. I am listening so intensely that I begin constructing answers before she has drawn breath. I prepare gentleness, then boundaries, then the distinction between what she feels and what the evidence supports. She watches me prepare.

The question does not come.

In the morning I repair the wall. The room is warmer the next night. I consider the problem solved.

In another life the old cassock fits me.

I am standing between two altars in a church large enough that my voice returns a moment after I use it. Men who had refused the same table have entered through opposite doors. I speak first in one vocabulary and then in the other. I do not pretend the wound was verbal, but I find words that allow each side to recognise its blood in the other's account.

When I finish, nobody applauds. It is a church. They lower their heads instead, which is better.

Later there are books. Other people place my name on them larger than I would have chosen. Students quote the sentence that opened the final door. I correct them when they detach it from the paragraph that made it safe.

At night, in a room provided by the church, I read what has been written about the reconciliation. One historian says conditions had been ready for years and any sufficiently patient intermediary might have succeeded.

I write a correction in the margin.

I do not send it.

The restraint warms me until morning.

In another I am thirty-four and very calm. I have decided that asking would impose, that love is proved by requiring nothing, and that remaining available without demand is the purest form of patience. I am admired for this. People bring me their difficult attachments and I teach them how to carry desire without making it another person's burden.

Years pass in the room while I speak.

The person I am preserving from my question builds a life outside it. I approve of each good thing from the correct distance. I call the pain a participation in her freedom. I do not notice that I have made her freedom perform in my private theology every day.

That man never asks. He also never leaves.

They are not endings. That took me a long time to see. Every one is a man still walking, and he goes on walking, and the light remains exactly where it is on his face.

There are a small number of them where I have stopped.

I looked for what is different in the small number where I stop. It is not that the man is better. At some point he turns, opens his mouth, and asks for something plainly, in his own voice, in a way that can be refused.

I cannot hear the questions. I can see the answers enter their bodies.

In one room the light comes closer.

The man in that room receives the answer he wanted. I see it because his whole body moves toward the person before him and she does not move away. Hands rise. Somebody other than either of them places what must be placed.

For a while the room is too bright to examine.

Then ordinary time begins. There are tired mornings, badly received sentences, an evening when one person needs silence and the other experiences the silence as a verdict. The man reaches for the old instruments. He counts who began the last conversation. He weighs the warmth in what was said against the word that was omitted. He turns a freely given yes into evidence that every later no is a contradiction.

The crown on him becomes difficult to see beneath the one he has drawn.

In that room, asking was not the last gate. It was only the first act performed after the fire.

In another it does not.

The man there asks and receives no word. The person before him lowers her eyes, shifts her weight, and looks toward the open door.

He waits.

At first the waiting is honest. The question has been asked and the answer belongs to somebody else. Then honesty begins manufacturing a rule: silence is not refusal; a decision made under fear is not free; leaving too quickly would punish uncertainty. I can see each principle arrive. Every one protects something real.

The man takes breath to explain that he will wait without pressure.

He stops.

The person is still looking at the door.

He moves aside so the path to it does not pass through him.

She leaves.

He does not call the departure an answer she was too afraid to speak. He does not call it a temporary condition or a wound to be healed. He remains until her footsteps end, then takes the opposite door.

That room contains no verdict. It contains two people leaving by different exits. I kept returning to it because the absence of a verdict made obedience harder, not easier.

In one, the person before him says something very short. The man bends as though struck. His mouth opens again with the beginning of an explanation. I know the explanation. It is a good one. It would prove that the question had been misunderstood and that the answer had therefore been given to something he did not ask.

He closes his mouth.

Then he goes.

Nothing follows him. No fire punishes him for leaving. No voice calls him back to complete the argument. He goes because he was told to go, and the obedience does not make him smaller, and the light remains where it was without becoming cruel.

That life was the one I could not stop watching.

When I looked back at you, you had angled away.

I stopped walking.

Then I looked down at my arm.

I have been carrying it this whole time. Since the first line. Since before the first floor.

It is paper.

It is a drawing of a crown. It is mine. I know the hand. I made it a long time ago on a night I remember exactly, and I made it well. I was always good at that. I carried it down seven floors, held it toward a gate that did not want it, put it back on my own head, and called the act restraint.

And it is warm.

It is warm the way it has been warm every hour since I put it on, and it is exactly as warm as the back of my own neck, and it has been sitting on my own head for the whole of this.

Nothing is warm on its own. I said that at the start. I said it about the fire and was right about the fire. I carried the other half on my head without once running the principle in the other direction.

The paper was not warm because something lived inside it.

It was warm because I did.

It came apart.

No, not like that. Nothing happened, that is the point, I opened my hand and the fold—there was no fold anymore. Dust between the fingers. I tried to keep the larger piece but it broke where I touched it and then that piece was the dust too and I closed my hand because some of it was still there, I could feel it, I could—

There is no piece left.

So I have—

I have nothing.

You are still angled away.

I do not close the distance.

The dust of the paper remains in the lines of my palms. I turn my hands over. It falls onto the stone between us.

You look down at it.

Then you look at my face.

I wait.

I am going to open my mouth now.
