**One: "One gift per hand."**

When you give the reader a sensation — the wall cold on her arms, the smell of blood, Ippo's voice still in the room — that's a gift. But if you hand them five gifts at once, they drop all of them. Your draft had the wall-hug and the blood-smell and Ippo's voice and the Makunouchi memory all in one sentence. That's four gifts in one hand. The reader's hand isn't big enough. One sensation. Then a period. Then the next one. Each sentence is one hand holding one gift out. The reader takes it, holds it, feels the weight of it. _Then_ you offer the next one.

It's not about each _movement_ carrying a gift. It's about each _sentence_. Here's the difference. Your draft did this: 

"she grabbed the back of her neck with her right arm looked above while passing her hand and fingers on her hair then closed her eyes, leaned with her back straight on the wall." That's one sentence carrying four physical actions. 

The reader's eye slides over all of them and lands on none. What it should look like is something closer to: 

"She reached back and grabbed the base of her neck. Her head tilted upward. Her fingers moved through her hair slowly, eyes closing. She leaned against the wall and stood there." Same four actions. But each one gets its own sentence, its own breath, and the reader can _see_ each one land before the next one starts. You're not adding content. You're adding periods.

**Two: "The body knows before the mouth opens."**

Before a character speaks, before they think, before they decide anything — their body has already reacted. Yamaguchi doesn't _decide_ she's troubled and then sigh. She sighs, and the sigh tells her she's troubled. The hand goes to the neck before the thought forms. The teeth clench before the word comes. You already do this instinctively — the temple-tapping, the hair — but you bury it inside paragraphs where it gets lost. Put the body first. Let the body be the sentence. Then let everything else follow from what the body did.

Not as a rigid rule but as a default. Before Ippo says "she said I was a ticking bomb," his hands should do something. His jaw tightens. He shifts his weight. He looks at the crutch. _Then_ the line comes. The reason this works is that in real life, emotion arrives in the body before it arrives in speech. You've been in conversations where you saw someone's face change before they told you the bad news. That half-second where the body betrays what the mouth hasn't said yet — that's where the tension lives. You don't need to do it before every single line of dialogue. But before any line that _matters_, give the body one beat first. Just one. That's enough.

**Three: "Touch the same stone twice."**

Pick one object in the scene. The notebook. The crutch. The door handle. Touch it early, when the emotion is one thing. Then touch it again later, when the emotion has changed. You don't need to explain that the emotion changed. The reader will feel the difference because the object is the same but the character isn't. That's what anchor objects do — they measure the distance someone has traveled without you having to say "and now she felt different." In your draft, Yamaguchi's pen was right there on the desk the whole time. You never went back to it. If you had ended with her noticing the pen still sitting where she left it — parallel to the notebook, unmoved — the reader would have felt the weight of everything that happened between the first time they saw it and the last.



**Four: "The big moment is the slow moment."**

When something important happens, your instinct is to speed up. The phone call — "in a sudden movement she went directly to her phone" — that's the most important beat in the entire section and you gave it half a sentence. That's backwards. The bigger the moment, the slower you write it. Count the steps. She pushes off the wall. She crosses the room. She picks up the phone. She dials. She waits. The silence before the other person answers. _Then_ the English line. Slow motion is how you tell the reader: pay attention, this matters. Speed tells the reader: this is just connective tissue, keep moving.

Slowing down a big moment doesn't mean writing more overall. It means _redistributing_ where your words go. Right now your drafts spend a lot of words on internal monologue — the thinking, the questioning, the reasoning. That stuff tends to run long in your writing. The physical moments, the ones that actually move the scene, get compressed into half-sentences. So it's not about writing more. It's about spending less time in the character's head and more time in the room. The phone call doesn't need five paragraphs. It needs five _sentences_. She pushes off the wall. She crosses the room. She picks up the phone. She dials. She waits. That's maybe forty words. Your internal monologue passages run two hundred words sometimes to say one emotional thing. So if you trade some of that thinking for physical beats, the chapter doesn't get longer. It gets _rebalanced_. The word count might stay the same. The weight shifts to the moments that deserve it.


**Five: "Don't tell me the rain — get me wet."**

You wrote "as if a heavy burden has been put on her shoulder." That's telling me about rain. Getting me wet would be: her shoulders drop, or her exhale fogs the window glass, or she presses her thumb into the muscle at the base of her neck the way you do when something has settled there and won't leave. The "as if" construction is almost always a signal that you're about to summarize an emotion instead of rendering it. When you catch yourself writing "as if" — stop, delete it, and ask: what would I _see_ if I were standing in the room watching this person? Write that instead.
