I get home.

I don't turn on all the lights. Just the one by the shelf. The bulb throws a pale circle across the lower half of the room and leaves everything else in a kind of bruised dark — the kitchen counter, the coat I dropped by the door, the glass of water I poured this morning and never drank. It is still sitting there. The water has gone flat and warm and the light doesn't reach it and I don't reach for it either.

I sit down on the floor with my back against the bed and I don't eat and I don't move and the apartment is quiet the way it is only late at night when even the building settles. The kind of quiet where you can hear the hum of the refrigerator and the faint ticking of the clock above the stove and the sound of your own breathing and nothing else. The walls hold still. The curtain doesn't move. The air sits heavy and warm against my skin, the way it does in rooms that have been closed all day with no one inside them, waiting.

My feet hurt from the walking. I didn't notice until now. My hands are in my lap and I am looking at the shelf the way you look at something when you are not really seeing it yet, when your eyes are pointed at it but your mind is still somewhere else — still on the street, still in the café, still watching Ippo pull his hands back from the table.

The books are there.

I run my finger along the spines of each and every one of the books that I have, and the memories flood me aggressively — years that I spent collecting them like beautiful emeralds, as if they hold the secrets to the universe and I was just there since the first year of my studies, treating them like a promise I made to myself, a promise I made to my brother and to everything that is dear to me. Even though I didn't fully comprehend the world I was involved in, I was smiling unlike my current self. I was lighter then. I remember carrying three of them home in the rain once, pressing them against my chest under my jacket so the covers wouldn't warp, and my brother laughed when I walked through the door soaking wet but the books were dry, perfectly dry, and I held them up like trophies. He didn't understand it then. But he didn't need to. He just smiled because I was smiling.

The self that is now a nurse. The kind of nurse I dreamt to be — the one I dreamt of being is my current reality. And I am unaware of the reasoning that led me to doubt myself, but I have achieved one dream of mine, the first step of my dream. I should be happier. But here I am doubting myself.

Most of the books I have read. Some of them I have only opened. A few I bought and put directly onto the shelf because having them felt like preparation even when I wasn't ready to read them yet. There is one — a deep green spine, no title visible from this angle — that I bought on a Tuesday after a lecture that made me feel like I understood nothing. I walked into the bookstore across from the campus and I didn't look for anything specific, I just needed to hold something that made me feel like I could still learn. So I bought it and I brought it home and I placed it on the shelf and I never opened it. Not once. But I looked at it every morning for a month and it helped. Somehow it helped.

All fields of science and medicine, piled up like that on top of each other. I have been collecting them since the first year and treating them like a promise I made to a version of myself that didn't yet exist.

I look at them for a long time.

The shelf is wooden, slightly bowed in the middle where the weight has bent it over the years. The books lean into each other at odd angles — some upright, some tilted, a few stacked sideways on top because there was no more room. There are small things between them too. A dried flower pressed flat between two spines. A pencil I used for anatomy diagrams. A folded note from a classmate I don't remember anymore but never threw away.

Maybe it was for this.

Maybe I am completely wrong and none of it reaches far enough.

My feet hurt and the room is quiet and I said I would help him and he smiled like I had already done it and I don't know. I genuinely don't know. That is the thing I keep arriving at no matter which direction I approach from — I don't know. And the not knowing is a different shape than it was this morning. This morning I was afraid of the commitment.

Now I am sitting on the floor at the end of it and the commitment is no longer the frightening thing. The frightening thing is the specific size of what I don't know. It has edges now. It has weight. It is sitting on the shelf in front of me in every book I haven't opened far enough.

The floor is cold beneath me. I can feel it through the fabric of my clothes, spreading slowly up through my legs. The bed frame presses into my back in a straight hard line and I don't adjust. I let it press. I let the discomfort keep me here, in this room, in this moment, because if I get comfortable I will fall asleep and if I fall asleep I will wake up tomorrow and nothing will have changed.

I pull the first one. The first book. I touch its spine, touch its cover, clean away the dust that has piled on top of it — even though it is not the dust of dirtiness but rather the dust of forgetfulness. I forgot to use this book. I forgot the smell and the texture of this one and of them all. They are now forgotten. Once they were my heaven. Now they are just there for decoration, and part of me feels a sense of disappointment when I think of something like that. That I let them become furniture. That I let the things I once loved sit still long enough to become part of the scenery.

The first book is about general anatomy. I remember how obsessed I was with it — trying to draw every single muscle of the body, every bone, every organ for the pursuit of my studying. I romanticized every part. I remember sitting at the small desk by the window with coloured pencils spread out like a painter's palette, sketching the layers of the forearm in red and blue and pale yellow, labelling the tendons in my neatest handwriting, and feeling, truly feeling, like I was learning the architecture of something sacred. I remember my brother walking in and asking why I was drawing meat and I laughed so hard I smudged the brachioradialis. And in a way all those memories flood me. They feel like they are conquering me. And the self-doubt is adding a pain to the existing pain because it feels like this will not help.

I don't know what I am looking for. I have all these books, each is a couple of thousand pages, and I am lost for words on what exactly to do to solve this mystery. Yes, Ippo is in need of me. He doesn't say it but I know it. I don't want to be the one who bandages and heals and stitches the bruises — I want to be the one that can help Ippo stop experiencing that kind of pain. The pain of having to be healed. I should prevent that.

'Will anatomy help?'

'Will physiology help?'

'Will rehabilitation help?'

'Will injury prevention help?'

'Will gym training science help?'

'Even if they do — that is too much. I don't know boxing.'

Should I learn boxing? Maybe. But how long did it take Ippo to learn it? Years and years. And Ippo is blessed with talent — you can't turn into a champion without a certain level of talent. While Ippo does work hard, harder than anyone I know, arguably sometimes I wonder about that. I remember my brother being confused at how much Ippo has worked out, how many hours, how many rounds, how many mornings he was already drenched in sweat before anyone else had finished wrapping their hands. And that is frightening. It makes me think that I can't reach that level either.

But I am a girl. I can't box at all. That is strange to even think of — that I even had that thought, to learn boxing for Ippo, the theory for Ippo, and practise it. That should not be needed. How do coaches become coaches? I think they used to be boxers but then grew old and they developed a certain reasoning that helped them teach the art of boxing. But is there even a coach who coached without ever fighting before? I am not sure at all that something like that has existed. So does this mean I should practise boxing for some unknown ceremony to be accepted as someone who can give advice, even when I am a woman?

Dr. Tomoko Yamaguchi is a woman and she was a great athlete before. She won multiple championships and medals and yet she is respected. No one discredits her when she tries to guide people in their respective sport. Even if they don't know that she used to be an athlete — just because she is a doctor of some kind, her words carry weight. So I think there is nothing wrong with a girl learning it theoretically and giving advice on it without even practising the sport. All that matters is that you are knowledgeable and accepted as a person who can give genuine, correct advice.

I open the book.

I read about chronic stress in joints. 'Repetitive rotational movements place enormous torsional stress on the joints of the lower kinetic chain — the ankles, knees, and hips — particularly when the athlete generates force while changing direction at speed.'

Oh no.

'Over time, the muscles surrounding these joints adapt and grow stronger far more quickly than the tendons and ligaments that anchor them. This mismatch creates a dangerous imbalance: the muscles can now produce forces that the connective tissue is not yet built to withstand.'

I read the sentence again.

I understand what this means. I get it. I see an image that scares me when I think of it. This is a nightmare. Everything that I am reading right now is recognisable in someone I care about because that is all he does, that is his fighting style, and the book is talking about him.

'The body, however, is not without safeguards. Within every tendon are small sensory structures known as Golgi tendon organs, which continuously monitor the amount of tension being transmitted through the muscle-tendon unit. When that tension spikes beyond what the central nervous system considers safe — a threshold determined largely by how neurologically adapted the athlete is to the load — these receptors trigger an involuntary inhibitory reflex. The signal travels through the spinal cord and, within milliseconds, shuts down the contracting muscle entirely. The limb simply gives out.'

'For a conditioned athlete, this threshold is trained high. Years of progressive loading teach the nervous system to tolerate greater and greater forces before triggering the reflex. But when an athlete operates under accumulated fatigue — when the muscles are already partially depleted and the tendons already inflamed from chronic overuse — that safety threshold drops. The reflex fires earlier, more abruptly, and at the worst possible moment: mid-movement, mid-rotation, mid-stride.'

'The result is a sudden, involuntary collapse of muscular support around the joint. The movement does not slow — it stops. And the momentum that the muscle was supposed to absorb is instead transferred directly into the tendon, the ligament, or the joint capsule itself. This is the mechanism behind many of the catastrophic tears seen in athletes who perform high-velocity rotational movements under fatigue. The body, in trying to protect itself, creates the very injury it sought to prevent.'

This is bad.

This is like losing your roots because you are strengthening and feeding yourself. This is self-destruction dressed as performance. This is the mechanism that makes athletes feel like ticking bombs — no, I understand now, I understand what Sensei once called them. Ticking bombs that wait and struggle to keep up before they explode into the inevitable, and they can no longer perform the way they used to.

But wait.

I haven't read this, or studied it, not for a while — but if this is true, then all that I have thought of — I remember a moment, in a boxing match, stopping mid-punch, mid-rotational pull, a sudden surprising break of movement. I remember seeing everyone surprised. Even Sawamura was surprised. I remember that. The way the arena went quiet for half a second before the noise came back, as if every person in the room had taken a breath at the same time and held it. I remember my hands gripping the edge of my seat. I remember not understanding what I had just seen. And I remember looking around for someone to explain it and no one could.

That was the moment.

I do see it now. A sudden stop.

...

I closed the book. Not gently. I pressed it shut like pressing a hand over a wound.

The room felt smaller now. The light by the shelf hadn't changed but everything it touched looked different — the spines of the books, the surface of the floor, the backs of my own hands. It all looked like evidence. Like the room had been holding this answer the entire time, waiting quietly on the shelf in a book I had let gather dust.

Does Ippo know this? Does anyone around him know this? What happens if I'm the only one who does?

The questions didn't come one at a time. They stacked. Each one pressed down on the one before it until the weight of them sat somewhere between my throat and my chest, in the place where breath is supposed to live but doesn't when you're afraid.

Dr. Yamaguchi.

The name surfaced the way names do when you've been circling a problem long enough — not as a decision, but as an inevitability. Dr. Tomoko Yamaguchi. Former track athlete. Sports medicine. The woman who once told me during a shift that the hardest part of treating athletes wasn't the injury — it was convincing them the injury existed before it became one.

She would know.

She would know exactly what this book was describing, and she would know whether what I saw in that match — that sudden frozen instant — was what the text said it was.

But knowing and asking are two different things.

What would I even say to her?
