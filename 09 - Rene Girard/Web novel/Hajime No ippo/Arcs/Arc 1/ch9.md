

_What would I even say to her?_

I tried to assemble a sentence. Something professional. Something that didn't sound like a nurse who made a promise she couldn't keep to a man she —

Something clinical. Start clinical.

_Dr. Yamaguchi, I've been reading about chronic stress on the musculotendinous unit and I wanted to ask —_

No. Too stiff. She'd see through it in a second. She has spent years reading athletes the way I read textbooks — not the words but the silences between them, the flinch before the admission, the careful rearrangement of pain into something presentable. She would hear the rehearsal in my voice the way a surgeon hears the lie in _it doesn't hurt that much_.

_I have a question about cumulative joint degradation in a patient who —_

He's not a patient. He's not her patient. He's not mine either. He's something I don't have a clinical word for and that is part of the problem — that every framework I reach for slides off the shape of what he actually is to me, and I am left holding language that doesn't fit, pressing it against him like a bandage cut for the wrong wound.

_There's someone I care about who performs high-intensity rotational movements and I believe his body is —_

Someone I care about. I could already hear the pause on the other end. The careful, professional silence of a doctor recalibrating who she was really talking to. Not a colleague. Not a student presenting a case. A girl on the floor of her apartment at night with a book open beside her and a feeling in her chest she cannot name and a phone she has not yet picked up.

_What do you actually want to ask her, Kumi?_

The real question. The one underneath all the rehearsed ones. The one I had been circling since I opened the book:

_Is it already too late?_

No — worse than that.

_If it's not too late, what does helping him actually look like? And is it something I can do, or is it something I just have to watch someone else do — or something no one can do at all?_

I put the book down on the floor beside me. Open. Spine up. The page I'd been reading still visible — that paragraph about the Golgi tendon organs, the involuntary reflex, the body shutting itself down to save itself and destroying itself in the saving. The words sat there on the page the way words do when they've stopped being information and started being evidence. Evidence of something I had watched happen and not understood. Evidence that my eyes had seen it before my mind could name it.

The phone sat on the edge of the shelf where I'd left it. Not far. Not close. The kind of distance that only matters when you're deciding whether to reach for it.

The room was still quiet. But the quiet had changed. At the start of the night it had pressed against me like a wall — something heavy, something architectural, something that kept me still because stillness felt safer than any direction I might move in. Now it was thinner. Hollow. It was the silence before a phone call I hadn't made yet, and the silence knew it, and I knew it, and we were both waiting for me to stop pretending I hadn't already decided.

Same room. Same floor. Same light falling the same way across the same shelf and the same books and the same dust I had wiped from one cover and left on all the others.

Same place I had that dream.

The ring. The dark. The hand on the canvas — palm up, fingers slightly open, the way hands go when they stop deciding anything. I had woken from it with the ceiling above me and a stone beneath my ribs and I had told myself it was just a dream, just the residue of worry, just the kind of image a mind produces when it has spent too long watching someone do dangerous things and caring about whether they survive them.

But the hand had come back. Not in sleep. In the café, when Ippo pulled his hands from the table. In the book, when the text described the limb giving out — _the movement does not slow, it stops_. In my own hands now, holding nothing, resting in my lap, palms up in a way I hadn't chosen and only just noticed.

I turned them over. Pressed them flat against my knees. Deliberate. Decided.

The phone.

I reached for it. Held it. Did not dial.

The screen lit up and my face reflected back at me in the glass — dim, partial, the way you see yourself in windows at night when you're inside and everything outside is dark. I looked tired. Not the kind of tired that sleep fixes. The kind that lives behind the eyes and has to do with knowing something you haven't said yet.

The Dempsey Roll.

That was what Kamogawa-sensei had told him. I remembered it the way you remember things you overheard rather than things you were told — secondhand, fragmented, assembled from Ippo's own retelling and Ryo's silence afterward. _Don't use it too often._ The old man's voice, or what I imagined of it, carrying the weight of someone who had watched enough fighters break to know what breaking looked like before it happened. He hadn't explained it in clinical terms. He wouldn't. He was a trainer, not a doctor. He understood the body the way a carpenter understands wood — by grain, by feel, by the sound it makes just before it splits.

But did he understand _this_?

The book described a mechanism. A sequence. A thing the body does to itself under specific, repeatable conditions — conditions that are met every single time Ippo loads his weight onto his lead leg, drops his centre of gravity, and begins the rotation. Every single time. Not sometimes. Not in extreme cases. Every time. The text was not ambiguous. The text was not hedging. The text laid it out the way textbooks lay things out when the science is settled and the only question left is whether anyone is paying attention.

Ippo's hands on the table.

The bruise on his knuckle that he moved his cup to cover.

The Sendō fight — the fourth round, the combination that started and then didn't finish, the half-second where his body froze and everyone in the arena saw it and no one said the right word for what it was. They said _fatigue_. They said _pacing_. They said _he'll recover between rounds_.

But the book said something else.

The book said: _The reflex fires earlier, more abruptly, and at the worst possible moment._

The book said: _The body, in trying to protect itself, creates the very injury it sought to prevent._

I looked at the phone. The screen had gone dark again. My reflection was gone. Just the black glass and the faint warmth of it in my hand from where I'd been holding it.

Does Ippo know this?

I asked myself the question and then I sat with it the way you sit with a question when you already know the answer but you are not yet willing to say it out loud because saying it makes it yours and once it is yours you have to do something with it.

No. He doesn't know. He knows his body the way athletes know their bodies — through performance, through output, through what it can and cannot do on a given day. He knows when he is sore and when he is tired and when something feels _off_ but he does not know _why_, not in the way this book knows why, not in the language of tendon organs and inhibitory reflexes and the slow, invisible degradation of structures that were never designed to absorb the kind of force he asks them to absorb.

Does anyone around him know?

Kamogawa-sensei knows something. He knows enough to warn. But warning is not the same as understanding and understanding is not the same as intervening and intervening is not the same as —

What happens if I'm the only one who does?

The question sat in the room like a third person. It had weight. It had posture. It leaned against the shelf beside me and looked at the books and looked at the phone and looked at me and waited.

I could be wrong. I could be a second-year nurse who read one chapter of one book and drew a line between a paragraph and a memory and called it a diagnosis. I could be the kind of person who sees patterns because she is looking for them, because she made a promise in a café and now needs the promise to have been worth making. I could be projecting. I could be afraid. I could be both, and both could be making me see things that aren't there.

But the hand in the dream was there. Palm up. Fingers open. And I had not chosen whose hand it was because my mind already knew it did not matter, that the mechanism does not care whose body it inhabits, that the reflex fires the same way in every human joint under every human name.

I unlocked the phone.

Dr. Yamaguchi's number was not in my contacts. I had never called her directly. I knew her the way young nurses know senior doctors — by reputation, by proximity, by the things they say during shifts that lodge somewhere in your memory and wait there, patient, until the night you need them. She had told me once — not me specifically, a group of us, during a rotation — that the hardest part of treating athletes wasn't the injury. It was convincing them the injury existed before it became one.

I hadn't understood that then. I had nodded. I had written it down. I had filed it under _things experienced doctors say that sound wise_ and moved on.

I understood it now.

I found the clinic number through the hospital directory. Bone clinic. Orthopaedic surgery. Dr. Tomoko Yamaguchi. The listing was plain. Name, specialty, phone, address. Nothing about the judo. Nothing about the championships. Nothing about the years she spent inside a body that performed at the highest level before she spent decades treating bodies that did the same. Just a name and a number and a small font that said _Tokyo_.

I dialled.

It rang once. Twice. Three times, and each ring was a room I walked through — the first one was doubt, the second one was the café, the third one was the ring in the dream, dark, with the ropes and the hand and the —

"Yamaguchi Clinic."

Not her. A receptionist. A voice that sounded like the end of a shift — polite, efficient, already halfway to the next call.

"I — yes. Hello. My name is Mashiba Kumi. I'm a nurse at —"

"Are you calling to schedule an appointment?"

"No. I — I wanted to speak with Dr. Yamaguchi directly, if possible. It's regarding a — a patient concern. A clinical question."

A pause. The sound of paper, or a keyboard — something being checked or noted.

"Dr. Yamaguchi is currently with a patient. Can I take a message?"

A message. A sentence. Something clinical. Something I could leave behind like a note on a counter and walk away from and wait.

"Yes. Could you — could you tell her that I have a question about cumulative joint degradation in an athlete who performs repeated high-velocity rotational movements under progressive fatigue, and specifically about the inhibitory reflex threshold and the risk of — of catastrophic connective tissue failure in —"

I stopped.

The receptionist was quiet. Not impatient. Just quiet in the way people are quiet when someone is saying something that has stopped sounding like a message and started sounding like something else.

"I'm sorry," I said. "Could you just — could you tell her Mashiba Kumi called. From the hospital. She might remember me from a clinical rotation. I have a question about an athlete I'm concerned about and I — I would appreciate the chance to speak with her when she's available."

"Of course. I'll pass the message along."

"Thank you."

I hung up. Set the phone down. Not on the shelf this time. On the floor beside me, next to the book. The phone and the book — two objects that now belonged to the same sentence, the same night, the same decision I had made without fully understanding what it would cost me or where it would lead.

The room was quiet again.

But it was a different quiet now. Not the hollow kind. Not the heavy kind. It was the quiet after something has been set in motion — a stone pushed from the top of a hill, a message left with a receptionist who would write my name on a slip of paper and place it on a desk and at some point, maybe tonight, maybe tomorrow, a woman who used to be a champion and is now a surgeon would read it and decide whether to call back.

I sat there.

The book open beside me. The phone dark beside the book. My hands in my lap — palms down, this time. Deliberate. Decided.

I didn't know if she would call tonight. I didn't know if she would call at all. I didn't know if what I had read was what I thought it was or if what I had seen was what I feared it was or if the dream meant anything or if dreams ever mean anything or if I was a nurse on the floor of her apartment at midnight making a problem out of a paragraph.

But I had called.

That was the first thing. The only thing. The rest of it — the answers, the diagnosis, the plan, the question of whether I was right or wrong or too late or just late enough — all of that belonged to the next conversation, the one that hadn't happened yet, the one that existed right now only as a name on a slip of paper on a desk in a clinic in Tokyo where a woman I barely knew held the one thing I needed most.

Not a cure. Not a promise.

A word for what I had seen.

The phone rang.

It had been — I don't know. Twenty minutes. Maybe thirty. Time had become unreliable the way it does when you're waiting for something and every minute is a room you're locked inside until the next one opens. I had not moved. The book was still open. My back was still against the bed. My feet still hurt from the walking and I had stopped noticing them again the way you stop noticing pain when a different kind of pain takes up all the space.

The phone rang and I looked at it and the screen said a number I didn't recognise and my hand — my actual, physical hand — hesitated. One ring. Two.

I picked up.

"Mashiba-san?"

Her voice. I had heard it before — in the hospital, in the hallway, once across a cafeteria table — but never like this. Never directed at me, specifically, with my name in it, at this hour, with this weight behind it.

"Yes. Dr. Yamaguchi. Thank you for — I'm sorry for calling so late, I —"

"My receptionist said you had a question about rotational joint degradation under fatigue. And an athlete you're concerned about."

Direct. No warmth and no coldness either. Just the clean, level tone of someone who has been asked clinical questions at odd hours enough times to know that the hour matters less than the question.

"Yes," I said. "I — yes."

A pause. Then:

"Tell me what you've seen."
