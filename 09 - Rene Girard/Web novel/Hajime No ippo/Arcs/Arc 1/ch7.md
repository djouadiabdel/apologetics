The date went well. We talked about many things and for once I felt like Ippo — even when he is thinking of boxing — he was there. He looked at me not as a ring, not as a square battlefield where everyone bleeds, but just as a person. And I loved that.

But I feel embarrassed that I told him what I told him. That I did what I did. This reminds me of the day I said I would always go to my brother's matches and support him even if it hurts — and the experience never got better. It hurt more than I could handle every single time. Every punch he takes, every punch he throws, the blood on his face, the bruises, his legs trembling, shaking, unable to stand after absorbing everything the fight gave him. That in itself hurts to see. And I fear I will deal with the same thing with Ippo. That I will commit to something far more dangerous than I will ever be able to handle.

But why am I even afraid?

As I walk down the street my thoughts roam — about many things, many endeavours of life — and they all again resemble boxing. As if boxing is special to me. As if I want to box. As if I love it. I think about it a lot despite my desire for a peaceful life. I still don't know why boxing keeps pulling me back inside of it, again and again. It is like how I became a nurse to help my brother. And why is everything revolving around my brother? Even Ippo was once a matter related to Ryo. Is everything in my life a manuscript written because of him?

Even if it was — true or not — I want to help my brother. I never wanted to see him in pain or see him hurt. I became a nurse to help him, to stop the pain, to stop him from feeling it, to support him again and again. That was always the goal. And now Ippo?

I think I can do it. I know I can do it. Just like how I entered nursing school not knowing what to expect, not knowing about the pain I would find in the rooms of patients who are tortured and broken. From rooms of death, life can come out and bloom. The faces of the cured and the faces of the uncured, the hurt — but what I always noticed was that having a family, having support, mattered more for the patients than surviving alone. The image of everyone I care about is in my mind. Maybe, just maybe, this is what pushes me forward. Maybe this is the peaceful life.

But I have to ask myself — how will I help Ippo? I am not a boxer. I am certainly not an expert in what Ippo has to do or think. All I know is to treat the pain, the bruises, the blood. To stitch Ryo and Ippo. That is the limit of what my knowledge can achieve right now. I am not even aware of what I would actually be able to do to help him. Will I even succeed? Did I just put myself in a problem I don't know how to solve? What did I even do? What would Ippo even need?

Let me think. Let me think.

Every time Ippo is in the ring he changes. He starts at a certain rhythm, he endures, he fights, he experiences all these kinds of emotions. I know them. I see them. I see his face, the bruises, the blood, his eyes — they change. He is mentally reformed and shifted by the ring into something different. Ippo is undergoing something in there. All those punches, all those failed moments, all that sweat — it is just the route he has to take in order to finally reach the end of the fight. So what would he need from me? In all his fights I remember being helpless. Opening and closing my eyes, praying for him not to be hurt, unable to watch, unable to do anything. What is even helping him?

Let me ask a simpler question. I don't know Ippo as well as I know Ryo. So what would my brother want?

My brother would feel guilty to even want something from me. He thinks he has to be the one who gives. Everyone looks at him and fears him but he is a good person — he does things only for his goal, and people misread that as coldness, as selfishness, as something hard and unkind. But I know what he needs. Even when I couldn't give it, I always knew what it was. I could look at Ryo after a loss and know exactly where the damage sat — not the physical kind, the other kind — and I knew what words would reach him and which ones would make it worse.

With Ippo I don't have that.

That is the thing I have been circling without landing on. Ryo I understand the way you understand something you have been studying your whole life without meaning to. Ippo I am only beginning to read, and he is written in a language that looks familiar and keeps meaning something different than I expect. I know what Ryo would need from me. I do not know what Ippo needs. I don't know if what Ippo needs is even something I am capable of giving. And I said I would help him grow stronger, and he smiled, and now I am walking home in the dark with the knowledge that loving someone and knowing how to help them are two completely different things and I have only ever had both at once with one person in my life.

Ippo is a sweet person. So is Ryo. But Ryo gets misunderstood. Ippo is sweet — but inside the ring he changes. What if I try to help Ippo with something ring-related and I meet that version of him? The Ippo that hits hard, the Ippo that is somewhere else entirely. Would he even accept my help? And that, in a way, makes me scared.

For a moment I come back to the street. I notice the people walking by — the synchronized movement, everyone moving simultaneously at a certain rhythm they do not know they are keeping. Everyone walking toward a life they cannot fully see, not noticing anything beyond the next step. Just like I was doing. I was walking and thinking of the promise I made and I did not notice anyone. Which means no one was noticing me either. Everyone moving as I was moving — in the direction of something, in the randomness of something, toward a goal they are already inside of without knowing it.

Is Ippo like that?

Will Ippo look at me, at my effort, and notice it? Will he accept it? Or will he be like those people — focused so completely on his strength that he walks past me the way they walked past each other, seeing nothing on either side?

I will take this as a sign. I have to work hard enough that Ippo notices me. Not so that he stops walking toward his goal — I would never want that — but so that when he walks toward it, I am there beside him. Part of the direction. Part of what makes the walking possible. He will notice that. He will not reject it. As long as I am walking with him, I will be seen. I will be part of his goal now.

And there is something else. Something I keep forgetting when the fear is loudest.

Ippo always accepts help. That is who he is.

His mother has been there since the first day. Since the day his father was gone, she stood beside whatever Ippo decided to become. She hates boxing. She hates watching her son hurt. But she understands that Ippo is who he is, and she was there — always there — and not once did Ippo turn her away.

Takamura. Ippo doesn't always say it but Takamura is the ceiling to him, the unreachable thing, the measure of everything. Any word from Takamura is sacred. Ippo walks toward it without question.

His coach. Aoki, Kimura, Itagaki. The whole gym. Ippo has never in his life looked at someone trying to help him and said no.

He will not say no to me.

He will not say no to me.

And yet.

I don't know what I am offering him. I said the words — I will help you grow stronger — and I meant them and I still mean them and I have absolutely no idea what they mean in practice. I am not a trainer. I am not a coach. I treat injuries. I stitch what breaks. I arrive after. I said I didn't want to arrive after anymore and now I am standing in front of a commitment I made before I understood its size and I am realizing, walking home alone in the evening with the city going quiet around me, that I have done this before. Every time. Nursing school. Ryo's first fight. I always walk through the door before I see how heavy it is.

What does Ippo even need from me that he doesn't already have?

What do I even know that could help him?

I know the damage. I know what it looks like. I know the names of what breaks and how long it takes to stop breaking and what happens when it breaks again before it has finished healing the first time. I know all of that. But knowing the damage is not the same as stopping it. I have been confusing those two things and I only realize it now, on this street, with my hands in my pockets and my mind louder than the traffic.

I get home.

I don't turn on all the lights. Just the one by the shelf. I sit down on the floor with my back against the bed and I don't eat and I don't move and the apartment is quiet the way it is only late at night when even the building settles. My feet hurt from the walking. I didn't notice until now. My hands are in my lap and I am looking at the shelf the way you look at something when you are not really seeing it yet, when your eyes are pointed at it but your mind is still somewhere else — still on the street, still in the café, still watching Ippo pull his hands back from the table.

The books are there. Anatomy. Physiology. Rehabilitation. Sports medicine. Injury prevention. I have been collecting them since the first year and treating them like a promise I made to myself about the kind of nurse I was going to become. Most of them I have read. Some of them I have only opened. A few I bought and put directly onto the shelf because having them felt like preparation even when I wasn't ready to read them yet.

I look at them for a long time.

Maybe it was for this.

Maybe I am completely wrong and none of it reaches far enough.

My feet hurt and the room is quiet and I said I would help him and he smiled like I had already done it and I don't know. I genuinely don't know. That is the thing I keep arriving at no matter which direction I approach from — I don't know. And the not knowing is a different shape than it was this morning. This morning I was afraid of the commitment. Now I am sitting on the floor at the end of it and the commitment is no longer the frightening thing. The frightening thing is the specific size of what I don't know. It has edges now. It has weight. It is sitting on the shelf in front of me in every book I haven't opened far enough.

I don't know. But I have to start somewhere.

I reach up and pull the first one out. My hands are still. Everything else is loud.
