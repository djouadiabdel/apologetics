The silence sat between them.

Ippo looked at the table. He looked at his hands on the table. The knuckles, the way the skin pulls across them when he spreads his fingers flat, the geography of scar tissue that has its own logic now, its own weather. He pulled them back.

Kumi was watching him do this.

She said nothing.

Ippo: I think — I think strength is, when you have nothing left and you still — when the legs go and the arms go and the only thing left is —

He stopped.

Kumi waited.

Ippo: I don't know how to say it outside of the ring.

He said it like a confession. He did not know it was a confession.

Kumi looked at him — at the bruise by his eye, at the way he kept his hands off the table now like he was not sure where to put them, at the way he had answered her question about his life by answering a question she had not asked — and she felt something move in her chest that she did not have a word for. Not sadness. Not the fear she carries for Ryo. Something quieter than both. Like watching someone walk toward a door they cannot see.

She picked up her cup. The café was still empty. Outside the window a bicycle passed, and a woman with a bag, and the ordinary city continued without them.

Kumi: Do you think about anything else?

Ippo: What do you mean?

Kumi: I mean — outside of boxing. Outside of the gym, outside of the next fight. When you wake up in the morning before you start thinking about training. What is there?

Ippo thought about the morning. He thought about how he wakes up and the first thing he does is open and close his hand. Check the grip. Check what the night did to the joints. He thought about how he walks to the window and watches the road before his run and already his mind is on his legs, on his pace, on the distance. He thought about the boat and how working on the boat is rest but rest is preparation and preparation is —

Ippo: I think about what I need to do to get better.

Kumi smiled. It was not a happy smile. It was the smile of someone who had just had something confirmed.

Kumi: Yes. I know.

She said it softly. There was no accusation in it. That was the thing Ippo did not notice — there was no accusation in it. Just recognition. Just the particular exhaustion of someone who has been watching something for a long time and has finally stopped being surprised by it.

Ippo noticed that she was smiling. He thought she was happy with his answer. He felt relieved.

The café filled a little. Voices came in from the door, a couple, a man with a newspaper. The noise returned. Kumi closed her eyes for just a moment — one breath — and then opened them and looked at him again.

Kumi: I'm glad you came.

Ippo: Me too.

He meant it completely. He was glad. He was sitting across from Kumi and the coffee was warm and he was resting as the coach ordered and everything was in its right place.

He looked at his hands again, just briefly.

Then he looked up at her and smiled.

The confusion, the question he could not answer — it was still there, sitting somewhere under his ribs. But Ippo looked at it and decided it was proof. He thinks about getting stronger. He thinks about improving. He takes care of his hands, his grip, his sleep. He rests as the coach told him. He does everything correctly, in the right direction, and the question Kumi asked him — what is strength — was not a challenge to that. It was confirmation. He had a goal and he was moving toward it. That was enough. That was more than enough.

He was glad she was here. She is always here. In the arena, row after row of noise, and Kumi somewhere in it watching him take a liver shot, watching the blood leave his mouth, watching him hit the canvas and get back up. She was there for all of it. His coach, his friends, his mother. All of them. Ippo looked at her across the table and felt something full and warm in his chest, the way you feel after a hard session when the body has done what it was asked to do.

---

Kumi watched him smile.

She had seen that smile before. After rounds. After the doctor cleared him. After Kamogawa told him something that meant he could keep going. It was the smile of a man who had just found a reason to continue.

She thought: he is thinking about boxing again.

She was wrong. But she did not know she was wrong, and so she sat with what she assumed was true, and what she assumed was true felt like a wall she had been pressing against for a long time without naming it a wall.

And still — she wanted to help him.

That was the part she could not argue herself out of. She had tried. She had watched Ryo come home with his body broken in the particular ways that sport breaks a body, and she had chosen medicine not to fix it but to understand it, and understanding it had not made it easier to watch, it had only made her more precise about what she was afraid of. She knew the names of the things that could go wrong. She knew how close some of them had already come.

But she wanted to help him.

Not just to be the one who cleaned the cuts after. She wanted to be ahead of it. To know what was building in his joints before it broke. To give him something that made him harder to damage. She had the knowledge. She was building more of it. And Ryo was there too — her brother, her first reason — both of them always hurt, always at the edge of something, and she in the middle of it with her hands and what she knew.

But then the other thought.

Ippo would not want this. That was the fear underneath the decision. He would look at her help and feel something shift in the balance between them, something he would not name either, and he would pull back the way he pulled his hands back from the table — quietly, instinctively, without explanation. Ippo carried everyone. That was what his hands did. They did not know how to receive.

She had the knowledge. She was building more of it. And Ryo was there too — her brother, her first reason. She thought about him the way she always did when she thought about Ippo, not separately but together, because they occupied the same place in her chest. The same fear, the same love, the same particular exhaustion of watching someone you cannot stop. She had spent years becoming someone who could help Ryo and still she stood at the edge of it, still not enough, still arriving after the damage. She did not want to arrive after the damage again.

She looked at Ippo.

He was still smiling at her, warm and uncomplicated.

Kumi: I want to help you get stronger.

She said it before she had decided to say it.

Ippo's face opened. He heard: I believe in you. He heard all the things he already felt in the room and now she had said them out loud and he felt the warmth in his chest move up into his face.

Ippo: Thank you, Kumi.

He meant it completely.

Kumi nodded. She looked at the table. She did not tell him what she meant. She did not know yet if she had the courage to tell him what she meant, or if it would matter, or if she would find a way to do it quietly, around the edges of his certainty, without him feeling helped.

She was scared. Not of boxing. Not of Ippo. She was scared of the specific thing she had just committed to — of walking toward the thing she loved and hated at the same time and trying to hold both without dropping either.

Outside the window the city continued. The café filled a little more. Ippo was happy.

Kumi picked up her cup and said nothing else.
