
"Are you calling to schedule an appointment?"

"No. I — I wanted to speak with Dr. Yamaguchi directly, if possible. It's regarding a — a patient concern. A clinical question."

A pause. The sound of paper, or a keyboard — something being checked or noted.

"Dr. Yamaguchi is currently with a patient. Can I take a message?"

A message. A sentence. Something clinical. Something I could leave behind like a note on a counter and walk away from and wait.

"Yes. Could you — could you tell her that I have a question about cumulative joint degradation in an athlete who performs repeated high-velocity rotational movements under progressive fatigue, and specifically about the inhibitory reflex threshold and the risk of — of catastrophic connective tissue failure in —"

I stopped.

The receptionist was quiet. Not impatient. Just quiet in the way people are quiet when someone is saying something that has stopped sounding like a message and started sounding like something else.

"I'm sorry," I said. "Could you just could you tell her Mashiba Kumi called. I have a question about an athlete I'm concerned about and I — I would appreciate the chance to speak with her when she's available."

"Of course. I'll pass the message along."

"Thank you."

I hung up. Set the phone down. Not on the shelf this time. On the floor beside me, next to the book. The phone and the book — two objects that now belonged to the same sentence, the same night, the same decision I had made without fully understanding what it would cost me or where it would lead.

The room was quiet again.

But it was a different quiet now. Not the hollow kind. Not the heavy kind. It was the quiet after something has been set in motion — a stone pushed from the top of a hill, a message left with a receptionist who would write my name on a slip of paper and place it on a desk and at some point, maybe tonight, maybe tomorrow, a woman who used to be a champion and is now a surgeon would read it and decide whether to call back.

I sat there.

The book open beside me. The phone dark beside the book. My hands in my lap — palms down, this time. Deliberate. Decided.

I didn't know if she would call tonight. I didn't know if she would call at all. I didn't know if what I had read was what I thought it was or if what I had seen was what I feared it was or if the dream meant anything or if dreams ever mean anything or if I was a nurse on the floor of her apartment at midnight making a problem out of a paragraph.

But I had called.

That was the first thing. The only thing. The rest of it — the answers, the diagnosis, the plan, the question of whether I was right or wrong or too late or just late enough — all of that belonged to the next conversation, the one that hadn't happened yet, the one that existed right now only as a name on a slip of paper on a desk in a clinic in Tokyo where a woman I barely knew held the one thing I needed most.

Not a cure. Not a promise.

A word for what I had seen.

The phone rang.

It had been — I don't know. Twenty minutes. Maybe thirty. Time had become unreliable the way it does when you're waiting for something and every minute is a room you're locked inside until the next one opens. I had not moved. The book was still open. My back was still against the bed. My feet still hurt from the walking and I had stopped noticing them again the way you stop noticing pain when a different kind of pain takes up all the space.

The phone rang and I looked at it and the screen said a number I didn't recognise and my hand — my actual, physical hand — hesitated. One ring. Two.

I picked up.

"Mashiba-san?"

Her voice. I had heard it before — in the hospital, in the hallway, once across a cafeteria table — but never like this. Never directed at me, specifically, with my name in it, at this hour, with this weight behind it.

"Yes. Dr. Yamaguchi. Thank you for — I'm sorry for calling so late, I —"

"My receptionist said you had a question about rotational joint degradation under fatigue. And an athlete you're concerned about."

Direct. No warmth and no coldness either. Just the clean, level tone of someone who has been asked clinical questions at odd hours enough times to know that the hour matters less than the question.

"Yes," I said. "I — yes."

A pause. Then: 

"Mashiba."

She said it the way doctors say names — not as greeting, not as warmth, but as placement. The way you place a pin on a map before you know what you are looking at. Mashiba. Boxer's sister. Nurse. Late call. Athlete.

I waited for the rest of the sentence. It did not come. She was not filling space. She was giving me room to fill it, and the room she gave me was enormous, and I did not know where to stand inside it.

"Yes," I said again. "I — I've been reading about the inhibitory reflex in conditioned athletes, specifically the Golgi tendon organ response under cumulative fatigue, and I — I saw something. In a fight. I saw something that I think — that I now believe might have been —"

"Mashiba-san."

She said it differently this time. Not placement. Interruption. But gentle — the kind of gentle that has nothing soft in it, the kind that cuts because it is precise, because it lands exactly where it needs to.

"Before you tell me what you read, tell me what you saw."

I opened my mouth. Closed it.

She was right. She was already ahead of me. I had been building the sentence backwards — starting from the textbook, from the mechanism, from the language that made it feel clinical and therefore manageable. She wanted the other thing. The thing that came before the book. The thing that sent me to the shelf in the first place.

"I saw a stop," I said.

Silence on her end. Not the kind that asks you to explain. The kind that is already listening.

"In a fight. A title defence. It was — the fourth round, I think, or near it. He was throwing a combination. A rotation. The kind he always throws — he drops low, shifts his weight, and the whole body turns, the punches come from the legs and the hips and the —"

I stopped because I could hear myself. I could hear that I was describing the Dempsey Roll without knowing I was describing the Dempsey Roll. I had never used the name. I did not know the mechanics the way the gym men knew them. But I had watched it enough times to have it living in my body the way music lives in people who do not play instruments — not as knowledge but as recognition, as a shape my eyes had memorised without asking my mind to store it.

"He was mid-rotation," I said. "And he stopped."

"Stopped how?"

Two words. No inflection. She could have been asking about the weather.

"Not — not the way you stop when you are tired. Not the way you slow down. It was sudden. His body just — stopped. Like someone had turned something off inside him. Mid-movement. The punch was there, it was coming, and then it wasn't. His leg — I remember this because I was watching his leg specifically, I don't know why, I think I was watching his leg because something about the way he planted it looked wrong to me even before the combination started — his leg just gave. Not collapsed. Gave. Like the signal to hold him up had been cut."

I was breathing harder than I wanted to be. The room was quiet. The book was still open on the floor beside me and the page was still visible and the paragraph I had read was still there — _the reflex fires earlier, more abruptly, and at the worst possible moment_ — and I was hearing my own words come back to me as if someone else had said them, someone who knew what they meant, someone clinical.

"And then?" she said.

"He recovered. He — everyone said he recovered. They said fatigue. They said pacing. Between rounds he was fine. He came back out and fought and won. But I —"

"You don't think it was fatigue."

It was not a question. It was a door she was holding open for me to walk through or not.

"No," I said. "I don't."

A pause. Longer this time. I could hear something in the background — a clock, maybe, or a pen, or nothing at all. The kind of sound that exists in someone else's room when you are listening too hard to your own breathing.

"The athlete," she said. "This is someone you know personally."

Again — not a question. A line drawn in the air between us that she was letting me cross or not.

"Yes."

"And the rotational movements you described — the weight shift, the drop, the full-body turn — this is a consistent part of his fighting style. Not occasional. Not situational. Foundational."

His. She had said his. She had not asked if the athlete was male. She had listened to the way I described the mechanics — _the kind he always throws_ — and she had heard the word _always_ and she had heard what _always_ means when it is said by someone who has watched it enough times to describe it from memory at midnight on a phone call to a doctor she has never spoken to.

"Yes," I said. "It is — it is the centre of everything he does."

"How long has he been performing this movement under competitive load?"

"Years. Since — since before I knew him. Since the beginning of his career, I think. His coach developed it with him. It is — it was designed for him."

I heard something shift on her end. A chair. A posture. Something physical that changed because something in the conversation had changed.

"Mashiba-san, when you say the movement was designed for him — do you mean adapted to his body, or do you mean built around a pre-existing physical attribute? A low centre of gravity, unusual leg strength, a particular hip flexibility?"

"All of those," I said. And then, because I had to, because the sentence had been building in me since I opened the book and I could not hold it any longer: "He is short for his weight class. He compensates with — with everything. With the legs, the core, the rotation. The whole style is built on generating force that his frame shouldn't be able to produce. And the rotation is how he does it. Every fight. Every round. Over and over. And the book said —"

"Tell me what the book said."

I looked at the page. I did not need to. I had read the sentences enough times that they lived in me now the way the Dempsey Roll lived in me — not as information but as weight.

"It said that muscles adapt faster than tendons. That the forces the muscles can produce outpace what the connective tissue can withstand. And that when the athlete is fatigued — when the tendons are already inflamed from chronic overuse — the Golgi tendon organs lower the threshold and trigger an involuntary shutdown. The reflex fires and the limb gives out. Mid-movement. Without warning."

I swallowed.

"And it said that the momentum the muscle was supposed to absorb gets transferred directly into the joint. Into the tendon. Into the ligament. And that this is the mechanism behind catastrophic tears in athletes who perform high-velocity rotational movements under fatigue."

Silence.

Not the listening kind. Not the placement kind. A different silence. The silence of someone who has heard something she already knew but is hearing it now in a context that changes what it means.

I sat with it. I let it sit. I did not fill it because she had taught me in the first three minutes of this call that silence in a conversation with this woman was not absence — it was work. She was working. She was assembling something on her end that I could not see and would not be shown until it was finished.

"The fight you described," she said finally. "The title defence with the sudden stop. How long ago was that?"

"Recent. Months. I — I don't remember the exact —"

"And since that fight, has he continued competing?"

"Yes."

"At the same intensity? The same rotational volume?"

"Yes. He — he doesn't know how to fight any other way. It is the only style he has. It is —"

"Has anyone discussed this with him? A physician, a trainer, anyone in his corner?"

"His coach told him not to use the rotation too often. But I don't — I don't think he explained why. I don't think the coach knows why in the way the book describes it. He knows it in the way that — that someone who has trained fighters for decades knows when something is wrong with the body. But I don't think anyone has told him the mechanism. I don't think anyone has said the words."

"What words?"

I pressed my hand against my knee. Palms down. Deliberate.

"That his body is trying to protect him. And that the protection is going to destroy him."

The silence this time was the longest. I counted my breaths inside it. Three. Four. Five. On the sixth she spoke.

"Mashiba-san. The athlete you are describing. I need you to understand that I am asking this not because of assumptions, but because specificity changes what I can tell you and what I cannot."

"I understand."

"Is this about Makunouchi?"

She said his name and the room changed. Not physically. The light did not shift. The book did not move. But something in the architecture of the conversation — something that had been held up by indirection, by the careful scaffolding of _the athlete_, _his style_, _the rotation_ — collapsed, and what was left was the bare thing, the actual thing, the thing I had been trying to say since I opened the book.

And I didn't know how to react now, where to put my face and hide it out of embarrassment..
