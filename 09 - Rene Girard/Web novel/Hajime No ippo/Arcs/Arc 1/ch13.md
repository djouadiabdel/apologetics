**

The walk to the Kamogawa gym was not long, but today it felt longer than it should have. 

Every step carried the weight of a question I could not yet form, a question that lived somewhere below thought, in the muscles and the marrow, in the parts of me that had learned to fight before they had learned to speak.

I had not been here in weeks. Not since the Sawamura fight. Not since my leg gave out in a way that did not feel like giving out — it felt like betrayal, like something inside me had chosen the worst possible moment to surrender. But that was behind me now. Or at least, that is what I told myself with every step that brought me closer to the front door.

The street narrowed as I approached. I could hear it before I saw it. The rhythm. That particular rhythm that belongs only to a boxing gym — the staccato crack of leather on leather, the dull thud of fists meeting heavy bags, the sharp exhale of breath leaving lungs in controlled bursts. Underneath all of it, the constant shuffle of feet on canvas, like a pulse, like the building itself had a heartbeat.

I stopped at the entrance.

Somehow, standing there, the nostalgia hit me harder than any punch I had ever taken. Not a sharp pain but a flooding one, warm and wide, filling the spaces between my ribs. The noise of the gym, the punches, the screams, the smell of sweat soaked into wood and leather — it was all so familiar that it ached. These sounds were not just sounds. They were the architecture of my identity. They were the walls of the only home I had ever chosen for myself.

I belong here.

The thought arrived without permission. It settled into me with a certainty that felt almost dangerous, because certainty is the thing that makes you careless, and I could not afford to be careless. Not now. Coach had asked me to come for a reason, and whatever emotion this place stirred in me would have to wait until I knew what that reason was.

Will it be about the next fight? Will it be about extending my rest? Will it be about the plan going forward — the plan that builds on everything before it, layer upon layer, each one designed to bring me closer to the fighter Coach believes I can become?

I did not know. And I hated not knowing. In the ring, I always knew. Not because I was clever — Coach would be the first to tell me I was not — but because I had trained the knowing into my body. Muscle memory. Ten thousand repetitions of the same movement until the movement stops being a decision and becomes a reflex, until the gap between thought and action collapses to nothing. I could remember Coach with his mitts, calling out combinations, his voice sharp and constant like a metronome, instilling the habit into me until the habit became instinct.

That was the thing about Coach. He did not just teach you what to do. He taught you how to trust what you had already done. And standing here now, questioning everything, second-guessing his summons like I was solving a riddle instead of answering a call — this was not how boxing worked. In the ring, you follow the fruits of your practice. You do not hesitate. You do not wonder. You move.

So move.

I pushed the door open.

— • —

The gym opened around me like a living thing. Bags swinging in slow arcs. Rope skipping in staccato bursts in the far corner. Someone shadow-boxing near the mirrors, throwing combinations at their own reflection with a focus that bordered on fury. The air was thick and warm and tasted like effort — salt and rubber and something faintly metallic that I had never been able to name but would have recognised anywhere.

Coach was standing near the back, his arms crossed, watching the room the way he always watched it — not looking at any one thing but seeing everything, the way a conductor watches an orchestra. His eyes found me before I reached him. They always did.

I bowed slightly, the way I always did, because some things do not change even when everything else does.

Ippo: “Hello, Coach. I came as you asked.”

He looked at me for a moment. Not at my face. At the way I was standing. At the way my weight sat on my legs. He was reading me the way he reads fighters — not the words they say but the sentences their bodies write.

Coach: “Hello, kid. Hmm… how are you?”

Ippo: “I am fine.”

A pause. His eyes did not move from my legs.

Coach: “Kid. I meant your injury. Your health.”

I felt my face warm. Of course he meant that. Of course he was not asking about my mood or my morning or whatever trivial thing my mind had jumped to. Coach never asked questions he did not already have a reason for asking.

Ippo: “Ah… it is well, actually. I do not feel hurt at all now. Everything is fine, especially since I have been resting. Doing nothing.”

I tried to say the last part lightly, like it was a joke, like weeks of forced stillness had not been the hardest discipline I had ever practiced. Harder than road work at dawn. Harder than sparring when your legs are dead. Doing nothing, when every part of you screams to do something — that is the fight no one sees.

Coach: “Good. Good, kid. I can see.”

He uncrossed his arms and walked to the bench near the ring apron. He sat down slowly, the way he always did — not because he was fragile, but because he was deliberate. Everything Coach did was deliberate. Every pause. Every breath. He gestured for me to sit.

I sat.

And the gym noise continued around us like a river flowing around two stones.

— • —

Coach: “I asked you to come because I had something important to say to you. After the Sawamura match, you focused on recovery — especially after that issue you had during the fight. The torn muscle fibres. I am sure it has healed by now, but it has lost its conditioning. That is something we will have to work on again, to get you back to where you were before.”

He said it plainly, the way he said all difficult things — without softening, without apology. The injury was a fact. The lost conditioning was a fact. And facts, in Coach’s world, were not things you felt sorry about. They were things you addressed.

I nodded. I thought of the weeks of rest, the walking aid I still carried some days, the strange hollowness in my leg where strength used to live. It would come back. It had to.

Coach leaned forward slightly. His voice dropped, not in volume but in register, the way it did when he was about to say something he had been holding.

Coach: “Do you remember when you had many challengers? When we did not know who to pick from the piles of names we had?”

I did remember. The lists. The phone calls. Manager fielding requests every week, sometimes every day. Names I did not recognise wanting to test themselves against the champion. I remembered the weight of that — the strange, heavy pride of being wanted.

Ippo: “Yes… I remember.”

Coach: “They withdrew their challenges. All of them. They stopped wanting to fight you.”

The words landed in a place I was not prepared for. I felt them settle into the silence between one heartbeat and the next.

They stopped wanting to fight me?

Coach: “Do you know what this means?”

I searched for the answer the way I search for openings in a fight — urgently, instinctively, knowing that the right one was there somewhere if I could just see it. But I could not. The question sat in front of me like a locked door, and every key I tried did not fit.

Ippo: “Well… Coach, I am not sure why.”

He did not look disappointed. If anything, there was something in his expression that resembled patience — the patience of a man who has spent decades teaching young men to understand things they are not yet ready to understand.

Coach: “It is because you stopped being a cheese.”

A cheese?

Coach: “Before the Sawamura fight, I called you a cheese champion. Everyone knew you were faulty. You relied on the Dempsey Roll over and over, and they thought they could defeat you. They thought you were not an unconquerable champion. Just like a piece of cheese, you had holes everywhere. Holes that could be exploited.”

He paused. Let the words breathe.

Coach: “But at that match, you showed everyone how you had improved. You filled the gaps. And now, no one can see something in you that they believe they can beat.”

I felt something shift inside my chest. Something warm and trembling, like a flame being cupped between two hands. The challengers had not left because they had lost interest. They had left because they were afraid. Because I had become something they could not solve.

I actually improved. I actually—

Coach: “STOP.”

The word hit like a jab. Clean. Sharp. Right on the chin of my rising confidence.

Coach: “Do not think highly of yourself. The reason you cannot walk correctly or train right now is because you improvised. You adapted in the ring, yes, but at a cost. You still have not done nearly enough for me. So do not think for a single moment that you have reached the top.”

The flame in my chest did not go out. But it lowered. It steadied. And I understood — not in the way you understand with your mind, but in the deeper way, the way muscle understands repetition — that Coach was not being cruel. He was being precise. He was cutting away the part of me that wanted to rest on a single victory, because resting on a single victory is how you lose the next one.

He is right. He is always right. The injury proves it. The torn fibres, the weeks of recovery, the aid I still carry — these are not the marks of a champion at his peak. These are the marks of a fighter who survived. And surviving is not the same as winning.

Coach’s eyes were on me, and in them I could see something he would never say out loud. Not pride, exactly. Something quieter. Something that lived underneath the sternness the way embers live underneath ash.

I have to show him that his belief in me was not in vain. I cannot be the person who makes him throw the towel again. I cannot be the person who makes him feel like everything he worked for is lost. I will not be that person.

Coach continued, his voice settling back into its teaching register — firm, measured, carrying the weight of decades.

Coach: “We have seen that you over-rely on your weapon. It is good to have a weapon. People looked at your Dempsey Roll and thought you were just moving your head from side to side — easily countered. But you improved it. Now people cannot counter it. That is the essence of having a weapon.”

He held up one finger. One.

Coach: “But you need to keep improving. Improve the basics. Improve yourself. That is the only way for you to become capable of winning against the world. The only way for you to be strong.”

Strong.

The word hung in the air between us, heavier than its two syllables had any right to be. Strong. Not fast. Not technical. Not clever. Strong. As if strength were not a measurement but a destination. As if it were the name of a place I had not yet arrived at, a place Coach had been pointing me toward since the very first day he held up his mitts and told me to hit.

So this is the way for me to be strong? To reach strength?

Coach always did this. He always left me with something burning. Not a plan, not a schedule, not a set of instructions I could follow like a recipe. Something larger. Something that felt like a horizon — always visible, always distant, always pulling me forward. And the thing about a horizon is that you never reach it. You only ever walk toward it. But the walking is the point. The walking is everything.

I am not sure how this will work out. But I know — I know for a fact — that as long as Coach is here, there is no need for me to worry. He was there when I created my weapon. He knew that I had to figure it out myself, but he gave me the tools. He made my legs strong. He made my body strong. All I have to do is follow his words. All I have to do is trust the process.

— • —

Coach’s tone shifted. The teaching voice receded and something more practical took its place.

Coach: “One more thing. You need to go and see Dr. Tomoko. So that we can know whether to resume your training or wait longer.”

The words hit me like a bell.

Training? I can train?

The thought alone sent a current through me, electric and immediate, the kind of excitement that lives in the body before the mind has time to process it. My fingers tightened on my knees. My feet pressed into the floor. Somewhere deep in the muscles that had been sleeping for weeks, something stirred — not movement, not yet, but the memory of movement. The promise of it.

Training. Road work at dawn. The heavy bag. The mitts. The sound of Coach’s voice counting combinations. The burn in my shoulders, the ache in my calves, the sweat running into my eyes until I can barely see and I throw the punch anyway because the body knows where the target is even when the eyes have lost it.

I cannot wait. I cannot wait to see her. I cannot wait at all.

But even as the excitement rose, a quieter thought moved beneath it, careful and measured, the kind of thought that comes from having been hurt.

Didn’t Coach just say everything was healing well? I have been resting. Using the aid. Doing nothing. So why does he need a doctor’s confirmation?

The answer came before I finished asking the question, and it came not as a thought but as a feeling — the ghost-ache in my leg, the memory of that moment in the ring when the muscle tore and the world tilted and for one terrible instant I was falling inside my own body.

Because resting is not the same as being healed. Because feeling fine is not the same as being ready. A professional has to confirm it. Not for my sake — for the work that comes after. So that when I train again, when I push again, when I throw myself into the storm of it all over again, the foundation beneath me holds.

— • —

Coach rose from the bench with the deliberateness that marked everything he did. He walked to the door of the small office, his steps unhurried, his shoulders square. And then, without warning, he pulled the door open with a swiftness that belonged to a much younger man.

Takamura was on the other side.

He was crouched slightly, one ear angled toward where the door had been, his massive frame folded into a posture so absurd, so completely at odds with his status as a world champion, that for a moment the scene refused to make sense. It was as if someone had told a lion to eavesdrop, and the lion had agreed but had no idea how to do it subtly.

Coach’s face went red.

Coach: “YOU! You are here doing childish things instead of training for your next match?! Are you not ashamed of yourself?!”

Takamura straightened to his full height — which was considerable — and crossed his arms with the air of a man who had been caught doing absolutely nothing wrong.

Takamura: “I am the strongest in the world. No one can beat me. You hear that, Ippo? What do you have to say?”

I laughed. I could not help it. It came out before I could stop it — real and involuntary, the kind of laugh that loosens something in your chest that you did not realise was tight.

Ippo: “Ha ha… Takamura, I always look up to you.”

I meant it. Every word. Takamura was ridiculous and enormous and insufferable and the strongest human being I had ever stood next to, and there was something about his presence that made the world feel simpler. As if strength, real strength, could also be joyful. As if it did not always have to be earned through suffering.

Coach lost whatever remained of his patience. His fist connected with the back of Takamura’s head — a blow that would have felled a lesser man but merely caused Takamura to stumble forward with an exaggerated yelp.

Coach: “Idiot! Get back to training! Now!”

Takamura retreated toward the heavy bags, muttering loudly about being underappreciated, about how legends deserved better treatment, about how Coach would regret this when he was old — though Coach was already old, which made the threat particularly empty.

I watched him go. And I smiled. Not the small, careful smile of politeness. A real one. The kind that uses the whole face.

— • —

I walked out of the office and into the gym.

The change was immediate. Or maybe the change had always been there and I was only now allowing myself to notice it. The gym shifted around me the way a room shifts when someone turns on a light — not the furniture, not the walls, but the quality of the air, the texture of the space, the way everything in it suddenly becomes sharper and more present.

Itagaki was in the corner. I saw him before he saw me — or rather, I saw him in the instant before he allowed himself to be seen, that brief unguarded moment when he was simply looking, watching me come through the door with an expression I could not name but that sat somewhere between relief and anticipation.

Then he noticed me noticing him, and the expression was gone, replaced by something more composed. But the first one was real. I knew it was real.

The noise of the gym rose around me. Everything was louder. The crack of the bags. The shuffle of feet. Someone counting reps in a voice that cracked with effort. The rattle of the speed bag like a mechanical heartbeat. The squeak of shoes on canvas. All of it — layered and constant and chaotic and alive.

And in that loudness, I was not annoyed. I was not overwhelmed. I stood in the middle of it and I let it fill me the way water fills a glass — completely, all at once, pressing against every edge.

Takamura being childish in the corner, still muttering, throwing punches at a bag with a force that made the chain rattle like it was afraid. Coach shouting at someone else now, his voice carrying above the noise like a foghorn. The younger fighters stealing glances at me, trying not to stare, failing. Itagaki shadowboxing with a rhythm so clean it looked like music made physical.

This was my family. Not the family you are born into, not the one that gathers around a table on holidays and passes the rice and asks you about your day. This was the family you build — the one you earn, punch by punch, round by round, year by year. The one that knows you not by your name but by your hands, by the sound your feet make on the canvas, by the way you breathe when you are tired.

I cannot wait to go to the doctor.

The thought was simple. Clean. Uncluttered by doubt. It sat in me with the same certainty that had greeted me at the door — the certainty of belonging, of purpose, of a path that was not finished yet.

I cannot wait to come back to this.

I walked toward the exit, and with every step I could feel it — the gym pulling at me, gently, the way gravity pulls at everything that belongs to the earth. Not holding me back. Just reminding me where I belonged.

The door closed behind me. The noise softened. The street opened wide and ordinary and full of daylight.

And I walked toward Dr. Tomoko with something in my chest that felt, for the first time in weeks, like the opposite of fear.
