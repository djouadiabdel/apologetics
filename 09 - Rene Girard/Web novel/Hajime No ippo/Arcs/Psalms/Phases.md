# **KUMI'S ARC — The woman who knew first.**

Station 1 (Imai Spar) — She's not present for the spar but she's living in the aftermath of the clinic visits. She gave her book to Yamaguchi. She told Ippo she'd help him. But now she's in limbo — she did the thing and nothing visible happened. The doubt here is did any of it matter? She goes back to her nursing shifts and tries to focus but she's reading every patient's chart differently now. She's seeing ticking bombs everywhere. A man comes in with a knee complaint and she thinks about Ippo. An athlete visits the ward and she watches how they walk. Her professional eye is sharpening without her permission.

Station 2 (Takamura's Eye) — Ippo comes to her and asks for a book about eye problems. She gives it to him. And something shifts — because now Ippo is doing for Takamura exactly what she did for Ippo. He's investigating a hidden injury in someone he loves. He's scared the way she was scared. And he doesn't see the mirror. She does. She hands him the book and watches him leave and for the first time she feels the loneliness of being the person who connects dots that nobody else is connecting. She could say "Ippo, this is what I've been doing for you" — but she doesn't. Because saying it would mean explaining what she found and she's not ready for that conversation.

Station 3 (Eagle and the Title Fight) — The spectacle. The arena. She's there because Ippo's world is there. But she's not watching the fight the way everyone else watches it. She's watching bodies. Takamura's body specifically — after the eye investigation she's now hyper-aware of damage in fighters. She watches Takamura take hits to his right side and she flinches in a way that has nothing to do with entertainment. Her brother Mashiba might be there too and she watches him and thinks about how every fighter she loves is carrying something they won't name. This is where her arc stops being about Ippo specifically and becomes about her relationship with the entire sport. She's becoming something. She doesn't have a word for it yet.

Station 4 (Itagaki's Tournament) — Quieter for her. She's on the periphery. But this is where you develop her and Yamaguchi's relationship. She visits the clinic again — not with new information, just to talk. Yamaguchi treats her differently now. Not as a patient, not as a young nurse with a crush, but as a colleague with instincts. Yamaguchi starts teaching her things. Not formally — just in conversation. Mentions a study. Recommends a book. Asks her opinion on something and actually listens to the answer. Kumi starts to realize that what she's becoming has a shape. This is the mentorship arc beginning.

Station 5 (Karasawa Fight) — She watches Ippo fight and he doesn't use the Dempsey Roll. Her heart lifts. He listened. He's protecting himself. Then after the fight Takamura asks why and Ippo says it was shortening his career. She hears those words and they hit her differently than they hit everyone else — because she knows the medical reason underneath Ippo's instinct. He's right for the wrong reasons. Or the right reasons that he can't articulate. And she's proud and terrified at the same time because limiting the Dempsey Roll is not the same as fixing the underlying problem. It's like putting tape over a crack in a wall.

Station 6 (Miyata Decision) — Ippo chooses more fighting. Relinquishes the belt to chase the Miyata match. Kumi hears this and something breaks a little. Not dramatically — quietly. She thought the Karasawa fight meant he was slowing down. He wasn't slowing down. He was redirecting. More fights, different fights, but still fights. She doesn't confront him. She goes to Yamaguchi and for the first time she says something like "he's not going to stop on his own, is he?" And Yamaguchi's silence is the answer.

Station 7 (Take Fight) — The nightmare. Ippo gets cut. Gets battered. Uses the Dempsey Roll again after saying he sealed it. Kumi watches this — maybe from the arena, maybe from a screen, but she watches it — and everything she feared confirms itself. "Try my very best" meant nothing. The body takes over. The ring takes over. The promise dissolves the moment the bell rings. This is Kumi's lowest point. Not anger — grief. The grief of watching someone you love choose the thing that's destroying them and knowing that the choice isn't even conscious anymore. It's reflex. It's who he is.

Station 8 (Aftermath into Confrontation) — Kumi has changed. She's not the scared girl who walked to the clinic avoiding the route. She's someone with knowledge, with a mentor, with months of watching and learning and hurting. When she finally learns who the blond man is — through Yamaguchi — she doesn't panic. She nods. Because of course Yamaguchi sent someone. Of course it wasn't enough to just tell Ippo. Of course someone had to watch. Kumi's arc ends this phase not with an answer but with a position — she's no longer on the outside looking in. She's part of the apparatus trying to save him. Whether that apparatus works is the next phase of your story.


# KAMOGAWA'S ARC — The man who loves wrong.

Station 1 (Imai Spar) — He watches Ippo struggle against Imai and he's not worried. He's calculating. He sees the gaps and he sees what training will fix them. This is Kamogawa in his element — problems are solvable, fighters are moldable, the path is clear. He ordered the spar for a reason. He wanted Ippo humbled so that Ippo would train harder. Everything is strategy. The injury report from Yamaguchi is a piece of paper in his desk drawer and it stays there.

Station 2 (Takamura's Eye) — Kamogawa is focused on Takamura now. The Eagle fight is coming. He's managing two fighters at world-level aspirations and his attention splits. But here's the thing — he's worried about Takamura's eye in the way he's NOT worried about Ippo's body. That inconsistency is important. He can see danger in Takamura because Takamura hides things aggressively and obviously. Ippo hides things by not knowing they exist. Kamogawa's blind spot is the fighter who doesn't complain.

Station 3 (Eagle and the Title Fight) — His peak. Takamura wins the middleweight belt. Kamogawa's philosophy is validated. Suffering produced greatness. Pushing past limits produced a world champion. This is dangerous because it reinforces everything he believes. If it worked for Takamura, it will work for Ippo. The reader should feel the triumph and the dread simultaneously.

Station 4 (Itagaki's Tournament) — He's managing Itagaki now too. Spreading himself across multiple fighters. But with Itagaki he does something interesting — he forbids Ippo from telling Itagaki he looks like a pro. He understands that praise too early kills growth. This shows his genius. He's not a fool. He's a brilliant coach with one devastating blind spot and that blind spot has a name.

Station 5 (Karasawa Fight) — Ippo wins without the Dempsey Roll. Kamogawa should be proud and he is. But there's a splinter. Ippo chose to seal it on his own. Kamogawa didn't instruct that. Ippo made a strategic decision about his own body without asking his coach. For a man like Kamogawa, who believes the coach's word is law, this is a tiny crack. He doesn't address it. He reframes it as maturity. But somewhere underneath he registers that Ippo is thinking about his own body in a way he didn't before. And that thinking didn't come from Kamogawa. It came from somewhere else.

Station 6 (Miyata Decision) — Ippo asks to relinquish the belt. Kamogawa accepts. He plans the path — defend once more then fight Miyata in spring. This is Kamogawa the architect. He's building a road to the world and Miyata is the next stepping stone. He's energized. He's planning training regimens. The injury drawer stays closed.

Station 7 (Take Fight) — The war. Ippo gets brutalized. Gets cut. Uses the Dempsey Roll again. Kamogawa watches from the corner and he sees the damage in real time. The cut. The diminished power. The moment where Ippo's rotation wasn't what it should be. And in the corner between rounds, Ippo begs him to keep going. And Kamogawa lets him. Because that's what Kamogawa does. He lets fighters fight. He almost threw the towel once before and Ippo proved him wrong. So he trusts. And that trust is beautiful and it is also the thing that's going to get challenged at Station 8.

Station 8 (Aftermath into Confrontation) — Kamogawa sits with the results of the Take fight. Ippo won but Ippo's body paid a price. The training for the Miyata fight needs to start. The numbers of fights, the accumulated damage, the recovery windows getting shorter. He doesn't panic. He plans around it. He adjusts training. He thinks he's managing it. And then the blond man walks in and tells him he's not managing anything — he's accelerating it.
