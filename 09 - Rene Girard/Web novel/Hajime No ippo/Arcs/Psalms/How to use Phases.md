**Who is holding the camera?** Every chapter belongs to one person's eyes. Not two, not three. One. Decide that before you write a single word. If it's Kumi's chapter, everything in that chapter — the environment, the details she notices, the way she describes a punch versus how Itagaki would describe it — filters through her. A nurse sees a bruise differently than a fan does. A mother sees a tired face differently than a coach does. The camera choice decides what the chapter notices and what it misses. What it misses is just as important because another character's chapter will catch it later.

**What station am I near?** You don't need to be at a station. You can be between stations. But you should know which one you just left and which one you're walking toward. If you're between the Imai spar and the Takamura eye investigation, then the emotional weather of your story is "Ippo just got humbled and nobody knows what's coming next." Every character is living inside that weather differently. Kumi is processing the clinic aftermath. Kamogawa is planning training. Itagaki is replaying the spar. You don't need to address the station directly — just know that it's coloring everything.

Then while you're writing, keep one rule: **every chapter should move at least one character's arc forward by one step, even if that character isn't holding the camera.** If Itagaki is holding the camera and the chapter is about training, somewhere in that chapter Itagaki should notice something about someone else — Ippo's posture, Kamogawa's tone, a blond man in the corner — that plants a seed for that other person's arc. Not a big moment. A sentence. A glance. Something the reader stores without knowing why yet.

And the character arc map I gave you — don't memorize it. Don't check it paragraph by paragraph. Just reread the relevant character's arc from the map before your writing session. Read Kumi's full arc before a Kumi chapter. Read Kamogawa's before a Kamogawa chapter. Then close it and write. The map should change how you see the scene before you start, the same way your three writing principles work — not as conscious rules mid-sentence but as lenses that shift what you notice before the first word hits the page.

That's it. Camera, station, one arc moved forward, reread the relevant arc before you sit down. Everything else is you.
