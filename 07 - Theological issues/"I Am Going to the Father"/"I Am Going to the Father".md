## The Addition — "I Am Going to the Father"

Now here is the powerful passage you want added. Jesus says this multiple times in John 14-17 and it is one of the most misunderstood clusters of verses in all of apologetics.

**John 14:28:**

> _"You heard me say, 'I am going away and I am coming back to you.' If you loved me, you would be glad that I am going to the Father, for **the Father is greater than I.**"_

Muslims and Jehovah's Witnesses use "the Father is greater than I" to argue Jesus is not God — a subordinate being, lesser in nature.

## What "Greater" Actually Means Here

The Greek word is **μείζων (meizōn)** — greater in position, rank, or role — not greater in **nature or essence.**

This is a critical Greek distinction:

- **Greater in nature (ousia)** = a different, superior kind of being. This would mean the Son has a lesser divine nature — subordinationism.
    
- **Greater in role/position (taxis)** = the same nature, but operating in a different function. A general is greater in rank than a soldier of equal humanity.
    

John 14:28 is about **position in the economy of salvation**, not nature. Here is how you prove it from the same passage:

**John 14:9:**

> _"Anyone who has seen me has seen the Father."_

If the Father is greater in **nature**, then seeing Jesus should not be equivalent to seeing the Father — they would be different kinds of beings. But Jesus says seeing him = seeing the Father. That is a claim to **shared nature**, not lesser nature.

**John 10:30:**

> _"I and the Father are **one** (ἕν — hen)."_

The Greek **hen** is neuter — not "one person" (which would be masculine heis) but **one thing, one essence, one being.** This is a claim to shared divine nature. Three verses later the Jews pick up stones for blasphemy — they understood it as a claim to equality with God.

So in the **same Gospel of John:**

- "I and the Father are one" (nature)
    
- "Anyone who has seen me has seen the Father" (nature)
    
- "The Father is greater than I" (role/position)
    

These are not contradictions. They describe **two different dimensions** of the same reality — same nature, different functional role in the incarnation.

## The Philippians 2 Key — Again

Philippians 2:6-8 explains John 14:28 perfectly:

> _"Who, though he was in the **form of God**, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but **emptied himself**, taking the form of a **servant**..."_

The Son **voluntarily subordinated himself** to the Father for the mission of redemption. The Father is greater **in the context of the incarnation** — the Son came as servant, the Father sent as master. This is not a permanent ontological hierarchy. It is a **functional hierarchy for the purpose of salvation.**

Paul confirms this ends after the mission is complete — **1 Corinthians 15:24-28:**

> _"Then the end will come, when he hands over the kingdom to God the Father... When he has done this, then the Son himself will be made subject to him who put everything under him, **so that God may be all in all.**"_

Even this final "subject to" is not inferiority — it is the completion of the redemptive mission, the Son presenting the finished work to the Father, the eternal triune life resuming its full expression. "God may be all in all" — not the Father alone, but God — the full Trinity.

## What "Going to the Father" Means

When Jesus says "I am going to the Father," he is describing:

1. **The completion of the servant role** — the humiliation of Philippians 2:7-8 is ending
    
2. **The return to shared glory** — John 17:5: _"Father, glorify me in your presence with the glory I had with you before the world began."_ He is returning to the glory he voluntarily set aside.
    
3. **The disciples should rejoice, not mourn** — because the servant-state limitations are ending. The one who said "nor the Son" in Mark 13:32 is returning to the full expression of his divine nature.
    

This is why he says **"if you loved me, you would be glad"** — going to the Father is not diminishment, it is restoration. The disciples are sad because they are losing him physically. Jesus says: understand what this means — the humiliation is ending, the glory is returning.

## The Muslim Trap This Creates

Now present this to the Muslim:

> "Jesus says 'the Father is greater than I' because he is in the servant-role of the incarnation — voluntarily subordinated for the mission. But in John 17:5, he prays to the Father to restore the glory he had **before the world existed.** A mere prophet does not have pre-existent glory with God before creation. A servant of God does not say 'restore to me the divine glory I shared with you before the universe began.' Either Jesus is lying — in which case Islam is false because he is a false prophet — or he is telling the truth, in which case he is the pre-existent divine Son returning to his eternal glory. Which is it?"

There is no third option.
