## The claim under test

"Jesus accepted worship, and only God may be worshipped, therefore Jesus is God."

I ran this against a debate transcript on 5 Aug 2026 and it does not hold. The argument should be retired in that form. Something better survives underneath it, and the something better is not a word study.

## The lexical facts

**προσκυνέω** *proskyneō* is the word translated "worship" in nearly every relevant verse. BDAG glosses it "(fall down and) worship, do obeisance to, prostrate oneself before, do reverence to," and lists human and divine recipients under one entry. It denotes a physical act of prostration before a superior. Context, not the word, supplies who the superior is.

**λατρεύω** *latreuō* is the narrower word for cultic religious service.

## The rebuttal that ends the popular version

Every one of these is προσκυνέω in the Septuagint, offered to someone who is not God.

| Recipient | Reference |
|---|---|
| Two angels, by Lot | Gen 19:1 |
| The Hittites, by Abraham | Gen 23:7, 23:12 |
| Jacob, in Isaac's blessing of him | Gen 27:29 |
| Esau, by Jacob | Gen 33:3 |
| Joseph, by his brothers | Gen 42:6 |
| David's servants, by Abigail | 1 Sam 25:41 |
| The dead Samuel, by Saul | 1 Sam 28:14 |
| Elisha, by the sons of the prophets | 2 Kgs 2:15 |
| **King David, by all Israel** | **1 Chr 29:20** |

And inside the New Testament: a master by his servant in a parable Jesus himself tells (Matt 18:26), and the church's opponents before the church (Rev 3:9).

**1 Chr 29:20 is the one that decides it.** The Greek has the assembly προσκυνέω "to the LORD and to the king" in a single construction governed by one verb. If receiving προσκυνέω entails deity, that verse makes Israel worship David as God. Nobody holds that. So the inference is invalid, and it takes one verse to show it.

## Four more facts that go the same way

**The counts are low and awkward.** Mark has προσκυνέω exactly twice, and neither helps: 5:6 is the Gerasene demoniac, and 15:19 is Roman soldiers mocking Jesus as king of the Jews. That second one is evidence about what the gesture meant, since you mock a pretended king with the gesture owed to kings. Luke has it three times, twice inside the temptation dialogue and once at 24:52 after the ascension. John has Jesus receiving it once, at 9:38.

**John 9:38 is missing from 𝔓75, א\*, and W**, plus some Old Latin and Coptic. A number of scholars who are not unitarians treat it as a liturgical expansion. This is the strongest single textual point on that side of the argument and it should be conceded rather than fought.

**"Only" attaches to the serving, not the worshipping.** Matt 4:10 and Luke 4:8 read Κύριον τὸν θεόν σου προσκυνήσεις καὶ αὐτῷ μόνῳ λατρεύσεις. The adjective μόνῳ is dative, agreeing with αὐτῷ inside the λατρεύσεις clause. Strictly parsed, the exclusivity is on λατρεύω. The verse is quoted constantly as "worship God only" and that is not what it says.

**And the quotation is altered.** Deut 6:13 LXX has φοβηθήσῃ, "you shall fear." The gospels substitute προσκυνήσεις. Worth knowing before someone else points it out.

## Where the unitarian version fails

**Revelation 22:3.** "The throne of God and of the Lamb will be in the city, and his servants will serve him," λατρεύσουσιν αὐτῷ, a singular pronoun following a compound subject that names God and the Lamb. Anyone claiming λατρεύω is never offered to Jesus in the New Testament has to deal with this verse, and the flat version of the claim does not survive it.

**Revelation cuts the other way twice more.** Rev 5:8-14 has the Lamb receive the throne room acclamation alongside the one seated on the throne, with prostration and προσκυνέω at 5:14. And Rev 22:8-9 has John rebuked for προσκυνεῖν an angel, which proves the book does police the gesture in cultic settings. Rev 3:9 is the weakest instance in Revelation, and it is the one the unitarian argument reaches for.

**Luke 24:52's textual problem is thin.** The omission is essentially Codex Bezae plus some Old Latin, against 𝔓75, א, B and A. Filing it beside the Mark ending and the pericope adulterae flattens a real difference in evidential weight. Check whether the objection has been confused with **Luke 24:51**, where the ascension clause is the genuinely bracketed reading. That item is already in my own ledger.

**The zero counts are true of the word and false of the gesture.** Luke has Peter fall at Jesus's knees (5:8), Jairus at his feet (8:41), the healed leper on his face at his feet (17:16). Mark has προσπίπτω at 3:11, 5:33 and 7:25. If the argument is that προσκυνέω means bodily prostration rather than cultic worship, then counting only προσκυνέω while ignoring the synonymous prostrations is inconsistent with its own semantics.

**The best unitarian point half disarms itself.** "Why does nobody προσκυνέω Jesus after the ascension?" followed by "because it refers to a physical action." If the answer is that the gesture needs a body in front of you, then the post-ascension silence tells you about bodies, not about the word's semantic force, and it cannot also serve as proof that the word never meant cultic worship.

## The structural point, which is the whole thing

**A single-lexeme word study cannot settle a question about practice.** Hurtado's case in *Lord Jesus Christ* (2003) and *At the Origins of Christian Worship* (1999) is not built on προσκυνέω at all. It is built on a pattern of behaviour:

- Prayer addressed to Jesus (Acts 7:59-60, Stephen praying "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit")
- Calling on his name as the community's self-identifying practice (Acts 9:14, 22:16, 1 Cor 1:2)
- *Maranatha*, untranslated Aramaic preserved inside a Greek letter to a Greek city, so from the pre-Greek stratum (1 Cor 16:22)
- Doxologies directed to Christ (Rev 1:5-6)
- Hymns, the supper held in his name, baptism into his name
- 1 Cor 8:6 restructuring the Shema itself around him

You can win every word count in the New Testament and still lose that. And an argument that "the word does not appear" does not touch a claim about what people did.

**So drop the worship argument and argue the practice.** It is stronger, it does not die to one verse from Chronicles, and it is where the actual scholarship lives. Bauckham's divine-identity framework in *Jesus and the God of Israel* is the direct answer to the whole word-study strategy, and neither side of the debate I checked mentioned it once.

## Two things the other side got wrong, worth having ready

**Exodus 33 does not say what it is usually quoted as saying.** The appeal to Yahweh saying "I will not go with you" as proof that Yahweh identified himself as the angel of the Lord runs into Exod 33:2-3, which contrasts the angel with Yahweh's own presence, and Moses objects at 33:15 precisely because an angel is not enough. The name-bearing passage is Exod 23:20-21. Citing Exod 33 here hands the verse to the opponent.

**κύριε κύριε is weaker than it is presented.** At Luke 6:46 the doubled vocative is what other people say to Jesus, not a self-designation. The LXX does use it for אדני יהוה, and it also functions as a plain Semitic emphatic, and Matt 7:22 has people saying it to someone who replies that he never knew them. Real datum, routinely overstated.

**Phil 2:6-7 is where the good argument is**, and it does not need translation help. The sequence runs from being in the form of God, to emptying, to taking the form of a slave, to being found in human likeness, which puts the humbling before the human existence and is very hard to fit to a man reflecting on being in God's image. Hurtado, Bauckham, Fee and Wright all reject Dunn's Adamic reading on that ground, and Dunn softened later. Hoover's 1971 study of the ἁρπαγμός idiom supports "something to be exploited," now the majority reading. And "every knee shall bow" at 2:10 is lifted from Isa 45:23, in a chapter where Yahweh says there is no other. **Do not lean on the NIV's "in very nature God" as though it were the Greek.** NRSV and ESV both have "in the form of God," and the structural argument wins without the help.
