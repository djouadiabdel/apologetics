## 0. What this document is for

There is one famous philosophical objection to the Trinity, usually called the Logical Problem of the Trinity (LPT). It claims the doctrine is not merely unproven but self-contradictory, the way a square circle is. If it succeeds, no amount of scripture helps, because scripture cannot make a contradiction true.

This file has the objection in its strong form, the answer, the formal notation that makes the answer precise, a glossary of every symbol, and an honest map of where the argument is actually still contested.

Read section 1 and stop. Try to feel the force of it before you read the answer. If the objection doesn't bother you at all, you have understood the weak version and you will lose to someone who has the strong one.

---

## 1. The objection

### The weak form (what you usually hear)

1. The Father is God.
2. The Son is God.
3. The Holy Spirit is God.
4. There is exactly one God.
5. By transitivity of identity, from 1 and 2, the Father is the Son.
6. But the creed says the Father is not the Son.
7. So the doctrine contradicts itself.

### The strong form (what you should assume you are facing)

The weak form dies fast, for reasons in section 2, and anyone who has read philosophy of religion knows it. The version to prepare for does not use identity at all. It uses counting.

1. The Father is divine.
2. The Son is divine.
3. The Holy Spirit is divine.
4. The Father is not the Son, the Son is not the Spirit, the Spirit is not the Father.
5. So there are at least three distinct things that are divine.
6. But there is exactly one divine thing.
7. Contradiction.

Notice what changed. Nothing in lines 1 to 3 says the Father *is identical to* God. They only say he has the divine nature. So the reply "you equivocated on the word is" does not touch this version at all. This is the objection as Dale Tuggy presses it, and it is the one worth your time.

**Rule: never state the objection in the weak form when arguing publicly.** Give the strong form. If you beat a version your opponent didn't hold, you have won nothing and you look like you're avoiding him. State his case better than he did, then answer it. That is the whole of not strawmanning.

## 2. First move: the word "is" does two jobs

This clears the weak form, and it is worth having even though it does not clear the strong one.

**Identity.** "2 + 2 is 4." Both sides are fully interchangeable. Anywhere you can write 4 you can write 2 + 2. Nothing is left over on either side. The older vocabulary for this is *adequate identity*, meaning each term exhausts the whole intelligible content of the other.

**Predication.** "Abraham is human." True, and fully true, since Abraham is not a partial human. But *human* is not used up by Abraham. Isaac is human too. This is *inadequate identity*, meaning the two terms are really identified but one does not exhaust the other.

Transitivity, the rule that two things equal to a third are equal to each other, is a rule about identity. Feed it predications and it produces nonsense with no theology involved:

> Abraham is human. Isaac is human. Therefore Abraham is Isaac.

Nobody accepts that. So step 5 of the weak form is invalid on its face.

**Field test.** Reverse the sentence. "4 is 2 + 2" works. "Human is Abraham" is gibberish. If it will not reverse, it is not identity, and transitivity has no grip.

## 3. Second move: the tritheism comeback

Your opponent now says the obvious thing, and he is right to:

> Fine. Then Father and Son stand to divinity as Abraham and Isaac stand to humanity. Abraham and Isaac are two men. So Father and Son are two gods.

This is the real argument. Everything before it was clearing ground.

### The answer

Ask why Abraham and Isaac are two rather than one. It is not because they are human, since that is precisely what they share. They are two because of how human nature gets passed on.

A form like human nature does not exist on its own. It has to be received into something that individuates it, in this case matter. So instantiation is a two-place operation, a nature *plus* a receiver:

$$\iota(\text{nature},\ \text{receiver}) \longrightarrow \text{individual}$$

New receiver, new individual. Every generation supplies a new one. That is why humans multiply when human nature is communicated.

The divine nature has no second argument. There is no matter in God, no potency, nothing standing by to receive and limit the essence. The divine essence is already individual in itself, so there is nothing for $\iota$ to vary over. It is not that God's nature is an enormous supply that happens never to run out. It is that the operation which multiplies natures has no input to work with in the first place.

So when the Father communicates the divine nature to the Son, no second instance is produced. The Son has the same essence, numerically the same, not a duplicate of the same type.

**Three whos, one what.** With humans you get three whos and three whats. That difference is the entire answer, and if you can only remember one sentence, remember that one.

## 4. The formal version

### 4.1 The instance-count function

Let $f_N(n)$ be the number of numerically distinct instances of nature $N$ in existence after $n$ acts of communication.

For a finite nature such as humanity:

$$f_H(n) = n$$

For the divine nature:

$$f_D(n) = 1$$

That is the disanalogy in two lines. $f_H$ climbs. $f_D$ is a constant function.

### 4.2 The communication operator

Write $\oplus$ for "another possessor comes to have this nature."

Finite nature, **additive**:

$$m \oplus m = 2m$$

Divine nature, **idempotent**:

$$D \oplus D = D$$

Idempotence is the algebraic signature of something communicated whole without producing a second of it. In ordinary algebra an idempotent element is one where $e \oplus e = e$, and that is exactly the behaviour claimed here. If you want one line on a slide, use that.

### 4.3 The two counts

This is the deepest form, and the one that answers the *strong* objection rather than the weak one. The point is that the doctrine's "one" and its "three" are counts over two different domains.

$$|\{x : x \text{ is God}\}| = 1$$

$$|\{r : r \text{ is a subsistent relation in the divine essence}\}| = 3$$

For creatures these two counts collapse into each other. The set of human natures and the set of human persons have the same number of members, which is exactly why tritheism-style reasoning is valid when applied to men.

In God they come apart. One counts instances of an essence. The other counts relations of origin within that one essence. The strong objection assumes without argument that these are one count. That assumption is the load-bearing move, and naming it is how you answer step 5.

### 4.4 Why exactly three

Three is not chosen to fit a text. It falls out of the structure.

Two processions, generation and spiration. Each yields two relations, one in each direction, so four in total: paternity, filiation, active spiration, passive spiration. But persons are really distinct only where there is *relative opposition*, meaning one relation is the mirror of the other. Active spiration is not opposed to paternity or filiation, because Father and Son spirate together. So it founds no distinct person and collapses into them.

$$4 \text{ relations} - 1 \text{ non-opposed} = 3 \text{ persons}$$

Not two, not seventeen. When someone asks why the number three, this is the answer, and it is far better than they expect.

## 5. Glossary of every symbol and term

### Symbols

| Symbol | Read it as | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $N$ | "some nature" | A nature in general, standing in for either $H$ or $D$ |
| $H$ | "humanity" | Human nature, the standard example of a finite nature |
| $D$ | "deity" | The divine essence |
| $n$ | "n" | How many times the nature has been communicated |
| $f_N(n)$ | "f of N at n" | How many numerically distinct instances of $N$ exist after $n$ communications |
| $\iota$ | "iota", individuation | The operation that turns a nature into a particular individual |
| $\oplus$ | "communicated to another" | The operation of another supposit coming to possess the nature |
| $m$ | "m" | A finite quantity of nature, one share of it |
| $\|\{x : P(x)\}\|$ | "the number of x such that P" | Cardinality, how many things satisfy the description inside |
| $\aleph_0$ | "aleph-null" | The size of the set of natural numbers. Appears here only as a warning, see 6.1 |

### Terms

**Nature (ousia, essence).** *What* a thing is. Answers "what is it." Divinity is a nature.

**Person (hypostasis, supposit).** *Who* a thing is. The one who has the nature and does the acting. Not a part of the nature and not an instance of a kind, in God's case.

**Supposit.** The technical word for the individual subject that possesses a nature. Every person is a supposit; not every supposit is a person (a rock is a supposit of rock-nature).

**Adequate identity.** Two terms where each exhausts the whole intelligible content of the other. $2+2$ and $4$.

**Inadequate identity.** Two terms really identified, where one does not exhaust the other. Abraham and humanity.

**Act and potency.** Potency is capacity-to-be-otherwise, the room in a thing for something to be received. God is pure act, meaning zero potency. This is why nothing in God can receive and limit the essence, which is the whole basis of section 3.

**Procession.** The eternal origin of one person from another. Two of them: generation (Son from Father) and spiration (Spirit from Father and Son).

**Relative opposition.** Two relations that are mirrors of each other, like begetting and being begotten. Only these found really distinct persons.

**Subsistent relation.** The claim that in God a relation does not inhere in a person but *is* the person. The Father just is paternity subsisting. This is the hardest claim in the system and the one that carries the most weight. See section 7.

## 6. Traps that will lose you the argument

### 6.1 Do not use cardinal arithmetic

You will be tempted, because $\aleph_0 + \aleph_0 = \aleph_0$ looks like it proves infinity can be shared without increase. It does not say that. It says $|A| = |B|$, that two sets are the same *size*. It does not say $A = B$.

Three infinite sets of equal cardinality are still three sets. So this models three Gods of equal infinite greatness, which is tritheism with better manners, and it gives away the exact thing the doctrine requires, namely numerical identity of essence. A good opponent will see this in seconds and you will have handed him the argument.

### 6.2 Do not build a mathematical model of the whole doctrine

Every attempt collapses in one of two directions. Anything built on identity gives you one thing under three descriptions, which is modalism. Anything built on set membership or type and token gives you three things of one kind, which is tritheism. Mathematics has those two relations and little else, and the doctrine needs something that is neither.

So $1 \times 1 \times 1 = 1$ is not an argument. It is the number one written three times.

Use the formalism only for the narrow claim, that communicating an infinite nature is idempotent while communicating a finite one is additive. That claim is defensible and the notation genuinely sharpens it. Then stop.

### 6.3 Do not say "quasi-universal"

You will find this in popular presentations. A Thomist will push back, because the divine essence is not a universal at all, not even approximately, and calling it one invites the objection you were trying to avoid. Say instead that the divine essence is not the sort of thing that gets instantiated, because there is no potency in it to receive limitation. Same point, less exposed.

### 6.4 Do not claim more than the argument delivers

This is a *defensive* argument. It shows that no contradiction has been demonstrated, because the objection needed a counting assumption it never earned. It does not prove the Trinity. Say so out loud. Claiming less makes you much harder to knock over, and it is also true.

### 6.5 Answer the objection they made

Restate their version in your own words and ask if you have it right, before you answer. If they gave you the counting form, do not answer the transitivity form. Half of what looks like winning online is just answering a different argument fast.

## 7. Where the fight actually is, unresolved

Do not paper over this. The honest position is that section 4.3 is contested by serious people arguing in good faith.

The pressure point is this. The Father is identical to the divine essence. The Son is identical to the divine essence. If both of those are *identity* claims, transitivity is back and the answer in section 2 has bought nothing. The reply is that the persons are identical to the essence inadequately, not adequately, which is what the whole apparatus of subsistent relations is for. Whether "inadequate identity" is a coherent relation or a name for the problem is precisely the disputed question.

Two families of modern answer, so you know the terrain:

- **Relative identity** (Peter Geach, developed by Peter van Inwagen). There is no such thing as identity full stop, only identity *relative to a sortal*. Father and Son are the same God but not the same person. Powerful, and it requires giving up classical absolute identity, which many find too expensive.
- **Latin / constitution models** (Brower and Rea). Persons stand to essence as a statue stands to the bronze it is made of, the same matter constituting numerically distinct things. Elegant, and criticised for importing a part-whole relation that does not belong in a simple God.

Gregory of Nyssa's *Ad Ablabium* is the fourth-century version of this same argument and is already in your reading plan. Aquinas is at *Summa Theologiae* I, qq. 27 to 30 and q. 39. Augustine, *De Trinitate* V to VII, is where the relation move is invented.

## 8. Questions to answer yourself

Do not look these up yet. Write your own answers in the margin, dated, and come back to them after Nyssa.

1. In the strong objection, step 5 infers "at least three distinct things that are divine" from steps 1 to 4. Which word in that step is doing the illegitimate work, and can you say why in one sentence without using any technical vocabulary?

2. Section 3 says there is no receiver in God because there is no potency. But where did you get "no potency in God" from? Is it a premise smuggled in to save the Trinity, or is it established somewhere else first? Your answer to this decides whether the whole argument is ad hoc.

3. Section 4.4 derives three persons from two processions. Why exactly two processions, and not three? What would a third procession have to be?

4. If a Muslim interlocutor grants everything in sections 2 through 4 and then says "you have shown it is not contradictory, but a nature that subsists in three relations is still not what the Shema means by one," what have you actually got left to say to him? Is that a philosophical question any more?
