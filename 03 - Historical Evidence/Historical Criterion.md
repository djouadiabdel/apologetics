## Why We Use a Point System At All

Before Criterion 1 even starts, you need to establish why scoring exists. Otherwise he'll say "you just made up numbers."

**The reason is this:**

When two people disagree about which explanation is better, they are usually doing one of three things:

1. Emphasizing different facts
    
2. Applying different standards secretly
    
3. Demanding certainty from one side and accepting vagueness from the other
    

A scoring grid forces both people to:

- Agree on what the facts are **before** evaluating them
    
- Apply the same standard to **every** explanation including their own
    
- Make their reasoning **visible** so it can be checked
    

The numbers are not arbitrary. Each number represents a judgment about how well a specific explanation handles a specific, agreed piece of data. The numbers just make the judgment transparent and comparable.

If he objects to the numbers, ask:

> "Then tell me in plain words which explanation you think handles this fact better and why. Whatever you say, we can translate it into a relative score. The numbers are just shorthand for the reasoning."

The point system is not the argument. It is a **discipline tool** to stop the conversation from being a shouting match and make it a genuine comparison.

# CRITERION 1 — COVERAGE OF AGREED HISTORICAL DATA

## Why This Criterion Exists

A historical explanation that only explains **some** of the data is not the best explanation. It is a **partial** explanation. A good explanation must account for all the agreed data without ignoring, dismissing, or explaining away any of it without independent reason.

This criterion exists because the most common failure of naturalistic explanations is exactly this: each one handles one or two facts well and then quietly abandons the rest.

---

## First Step: Establishing the Agreed Data Set

Before scoring anything, you and your brother must agree on what facts are actually in the data set. These are not Christian claims — they are historical claims that even most non-Christian scholars accept as the baseline for any serious discussion.

Here are the agreed data points, with the reasoning for why each one counts as agreed:

---

**DATA POINT 1 — Jesus was publicly executed by Roman crucifixion**

Why it is agreed:

- Confirmed by Roman historian Tacitus (Annals 15.44) — a hostile, non-Christian source
    
- Confirmed by Jewish historian Josephus (Antiquities 18.3.3) even in the minimal form accepted by secular scholars
    
- No serious historian of any persuasion disputes the execution
    

What it contributes:

- Rules out "maybe he never existed"
    
- Establishes that his followers knew he died — they were not confused about this
    
- Sets up the central problem: a dead leader normally ends a movement
    

Scoring implication:  
Any explanation that does not start with this as a non-negotiable foundation scores 0 on this data point. Immediately.

---

**DATA POINT 3 — Multiple, diverse people reported seeing Jesus alive after death**

Why it is agreed:

- Paul writes in 1 Corinthians 15:3-8 — within roughly 20 years of the events — that Jesus appeared to Peter, then to the twelve, then to over 500 at once, then to James, then to Paul himself
    
- Gerd Lüdemann, an atheist New Testament scholar, accepts that Peter and Paul really did have experiences they interpreted as encounters with the risen Jesus
    
- Bart Ehrman, another non-Christian scholar, accepts that the disciples really believed they had seen Jesus alive
    
- The appearances are reported across multiple independent sources: Paul's letters, Mark, Matthew, Luke, John — these are not all the same source copying each other
    

What it contributes:

- The appearances were not one person's isolated claim
    
- They happened to:
    
    - Individuals (Peter, Paul, James)
        
    - Groups (the twelve, the 500)
        
    - People with different psychological profiles (grieving followers and hostile skeptics like Paul and James)
        
    - Across different times and locations
        

Scoring implication:  
Any explanation that only explains one category of appearance (e.g., individual grief visions) without explaining the others is penalized proportionally. The hallucination model for instance handles individual visions passably — but group appearances, hostile conversions, and different-time-different-place appearances are a completely different problem that needs independent explanation.

---

**DATA POINT 4 — Strict monotheistic Jews began worshipping Jesus as Lord almost immediately**

Why it is agreed:

- 1 Corinthians 1:2 — written within 20 years of the crucifixion — already describes Jewish and Gentile communities calling on the name of Jesus as Lord
    
- The phrase "calling on the name of the Lord" is a Hebrew Bible formula exclusively used for Yahweh worship (Joel 2:32, Genesis 21:33)
    
- Early hymns embedded in Paul's letters (Philippians 2:6-11, Colossians 1:15-20) show worship of Jesus at a divine level very early — too early for slow legend development
    
- Jewish monotheism was the most rigid in the ancient world — Jews were routinely martyred rather than worship any other god
    

What it contributes:

- This is the single hardest data point for any naturalistic explanation to handle
    
- Jews did not casually add a second divine being to their theology
    
- The barrier to Jewish worship of Jesus was not low — it was the highest possible cultural and theological barrier
    

Scoring implication:  
Any explanation that doesn't seriously address why strict Jewish monotheists began worshipping a recently crucified man as Yahweh scores very low on this data point. Hallucinations explain why someone might think a person is alive — they do not explain why that person would conclude the living person is the Lord of heaven and earth to be worshipped.

---

**DATA POINT 5 — Gentiles abandoned their gods and accepted potential persecution to follow Jesus**

Why it is agreed:

- Paul's letters describe Gentile converts stopping temple worship — something that carried genuine social and legal risk
    
- The Roman writer Pliny the Younger (around 112 AD) reports that Christians refused to sacrifice to Roman gods even under threat of execution
    
- Acts describes Gentile conversions across multiple cities — this is corroborated by early non-Christian sources describing the movement's spread
    

What it contributes:

- The movement was not just a Jewish internal development
    
- Gentiles who had no prior investment in Jewish Messianic expectations were convinced enough to face persecution
    
- This is the most cross-culturally surprising element: a Jewish Messiah convincing pagans to abandon their own religious traditions at personal cost
    

Scoring implication:  
Any explanation rooted in Jewish psychological dynamics (grief, messianic disappointment, cognitive dissonance) has to separately explain the Gentile dimension. If it can't, it covers only part of the data.

---

**DATA POINT 6 — The movement started in Jerusalem**

Why it is agreed:

- Acts, the earliest historical narrative of the church, explicitly locates the movement's origin in Jerusalem
    
- Paul's letters confirm early leadership based in Jerusalem (Galatians 1:18-19)
    
- This is not disputed by any serious historian
    

What it contributes:

- Jerusalem was the worst possible location to start a false resurrection claim
    
- The crucifixion was a public event witnessed by thousands
    
- The tomb's location was known
    
- The Jewish and Roman authorities who wanted the movement crushed were based there
    
- Anyone who wanted to check the tomb or challenge the claim was within walking distance of the evidence
    

Scoring implication:  
Any explanation involving gradual legend development is most severely challenged by this data point. Legends grow most easily when there is geographical and temporal distance from the events. Jerusalem at Passover, weeks after the crucifixion, is the exact opposite of ideal legend-growing conditions.

---

**DATA POINT 7 — The movement produced willingness to suffer and die for the resurrection claim specifically**

Why it is agreed:

- Tacitus reports Christians being killed under Nero
    
- Clement of Rome (late 1st century) describes Peter and Paul dying for their faith
    
- The deaths of Stephen (Acts 7), James the son of Zebedee (Acts 12), and James the brother of Jesus (Josephus, Antiquities 20.9.1) are attested in multiple sources
    
- This is not disputed by secular historians
    

What it contributes:

- People die for things they are wrong about — this alone does not prove resurrection
    
- But people do not typically die for things they know they fabricated
    
- The relevant distinction is between sincere belief and manufactured deception
    
- The willingness to die is decisive primarily against the conspiracy/fraud model
    

Scoring implication:  
The fraud model scores near zero on this data point. The hallucination and cognitive dissonance models score moderately because they preserve sincere belief. But they still have to explain why that sincere belief was specifically resurrection-shaped and not some other form of vindication theology.

## Second Step: The Scoring System for Criterion 1

Each explanation gets scored out of 20 for Criterion 1.

The 20 points are distributed across the 7 data points as follows:

|Data Point|Maximum Points|
|---|---|
|1. Public execution|2|
|2. Empty tomb|3|
|3. Multiple diverse appearances|4|
|4. Jewish monotheists worshipping Jesus|4|
|5. Gentile conversions at cost|3|
|6. Jerusalem origin|2|
|7. Willingness to die|2|
|**Total**|**20**|

Data points 3 and 4 carry the most weight because they are the most historically unusual and the hardest for any natural explanation to handle.

## Third Step: How to Score Each Data Point

For each data point, ask three questions:

1. **Does the explanation acknowledge this fact or try to dismiss it?**
    
    - If it dismisses it without independent evidence: 0 points for that data point
        
    - If it acknowledges it and tries to explain it: move to question 2
        
2. **Does the explanation explain this fact naturally within its own model, or does it need an extra assumption?**
    
    - Explains it naturally within the model: full points
        
    - Needs one extra assumption: half points
        
    - Needs multiple extra assumptions: quarter points
        
3. **Is the explanation's handling of this fact consistent with how it handles the other facts?**
    
    - If handling one fact contradicts how another fact is handled: deduct points accordingly

# CRITERION 2 — Internal Coherence (0–20)

Does the explanation contradict itself? Does it require people to act in psychologically impossible or mutually contradictory ways?

**Scoring:**

- 0–5: Major internal contradictions
    
- 6–14: Minor tensions that require extra assumptions
    
- 15–20: Fully coherent, no internal contradictions

## CRITERION 3 — Economy / Parsimony (0–20)

How many independent assumptions does the explanation require? The fewer the better.

**Scoring:**

- 0–5: Requires 4+ independent assumptions each of which must be true simultaneously
    
- 6–14: Requires 2–3 assumptions
    
- 15–20: One or two assumptions that follow naturally from the data
    

---

## CRITERION 4 — Fit With What We Know About Human Behavior (0–20)

Does the explanation require people to behave in ways that are historically unparalleled or psychologically implausible?

**Scoring:**

- 0–5: Requires behavior with no historical parallel
    
- 6–14: Requires unusual but possible behavior
    
- 15–20: Describes behavior consistent with documented human patterns
    

---
