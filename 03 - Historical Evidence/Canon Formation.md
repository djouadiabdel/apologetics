## The shape of it

The letters were treated as authoritative by the churches that received them almost immediately. Nobody drew up a list of scripture until much later.

That gap is the whole subject. Authority came first and the list came second, and the list took about three hundred years to settle.

## The timeline

**First century.** Gospels and letters written. The letters carry weight where they land. No canon.

**Around 140.** Marcion produces an edited Luke and an edited Paul with the material about the created world removed, and rejects the Old Testament outright.

Harnack argued the church built its canon in reaction to this. That thesis is widely repeated and it is contested. Treat it as a live scholarly position, not a fact.

**Around 180.** Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 3.11.8, argues for exactly four gospels and no more, on the grounds that there are four zones of the world and four principal winds. The argument is bad. The datum is good, because it tells you the fourfold gospel was already fixed enough that he felt he had to justify the number.

**Around 170 to 200.** The Muratorian fragment, the earliest surviving list. Some scholars date it to the fourth century instead, so cite it with the range.

**Around 325.** Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.25, sorts the writings into three groups.

**367.** Athanasius, 39th Festal Letter. The first list that matches the twenty-seven books we have.

Councils at Hippo and Carthage in the 390s ratify essentially the same list. They are recognising what was already in use, not voting on it.

## Eusebius, and what he admits

His three categories are worth knowing by name because they are the honest picture.

**Accepted.** The four gospels, Acts, Paul's letters, 1 John, 1 Peter, and Revelation "if it really seem proper."

**Disputed, the *antilegomena*.** James, Jude, 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John. Recognised by many, disputed by others.

**Rejected.** Acts of Paul, the Shepherd of Hermas, the Apocalypse of Peter, the Epistle of Barnabas, the Didache, and Revelation again, which he lists in two places because opinion was split.

He puts Revelation on both ends of the same list. That is not a man hiding anything.

## The books that were genuinely disputed

Hebrews, because nobody could agree who wrote it. Paul, Apollos, someone else.

James, Jude, 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John. Eusebius, *HE* 2.23.25, has James in the *antilegomena*, which kills the line that Luther was an outlier for doubting it. It does not show the early church saw a conflict with Paul. Report both directions.

Revelation, disputed for centuries in the East.

The Syriac churches did not include the catholic epistles until much later. Ephrem the Syrian and John Chrysostom were working from Bibles containing Paul, including Hebrews, without them.

## What this costs and what it does not

**It costs** the picture of a canon delivered whole and recognised instantly. That picture is not what happened and the sources say so plainly.

**It does not cost** the core. The disputes are at the edges. The four gospels, Acts and the Pauline corpus were not seriously contested by anyone inside the church. Nobody was arguing about Romans.

**And the argument runs the other way.** The disputes are recorded. Eusebius wrote down which books people doubted and preserved the doubt. A church inventing its own foundation does not leave that in.

## What to say

There was no list for two centuries and no agreed list for three. The books at the edges were argued about for a long time and the arguments are preserved in the sources, by the men on the winning side.

The books that carry the doctrine were never in question.
