What these books establish as pre-Pauline is _divine Christology_, not _the Trinity_ as a doctrine. Those are different things. Divine Christology means Jesus is included within the identity of the one God of Israel, receives worship, and is identified with YHWH's actions. The Trinity as a fully articulated doctrine (one God, three persons, coequal, coeternal) is a later theological formulation — Nicaea in 325, Constantinople in 381.

Think of it this way: the early community was _doing_ something (worshipping Jesus as divine within Jewish monotheism) that the later councils then _explained_ theologically. The raw data is first-century. The systematic explanation is fourth-century. But the fourth-century explanation isn't an invention — it's an articulation of what was already happening.

So when you're in a debate, say "Jesus's divine identity is pre-Pauline" — not "the Trinity is pre-Pauline." The first claim is what the scholarship supports. The second claim gives your opponent an easy win because they can point to the word "Trinity" not appearing until Tertullian in the 3rd century, and you'd be technically wrong even though the underlying reality you're pointing to is correct.


----
The key pieces of evidence they use are things you can learn to deploy yourself. Philippians 2:6-11 is identified by critical scholarship as a _pre-Pauline hymn_ — meaning Paul is _quoting_ something the community already sang/confessed before he wrote it down. Same with 1 Corinthians 8:6, which restructures the Shema to include Jesus. Paul didn't invent these formulas. He received them. He even says so explicitly in 1 Corinthians 15:3 — "I delivered to you what I also _received_."

So the argument is airtight on that specific claim: high Christology predates Paul's writings.
