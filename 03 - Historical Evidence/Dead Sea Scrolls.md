## What they are

Jewish manuscripts found along the western shore of the Dead Sea, dating from roughly the third century BC to the second century AD.

Most came from Qumran, eleven caves, with manuscripts in ten of them. More were later found further south at sites including Murabba'at and Masada, and the term now covers all of it.

Hundreds of scrolls, mostly on leather or vellum, a handful on papyrus. Around thirty percent are texts from the Hebrew Bible. The rest is other literature, and that rest is where most of the value is.

## The discovery

1947. A bedouin shepherd throws a rock into a cave and hears pottery break. Inside are jars holding bundles wrapped in cloth. Discoveries continued until about 1960.

Before this, nobody thought anything that old could survive in those conditions. That assumption is part of why the Shapira fragments were dismissed as forgeries in the 1880s. Those are now lost, so the question cannot be settled, and some scholars have recently argued they may have been genuine after all.

## The first seven

All from Cave 1, so all prefixed 1Q.

- **1QIsaᵃ**, the Great Isaiah Scroll. Complete, all sixty-six chapters, around nine metres long.
- **1QIsaᵇ**, a second Isaiah copy.
- **1QM**, the War Scroll, an apocalyptic text about the sons of light against the sons of darkness.
- **1QpHab**, a commentary on Habakkuk. Verse, then what it means for the community now.
- **1QS**, the Community Rule.
- **1QH**, the Hodayot, a collection of hymns and poems in the register of the Psalms but entirely original compositions.
- **1QapGen**, the Genesis Apocryphon, in Aramaic.

The naming works as cave number, then text, then which copy. So 4QJerᵃ is the first Jeremiah copy from Cave 4.

## Who wrote them

Probably the Essenes, or a group very like them.

Josephus and Philo both describe a Jewish sect concentrated on the western shore of the Dead Sea, secretive, extremely concerned with ritual purity, stricter about the Torah than the Pharisees. The self-describing texts in the collection line up with that, and the geography lines up.

Hold it as the best available identification, not as settled.

## What they actually changed

**The dating of the Hebrew Bible text.** Before 1947 our oldest Hebrew manuscripts were the Leningrad and Aleppo codices, tenth century AD. 1QIsaᵃ is late second century BC. That is a thousand years earlier in one jump.

**Textual pluriformity.** Several different editions of the same books in circulation at the same time, in the same community. See [[Textual Variants — What They Are and Aren't]].

**A window into Judaism at the time of Christ.** No New Testament manuscripts, nothing about Jesus. What you get instead is what Jews in that region believed and read in the period leading up to him, which is worth more than a proof text.

**How Jewish the New Testament is.** The books quoted most in the New Testament are the books most copied at Qumran. Isaiah, Genesis, Psalms, Deuteronomy. And 1 Enoch, which Jude quotes as prophecy, turns out to have been enormously popular there, with multiple copies and a whole body of literature built on it.

## The Isaiah two-composition case

Kipp Davis stacks five independent lines of evidence that 1QIsaᵃ was treated as two books.

1. A three line blank break at the end of chapter 33, which also falls at the seam between two sheets.
2. Multi line breaks elsewhere in Second Temple manuscripts occur only at the ends of books.
3. The second half was copied from a different exemplar, one damaged along the bottom, which Drew Longacre argued in 2014 from a repeating pattern of gaps and paraphrases.
4. Machine learning paleography by Popović and Dhali concluded the two halves were written by two different scribes, though they look identical to the naked eye.
5. Of twenty-one Isaiah manuscripts at Qumran, seventeen preserve text from only the first half or only the second.

The first half also shows fold marks, so it was stored folded while the second half was stored rolled, before they were joined.

**Note the break is at 33/34, not the classic 39/40.** So this does not simply confirm the standard Deutero-Isaiah division. It points to a division in a different place.

**And note what it does not cost.** Multiple authorship costs single author inerrancy. It costs nothing on the messianic argument, because even the latest critical dating puts the second half in the sixth century BC and 1QIsaᵃ puts the whole thing in a cave by 125 BC. The one thing it does cost is Cyrus. Isaiah 44:28 and 45:1 name a sixth century king, and if those chapters are exilic then that is not a prediction. Concede it. It was never needed.

## The forgeries

Starting around 2002, roughly 140 small fragments appeared on the private antiquities market claiming to be Dead Sea Scrolls, with no connection to any documented find.

They were bought almost entirely by conservative evangelical institutions. The Museum of the Bible bought sixteen. Southwestern Baptist and Azusa Pacific bought more. Six figures for pieces measuring three centimetres.

They are modern forgeries. The forensic evidence includes cubic salt crystals consistent with modern processed salt rather than natural halite, with ink lying on top of them, and writing on delaminated surfaces, meaning the text was applied after the leather had already deteriorated. The surfaces are coarse leather rather than prepared parchment.

Kipp Davis found this while employed to publish the collection, under a non disclosure agreement, and wrote it into his own chapter of the official volume. The volume was retracted.

**The lesson to keep.** One seminary paid extra for a fragment of Leviticus 18 and 20 because, in the director's words, it attested to a timeless truth. Nobody in Roman Palestine was collecting proof texts about homosexuality. A forger read what the buyer wanted off the price he was offering and produced it.

Demand created the evidence. This is what "never tune the sample to pass" looks like when someone else does it to you.

## What to claim and what not to

**Do not say** the Isaiah scroll is word for word identical with the Masoretic text. It has around 2,600 variants.

**Do say** that Isaiah existed complete in Hebrew around 125 BC, which settles that nothing in it was written after the fact. That answers corruption claims. It does not answer anything about interpretation, and it was never the live academic dispute, so do not present it as beating scholarship.
