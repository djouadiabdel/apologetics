**TRACK 1: THE FACTUAL CASE FOR PAUL (the "impossible to deny" layer)**

**1. Master the creed timeline.** 1 Corinthians 15:3-5 — Paul says "I received" and "I passed on." The Greek terms (_paralambanō_ and _paradidōmi_) are technical rabbinic vocabulary for formal transmission of tradition. This creed dates to within 2-5 years of the crucifixion. Paul didn't invent the resurrection claim. He inherited it from the people who were there. Read 1 Corinthians 15:1-11 slowly, multiple times. Know it cold.

**2. Master the verification chain.** Galatians 1:18 — 15 days with Peter, three years after conversion. Galatians 2:1-10 — 14 years later, submits his gospel to James, Peter, and John. They endorse him. They ask only that he remember the poor. Wright's chronological table in the biography puts this at AD 36 for the Peter visit and AD 46/47 for the Jerusalem council. These aren't vague claims. They're datable meetings with named, living witnesses who could have contradicted him and didn't.

**3. Master the cost argument.** Philippians 3:4-8 — Paul's credentials: circumcised on the eighth day, tribe of Benjamin, Pharisee, blameless under the law. He calls all of it "rubbish" (_skybala_ — the Greek is closer to a profanity). Then 2 Corinthians 11:23-28 — the suffering catalogue. Five times 39 lashes, three times beaten with rods, once stoned, three shipwrecks, constant danger. Wright makes the point that Paul kept submitting to synagogue discipline (the lashings), which means he kept showing up _knowing_ he'd be beaten. A fraud does not do this. A power-hungry manipulator does not voluntarily submit to beatings from institutions he could simply walk away from.

**4. Learn the "Paul echoes Jesus" list.** The first transcript gives you a rapid-fire list. But go deeper on three or four that you can actually cite chapter and verse for both Paul and Jesus. Pick the strongest ones: love fulfills the law (Romans 13:8-10 / Matthew 22:36-40), bless those who curse you (Romans 12:14 / Luke 6:28), the Lord's Supper (1 Corinthians 11:23-26 / Luke 22:19-20), return evil with good (Romans 12:17-21 / Matthew 5:38-48). Don't memorize the whole list. Own four or five deeply.

**5. Understand the external corroboration argument.** From the second transcript: King Aretas IV controlling Damascus around 37-39 AD (2 Corinthians 11:32-33) — a narrow historical window a second-century forger wouldn't know. Gallio in Corinth from 51-52 (Acts 18). Claudius's expulsion of Jews from Rome (Acts 18:2, confirmed by Suetonius). These are not things someone invents. They're incidental details that anchor the letters in a specific time and place.

**TRACK 2: THE GIRARDIAN DIAGNOSIS (why people need Paul to be the villain)**

**6. Internalize the scapegoat structure.** You'll get this more fully from _Things Hidden_, but the basic mechanism is already in _Deceit, Desire and the Novel_. The scapegoat resolves a community's internal tension by concentrating all blame onto a single figure who is both inside and outside the group. Paul is the perfect scapegoat: inside enough to matter (apostle, half the New Testament), outside enough to target (not one of the Twelve, former persecutor). When someone says "Paul corrupted Christianity," they're performing the scapegoat mechanism. They're purging their discomfort with Christian doctrine by expelling Paul from the narrative.

**7. Learn Concept 11 — the Psychological Circle.** "Those who judge condemn themselves." The person most vocal about a fault is the one most possessed by it. Apply this: the critics who accuse Paul of importing foreign ideas into Christianity are themselves importing foreign frameworks — modern secular assumptions, Islamic theology, Enlightenment anti-clericalism — into their reading of the text. They accuse Paul of exactly what they're doing. This is not a rhetorical trick. It's a structural observation about how mimetic accusation works.

**8. Learn Concept 47 — the Skandalon.** The stumbling block. The mediator who is simultaneously obstacle and magnet. Paul is a skandalon for his critics: they cannot stop talking about him, cannot stop writing about him, cannot build their alternative narrative without constantly returning to him. Every "Paul corrupted Christianity" argument has Paul at its center. He is the obstacle they cannot get past and the figure they cannot look away from. This is the mimetic structure of scandal — the thing that offends you is the thing that holds you captive.

**9. Connect the skandalon to the cross.** This is the deepest move. Paul himself says the cross is a skandalon (1 Corinthians 1:23). The cross — God dying in weakness, refusing to compete for power — is the ultimate stumbling block because it doesn't play the game of mimetic rivalry. Paul's biography embodies this: he abandoned status, took suffering, preached foolishness. The people who accuse Paul of being a power-hungry corruptor are projecting onto him the mimetic desire (for control, for narrative authority) that drives their own project. They need Paul to be playing the power game because that's the only game they can see.

**10. Learn Concept 28 — Negative Imitation.** Rebellion that imitates through opposition. The "Paul vs. Jesus" crowd defines their entire position _against_ Paul. They are doing negative imitation: they construct their identity by opposing Paul, which means Paul is still their mediator. Without Paul as villain, their framework collapses. This is structurally identical to what Girard shows in Dostoevsky's underground man — the rebel who is more dependent on his enemy than any disciple.

**TRACK 3: THE ATHANASIUS CONNECTION (what Paul is actually teaching)**

**11. Read Athanasius with the incarnation logic in mind.** Athanasius's argument in _On the Incarnation_ is that God became human not because humanity needed a teacher or a moral example, but because death had a legal claim on humanity through sin, and only the Author of life could cancel that claim by entering death himself and destroying it from within. This is not Greek dualism (escape from the body). This is the opposite: God enters the body, enters death, and transforms both from within.

**12. Map Athanasius onto Paul's resurrection teaching.** The fourth transcript (the resurrection video) makes this connection explicit. Paul in 1 Corinthians 15 is not teaching Greek escape-from-the-body. He's teaching Jewish transformation-of-the-body. "Sown in dishonor, raised in glory" — the seed metaphor is continuity through transformation, not replacement. Athanasius and Paul are saying the same thing: the physical is not abandoned but redeemed. The body is not a prison. It's the site of God's redemptive work. This directly refutes the "Paul imported pagan ideas" accusation.

**13. Understand the "second Adam" logic.** Both Paul (1 Corinthians 15:20-28, 45-49) and Athanasius use the Adam/Christ parallel. Adam brought death into the cosmos. Christ, as the new Adam, brings life — not just for individuals but for the entire created order. Athanasius frames this as a cosmic rescue: the Word who made humanity enters humanity to remake it. Paul frames it eschatologically: Christ is the "firstfruits," guaranteeing the harvest of resurrection for all who belong to him. Same theology, different angle. Learn to state this in your own words.

**14. The "why death was necessary" argument.** This connects to the Blogging Theology transcript (document 3) where Paul Williams pushes the "human sacrifice is immoral" objection. Athanasius answers this directly: it's not that God _demands_ a sacrifice (that's the penal substitution framing Paul Williams is attacking). It's that death had a claim, and only the one who is Life itself could enter death and break it. Paul's ransom language (1 Corinthians 6:20, 1 Timothy 2:6) fits here. The cross is not God punishing an innocent victim. It's God entering the domain of death to destroy death's power from within. Know this argument.

**HOW TO STUDY THIS OVER THE NEXT DAYS**

Each day, take one point from each track. Read the primary text (Paul's letter, the Girard concept, the Athanasius chapter). Write one paragraph in your theology notebook on what you learned — not a summary, but "what did this do to me" in the format you already use. By the time you've gone through all fourteen points, you won't just know the material. You'll own it, because you'll have wrestled with it in writing.

The convergence point is this: Paul's biography, Paul's theology, and Girard's diagnosis of why people attack him all point to the same thing. The cross is the revelation that breaks the scapegoat mechanism. Paul understood this. Athanasius understood this. The people who attack Paul are, without knowing it, reenacting the very mechanism the cross was designed to expose.
