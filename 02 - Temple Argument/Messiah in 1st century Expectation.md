### 1. Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 17a (Epigraph)

**Exact quote in the book**: “In the World to Come there is no eating or drinking … but the righteous sit with crowns on their heads, feasting on the brightness of the divine presence, as it says, ‘And they beheld God, and did eat and drink’ (Exodus 24:11).”

**Verification**: This is correct and well-known. It appears in the standard Vilna edition of the Babylonian Talmud (Berakhot 17a). The context is a discussion of the future reward of the righteous. The book uses it to show that some Jewish traditions expected a future time of miraculous, heavenly “feasting” on the divine presence (Shekhinah) — which Pitre links to the Eucharist as the “bread of the presence” / new manna.

### 2. Babylonian Talmud, Menahot 29a (Epigraph)

**Exact quote in the book**: “[The priests in the Temple] used to lift up [the golden table] and exhibit the Bread of the Presence on it to those who came up for the festivals, saying to them, ‘Behold, God’s love for you!’”

**Verification**: Accurate (standard edition, Menahot 29a, in the context of the Temple service and the showbread / Bread of the Presence). Pitre uses this to argue that the Bread of the Presence in the Temple was already understood as a sign of God’s love and presence — and that Jesus presented the Eucharist as its fulfillment (“the new Bread of the Presence”).

### 3. Ecclesiastes Rabbah 1:28 (or 1:9 in some editions) – “As the first redeemer, so the last redeemer”

**Key part quoted/paralleled in the book’s discussion (chapter 2 and 4)**: “Rabbi Berekiah said in the name of Rabbi Isaac: ‘As the first redeemer [Moses] was, so shall the latter Redeemer [the Messiah] be. … As the former redeemer caused manna to descend … so will the latter Redeemer cause manna to descend…’”

**Verification**: This is a real midrash (Ecclesiastes Rabbah / Qohelet Rabbah). The “as the first redeemer so the last” formula is very common in rabbinic literature (also appears in other midrashim like Exodus Rabbah, Numbers Rabbah, etc.). It explicitly includes the expectation that the Messiah (latter redeemer) would bring manna again, just like Moses. Pitre cites this tradition to show that the idea of a **new-Moses Messiah who brings new manna** was alive in ancient Jewish thought.

### 4. Josephus, _Antiquities_ 20.97–98 (Theudas)

The book references the broader context of 1st-century Jewish prophetic/messianic claimants who tried to reenact Exodus miracles (parting waters, leading people out, etc.). Your earlier citation of Theudas is exactly the example Josephus gives — a self-proclaimed prophet who promised to divide the Jordan “by his own command” so the people could cross (new Exodus imagery). Josephus calls him a fraud, but the episode shows that such expectations existed in popular circles.

### 5. Biblical sources the book relies on heavily

- **Deuteronomy 18:15–18** (“a prophet like me [Moses]”) — interpreted in later Jewish tradition (including the midrash above) as pointing to the Messiah.
- **Zechariah 9:9** (Messiah riding on a donkey) — paired with the Moses parallel in the midrash.
- **Exodus 16** (manna in the wilderness) and **Psalm 72:16** (used in the midrash for the Messiah causing manna to descend).
- **John 6:53–58** and the Bread of Life discourse — Pitre argues this is Jesus deliberately presenting himself as the new manna (fulfilling the Jewish hope).

All of these are correctly quoted and contextualized in the book.

### Summary: What the book actually shows

Pitre’s central argument (which aligns with what you’ve been saying) is this:

> Many ancient Jews — especially in popular and midrashic traditions — **did** expect a Messiah who would act like a **new Moses**: leading a **new Exodus**, establishing a **new covenant**, and providing **new manna** / miraculous bread from heaven. Jesus deliberately fulfilled (or claimed to fulfill) these expectations in the Eucharist: the new Passover lamb, the new manna (“this is my body”), and the new Bread of the Presence.
