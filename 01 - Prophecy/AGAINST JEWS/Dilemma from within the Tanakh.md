## Move 1: Zechariah 12:10 — The Hardest Verse in the Tanakh

_"They will look on **me**, the one they have pierced, and they will mourn for **him**."_

Ask them one question:

**Who is speaking here?**

The context is unambiguous — God is speaking throughout Zechariah 12. So God says "they pierced **me**." Then immediately switches to "mourn for **him**."

The pronoun shift is in the Hebrew. It's not a translation issue. God is pierced, but mourned as a separate figure. This forces a choice:

- Either God himself is pierced — which requires God entering humanity
- Or someone so identified with God that piercing him = piercing God

There is no comfortable third option. And the DSS confirm this text is unchanged from before Jesus. They cannot claim Christian editing.

Watch them squirm on this one.

## The Text First

Hebrew original, Zechariah 12:10:

_"And I will pour out on the house of David and the inhabitants of Jerusalem a spirit of grace and supplication. They will look on **me**, the one they have pierced, and they will mourn for **him** as one mourns for an only child, and grieve bitterly for **him** as one grieves for a firstborn son."_

---

## The Three Problems This Verse Creates For Everyone

---

### Problem 1: The Pronoun Shift

This is the nuclear problem. It's not subtle, it's not a translation artifact — it's sitting in the Hebrew text in plain sight.

God is the speaker throughout Zechariah 12. The entire chapter is framed as "declares the Lord." There is no ambiguity about who is speaking.

Then in verse 10:

- "They will look on **me** — the one they have pierced" → God speaking in first person
- "They will mourn for **him**" → suddenly third person

Same sentence. Same referent. First person then third person.

In Hebrew:

- **Elai** — "to me" (first person)
- **Alav** — "to him" (third person)

This is not a copyist error. The Dead Sea Scrolls confirm this reading. The Masoretic text confirms it. Every major manuscript tradition confirms it.

So you have God saying "they pierced **me**" and then "they mourn for **him**" in the same breath about the same figure.

**What does this force?**

Either:

- God is pierced — which means God entered a physical, pierceable form
- Or someone is so ontologically united with God that piercing him equals piercing God

There is no third reading that preserves normal Jewish theology and handles this verse cleanly. None.

---

### Problem 2: God Cannot Be Pierced In Normal Jewish Theology

This is the explosive implication.

Classical Jewish theology insists God is:

- Incorporeal — no body
- Impassible — cannot suffer
- Transcendent — beyond physical interaction

Yet the text says **God is pierced.**

The standard Jewish rabbinic escape is to say this refers to the piercing of the Messiah son of Joseph — a separate messianic figure in rabbinic tradition who suffers and dies before Messiah son of David brings the kingdom.

Here's the problem with that escape:

**The speaker is still God.** Even if you insert Messiah son of Joseph as the one physically pierced, you still have God saying "they pierced **me**" about someone else's death. That means God so identifies with this figure that his death equals God being pierced. Which still forces the same ontological conclusion — this figure shares God's identity in some unique way.

The escape doesn't actually escape. It just relocates the problem.

---

### Problem 3: The Mourning Is Described As For An Only Child and Firstborn

_"They will mourn for him as one mourns for an **only child**, and grieve bitterly for him as one grieves for a **firstborn son**."_

These are not random grief comparisons. In Hebrew culture:

- **Only child** — yachid — the same word used in Genesis 22:2 when God says to Abraham "take your son, your **only** son Isaac." The Akedah. The binding. The near sacrifice.
- **Firstborn** — bechor — carried covenantal weight, divine ownership, redemption themes throughout the Torah

The grief language is not generic. It's loaded with specific covenantal vocabulary pointing back to sacrificial and redemptive frameworks. The author is drawing from the deepest wells of Israelite theology to describe this mourning.

An ordinary prophet's death doesn't get described with Akedah vocabulary.

## The Historical Jewish Interpretations — Before Christianity

This is where it gets really powerful for your argument.

### The Targum Jonathan

The Targum Jonathan is an Aramaic paraphrase of the prophets used in synagogues. It predates Christianity as an authoritative Jewish interpretive tradition.

It interprets Zechariah 12:10 as referring to **the Messiah son of Joseph** who is slain.

This is massive. Because:

1. Pre-Christian Jewish interpretation already saw a **pierced, dying messianic figure** in this verse
2. They just split the messiah into two figures (son of Joseph who dies, son of David who reigns) to avoid the full implication
3. But the text itself doesn't mention two messiahs — that's rabbinic theological maneuvering to manage the problem the verse creates

The pre-Christian Jewish tradition already knew this verse was about a pierced messiah. They didn't invent Messiah son of Joseph after Christianity to counter it — it appears in sources that predate that debate. Which means the messianic piercing reading is Jewish, not Christian.

### The Talmud — Sukkah 52a

This is perhaps the most striking source you can use against a Jewish interlocutor. It's from their own Talmud:

_"And the land shall mourn... What is the cause of the mourning? R. Dosa and the Rabbis differ on the point. One explained: the cause is the slaying of Messiah the son of Joseph, and the other explained: the cause is the slaying of the Evil Inclination."_

Sukkah 52a is directly commenting on Zechariah 12:10-12. And the dominant rabbinic interpretation is that the mourning is for **the slain Messiah son of Joseph.**

So the Talmud itself — not Christians, not missionaries — reads Zechariah 12:10 as about a **killed messiah** who causes national mourning.

Ask your Jewish interlocutor: why does your own Talmud say this verse is about a slain messiah?

## The DSS Confirmation

4Q521 from Qumran — the Messianic Apocalypse scroll — describes the messiah in terms that include suffering and death alongside glory. This is pre-Christian Jewish sectarian literature showing that **a suffering messiah was not a Christian invention** but an existing Jewish expectation.

The DSS also contain the text of Zechariah completely intact and matching the MT reading. There is zero manuscript evidence of Christian tampering.

This is not a vague allusion. It's a direct citation at the exact moment of fulfillment — a Roman spear pierces Jesus' side publicly, witnessed by multiple people, and the author quotes Zechariah 12:10 explicitly.

The structure matters:

John is not creatively reinterpreting. He is claiming the literal fulfillment of what the Talmud itself says is about a slain messiah.

## The Revelation Echo

This places the full mourning at the second coming — meaning the verse has a **two-stage fulfillment structure**:

This is internally consistent and matches Hosea's structure — deprivation now, restoration later.

# Escape from pronouns and prophets

God sometimes speaks in solidarity with his messengers. When Israel rejects Samuel, God says in 1 Samuel 8:7 "they have not rejected you, they have rejected **me**." So maybe Zechariah 12:10 is the same move — God saying "they pierced me" meaning "they pierced my prophet, which is like piercing me."

That's a coherent reading on its surface. Here's why it collapses under pressure.

---

## Problem 1: The Grammar Doesn't Allow The Escape

In 1 Samuel 8:7 the structure is clean:

- They rejected Samuel → God says this equals rejecting me
- Two distinct referents, clearly separated

In Zechariah 12:10 the structure is:

- "They pierced **me**" then "they mourn for **him**"
- Same sentence, same referent, pronoun shifts mid-clause

If God is just speaking in solidarity with a prophet, why does the pronoun shift to third person **within the same statement about the same figure?**

If the reading is "they pierced my prophet which equals piercing me" — then the mourning clause should say "they mourn for **him**" consistently throughout. Instead God starts in first person then switches to third person about the same person.

This grammatical anomaly is the whole problem. The solidarity reading doesn't produce this grammar. It just produces consistent first person or consistent third person. The shift requires something more — a figure who is simultaneously identified with God (first person) and distinct from God (third person).

That is exactly the Trinitarian structure. One who is both God and a distinct person.

---

## Problem 2: The Mourning Scale Doesn't Fit A Regular Prophet

Verses 11-14 describe the mourning:

_"On that day the weeping in Jerusalem will be as great as the weeping of Hadad Rimmon in the plain of Megiddo. The land will mourn, each clan by itself — the clan of the house of David... the clan of the house of Nathan... the clan of the house of Levi..."_

This is **national, structured, total mourning.** Every clan separately. The entire land.

Ask your brother: when in Israel's history did the death of a prophet produce this scale of national mourning organized by clan?

It never happened. Not for Isaiah, not for Jeremiah, not for Ezekiel. Prophets were frequently ignored, imprisoned, killed — and life went on. Nobody organized clan-by-clan national mourning for a slain prophet.

The scale of mourning described requires someone whose death carries weight equivalent to a national catastrophe. That's not prophet-level. That's cosmos-level.

---

## Problem 3: Ask Him To Name The Prophet

This is your simplest move. Just ask:

**Which prophet got pierced and caused this mourning?**

If this is just about a prophet, name him. Point to the historical event. Show the clan-by-clan mourning that followed.

He can't. Because it didn't happen to any prophet in Israel's recorded history. The verse is either unfulfilled — which means Israel is still waiting for a pierced figure whose death causes national mourning — or it's fulfilled in Jesus.

Either way he has to deal with a pierced messianic figure. He can't make the verse disappear.

---

## Problem 4: The Talmud Already Rejected His Reading

Remind him that the Talmud in Sukkah 52a doesn't interpret this as a prophet. It interprets it as **the Messiah son of Joseph.** Not a regular prophet. A messianic figure.

So his "just a prophet" reading isn't even the standard Jewish escape. The rabbinic tradition already knew this verse required someone bigger than a prophet. That's why they invented a whole separate messianic figure to absorb it.

His reading is actually weaker than the traditional Jewish response.

---

## The One Line Summary

Tell him: your reading requires a pierced prophet who caused organized clan-by-clan national mourning across all of Israel. Name him and show me the historical record. If you can't, the verse is still waiting for fulfillment — and the only candidate who was publicly pierced and whose death immediately produced mourning among his followers while being explicitly identified as God's only son is Jesus.

# Move 2: Psalm 110:1 — Jesus Already Used This and They Couldn't Answer

## The Text In Full First

Psalm 110:1-4, David writing, ~1000 BC:

_"The Lord (Yahweh) said to my Lord (Adoni): 'Sit at my right hand until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet.' The Lord will extend your mighty scepter from Zion, saying 'Rule in the midst of your enemies.' Your troops will be willing on your day of battle. Arrayed in holy splendor, your young men will come to you like dew from the morning's womb. The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind: 'You are a priest forever, in the order of Melchizedek.'"_

Most people focus only on verse 1. The full psalm is actually more devastating. We'll get to that.

## The Hebrew Problem Nobody Can Escape

Two different words get translated "Lord" in English which hides the problem. In Hebrew they are completely distinct:

**Yahweh** — the personal covenant name of God. The tetragrammaton. YHWH. Unrepeatable, untransferable, belongs to God alone.

**Adoni** — "my Lord." A term of supreme personal address. Used for someone the speaker personally submits to as his superior.

So the sentence is:

**Yahweh said to my-personal-Lord: sit at my right hand.**

David is saying Yahweh spoke to someone David himself calls his personal superior and lord.

Now here is the grammatical wall your Jewish or skeptic interlocutor hits immediately:

The word **Adoni** — with the first person possessive suffix — is used throughout the Hebrew Bible to address human superiors. Kings, masters, angels. It is the word a subject uses for his king.

But David is the king. David has no human superior. The whole point of the Davidic monarchy is that David and his line are the apex of Israelite political authority under God.

So who is David calling **his** Lord?

It cannot be a future descendant because:

1. In ancient Near Eastern culture and language a man never addresses his descendant as his personal superior
2. Kings specifically never call future heirs their lords — the heir serves the king, not the reverse
3. The possessive suffix **my** lord makes it personal — David is personally subordinating himself to this figure right now in the act of writing

It cannot be just God (Yahweh) because:

1. Yahweh is already in the sentence as the speaker
2. Yahweh is speaking **to** Adoni — they are two distinct figures in conversation
3. If Adoni just meant God again, the sentence would be God talking to himself with no content

The only coherent reading: Adoni is a figure who is simultaneously David's personal superior AND the recipient of Yahweh's direct speech AND distinct from Yahweh AND invited to sit at Yahweh's right hand.

That is an extraordinary combination that has no clean parallel anywhere in the Hebrew Bible for a purely human figure.

---

## Sitting At The Right Hand

"Sit at my right hand" — this is not decorative language.

In ancient Near Eastern royal protocol the right hand position meant:

- Coregency — sharing the throne's authority
- Direct delegation of the king's own power
- The highest possible position a figure could occupy short of being the king himself

But this is **Yahweh's** right hand. Not an earthly king's right hand.

No angel sits at God's right hand in the Hebrew Bible. No prophet. No patriarch. No human king. This position is described nowhere else in the Tanakh as occupied.

The figure in Psalm 110 is invited to occupy a position that has no precedent in all of Israel's literature — seated in coregency with Yahweh himself.

Ask your interlocutor: who in the Hebrew Bible sits at Yahweh's right hand? If this is just about an ordinary Davidic king or a prophet, why does no other text place anyone in this position?

---

## The Melchizedek Clause — Verse 4

This is where the psalm becomes almost impossible to handle on purely human terms.

_"The Lord has sworn and will not change his mind: You are a priest forever in the order of Melchizedek."_

Three explosive problems packed into one sentence:

**Problem 1: The Constitutional Impossibility**

Israel had a strict separation between the kingly and priestly offices:

- Kings came from the tribe of Judah (Davidic line)
- Priests came from the tribe of Levi (Aaronic line)
- When King Uzziah tried to perform priestly duties in 2 Chronicles 26, God struck him with leprosy immediately

These offices were constitutionally separated and their mixing was treated as a category violation punishable by divine judgment.

Yet Psalm 110 — written by David, a king — explicitly calls the Adoni figure both a ruler (verses 1-3) and a **priest forever.**

This figure breaks the constitutional order of Israel by divine oath. Not by accident. Not by violation. By God's sworn decree.

A purely human Davidic king cannot be this figure without violating the Mosaic constitution. The psalm is describing someone who transcends the entire Mosaic framework.

**Problem 2: The Order Of Melchizedek**

Why Melchizedek specifically?

Melchizedek appears in Genesis 14:18-20. He is:

- King of Salem (Jerusalem)
- Priest of El Elyon — God Most High
- Has no recorded genealogy, no recorded birth, no recorded death
- Receives tithes from Abraham — meaning he is superior to Abraham
- Blesses Abraham — in Hebrew culture the greater blesses the lesser

The author of Hebrews in chapter 7 builds an entire argument on this: because Melchizedek has no recorded genealogy in the text, he prefigures a priesthood that is not based on biological descent but on the power of an indestructible life.

But even before Hebrews — just within the Tanakh — why does David invoke this obscure figure from Genesis 14 to describe the priesthood of the Adoni?

Because the Levitical priesthood is:

- Genealogical — you're a priest because of your biological descent
- Temporal — you serve until death
- Imperfect — requires constant repetition of sacrifice

The Melchizedek priesthood as described in Psalm 110 is:

- **Forever** — eternal, not biological
- **By divine oath** — God swears it, immutable
- **Non-Levitical** — predates and supersedes the Mosaic system

This means the figure in Psalm 110 has a priesthood that supersedes the entire Mosaic sacrificial system. He renders the Levitical system obsolete by divine design.

Ask your Jewish interlocutor: who in their tradition fills this role? Who is simultaneously a Davidic ruler and an eternal Melchizedekian priest? No one in rabbinic theology has a clean answer because the combination is constitutionally impossible for a human figure within the Mosaic framework.

**Problem 3: "Forever"**

Priests die. That's the whole point of priestly succession — when one dies another takes over.

An eternal priest requires either:

- A figure who doesn't die — which no human being is
- Or a figure who dies and returns to life permanently — which is resurrection

The word forever — **l'olam** — is not metaphorical here. It's reinforced by "the Lord has sworn and will not change his mind." God locks this in with an oath. This priesthood does not end.

The only way a human figure has an eternal priesthood is through resurrection. The psalm is structurally pointing toward a figure who conquers death.

## The Matthew 22 Debate — What Actually Happened

Matthew 22:41-46, Jesus is in the temple, the Pharisees have been trying to trap him all day — taxes to Caesar, resurrection questions, the greatest commandment. Now he turns the table:

_"While the Pharisees were gathered together, Jesus asked them: 'What do you think about the Messiah? Whose son is he?' 'The son of David,' they replied. He said to them, 'How is it then that David, speaking by the Spirit, calls him Lord? For he says, The Lord said to my Lord: sit at my right hand until I put your enemies under your feet. If then David calls him Lord, how can he be his son?' No one could say a word in reply, and from that day on no one dared to ask him any more questions."_

Several things to note here that your brother and Jewish interlocutors need to confront:

**First:** Jesus attributes the psalm to David explicitly — "David, speaking by the Spirit." This is not a random attribution. Jesus is treating this as Davidic authorship under divine inspiration. The Pharisees don't challenge this attribution because they agree with it. Psalm 110 was universally accepted as Davidic in first century Judaism.

**Second:** Jesus assumes the Messianic interpretation without arguing for it. He doesn't say "what if this is about the Messiah" — he says "David calls **him** Lord" referring to the Messiah. The Pharisees don't challenge this interpretation either. Because in first century Judaism, Psalm 110 was already understood as Messianic. This is confirmed by the DSS and by multiple rabbinic sources.

**Third:** The question Jesus asks is logically airtight. The dilemma is:

- The Messiah is David's son (universally agreed)
- David calls this figure his personal Lord (text says so)
- A man does not call his descendant his lord in ANE culture
- Therefore the Messiah must be both David's descendant AND David's superior simultaneously
- That logical combination requires a nature beyond ordinary humanity

**Fourth:** They couldn't answer. Matthew records this not as a rhetorical flourish but as a historical fact that ended the public debate. The greatest Torah scholars of the day — men who had memorized the entire Hebrew scriptures, who had spent their lives in textual analysis — could not resolve the paradox.

This is significant because these were not stupid men. These were the intellectual elite of first century Judaism. Their silence is not ignorance. It's the recognition that the question exposes a genuine paradox in their own text that their theology couldn't resolve.

## Why No Human King Works As The Referent

The standard Jewish escape is: this is just about Solomon, or about a generic Davidic king being honored at his coronation.

Here's why that collapses:

**The "my Lord" problem remains.** David still calls this person his personal superior. No Jewish commentator has ever explained why David would address his son Solomon or any future heir as his own personal lord. The cultural and linguistic problem doesn't disappear by naming a specific king.

**No Davidic king was an eternal Melchizedekian priest.** Solomon was not a priest. No Davidic king served as high priest. The psalm's verse 4 has never been fulfilled by any historical Davidic monarch. If this is just about a coronation ceremony it's a promise that was never kept — God swore an oath about an eternal priesthood that never materialized.

**No Davidic king sat at Yahweh's right hand.** This is not a metaphor used anywhere in the Tanakh for an ordinary king's honor. You can't point to a text that places David, Solomon, Hezekiah or any other king at Yahweh's right hand in this specific language.

**The enemies as footstool language is cosmic.** The scope of rulership described — enemies made a footstool, rule in the midst of enemies, troops from the womb of the dawn — exceeds anything any historical Davidic king accomplished. Solomon's kingdom fell apart at his death. The imagery in Psalm 110 is describing a universal and permanent subjugation of enemies that no earthly Davidic ruler achieved.
