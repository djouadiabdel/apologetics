## The verse

> "After the sixty-two 'sevens,' the Anointed One will be put to death and will
> have nothing. The people of the ruler who will come will destroy the city and
> the sanctuary. The end will come like a flood: War will continue until the end,
> and desolations have been decreed."
> — Daniel 9:26 (NIV)

---

## The claim that actually works

Forget the arithmetic for a moment and read the order of events.

An anointed one is put to death. Then the city and the sanctuary are destroyed.

Jerusalem and the temple were destroyed in AD 70. So whatever this passage is describing, it puts the death of an anointed one before that date.

That is the whole argument. It is a sequence, not a calculation, and it cannot be satisfied by anybody arriving later. Most prophecy arguments can be met by pointing at some other candidate somewhere in history. This one has a closing door.

## The dating, and why you use theirs

Two dates are on offer.

**Traditional.** Sixth century BC, Daniel in Babylon.

**Critical consensus.** Around 165 BC, written during the persecution under Antiochus IV. The reason is Daniel 11, which tracks Hellenistic politics accurately down to about 165 and then gets Antiochus's death wrong. Scholars read that as the point where the writer stopped reporting and started predicting.

**Take the critical date. Always.**

You do not need the sixth century. On the late date the book is still 200 years before the crucifixion, and eight copies of Daniel were found at Qumran, the earliest around 125 BC. So the text is in the ground, in a cave, before Jesus was born, and you did not have to win a single argument to get there.

Arguing for the sixth century is a fight you can lose and do not need. Conceding it costs you nothing and buys you the other person's attention.

## Do not lean on the arithmetic

The popular version calculates the seventy sevens down to an exact date, usually landing on Palm Sunday. Robert Anderson and Harold Hoehner are where those numbers come from.

The problem is the choices you have to make to get there.

- **Which decree starts the clock.** Cyrus in 538, Darius in 520, Artaxerxes in 458, or Artaxerxes in 445. Four candidates, and the result changes with each.
- **How long a year is.** Anderson uses a 360 day "prophetic year." That is a choice made to make the arithmetic land, and it is the weakest joint in the whole thing.

Present the calculation and you have handed over four places to attack. Present the sequence and there is nothing to grip.

## The real objection

Not "the dating is wrong." The critical reading of the same verse.

On that reading the anointed one cut off is **Onias III**, the high priest murdered around 171 BC, and the ruler who comes is **Antiochus IV**, and the abomination is the desecration of the temple in 167 BC.

That reading is coherent and you should say so out loud before someone says it to you.

**Here is where it strains.** The verse says the people of the ruler will destroy the city and the sanctuary. Antiochus desecrated the temple. He did not destroy it, and he did not destroy Jerusalem. The temple was cleansed and back in service within three years, which is what Hanukkah is.

Destruction language fits AD 70. It does not fit 167 BC.

So the honest position is that the Maccabean reading handles the anointed one better and the destruction worse, and the argument turns on which half of the verse you weight.

## What to say

The dating is not in dispute and I will use your date, not mine. On the late date Daniel is 200 years early and there are copies at Qumran from around 125 BC.

The verse puts an anointed one dying before the city and the sanctuary are destroyed. That happened in AD 70. Anything after that is too late to qualify.

I am not going to do the arithmetic for you. The calculations need four choices I cannot justify, so I do not use them.
