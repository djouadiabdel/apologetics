## The Text In Full

Proverbs 30:1-4, written by Agur son of Jakeh:

_"The words of Agur son of Jakeh — an inspired utterance. This man's utterance to Ithiel: I am weary, God, but I can prevail. Surely I am only a brute, not a man; I do not have human wisdom. I have not learned wisdom, nor have I attained to the knowledge of the Holy One. Who has gone up to heaven and come down? Who has gathered the wind in the hollow of his hands? Who has wrapped up the waters in a cloak? Who has established all the ends of the earth? What is his name, and what is the name of his son? Surely you know!"_

The last line in Hebrew — **ki teda** — is dripping with irony. "Surely you know!" It's a challenge. Almost sarcastic. Agur is saying: if you think you understand God, answer me this. Tell me his name. And tell me his son's name.

---

## Who Is Agur First

This matters because skeptics will try to dismiss the verse by dismissing the author.

Agur son of Jakeh appears nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible. No genealogy. No tribe. No historical placement. Some rabbinic sources identified him as Solomon writing under a pseudonym. Others treat him as a separate wisdom figure. Either way:

- The text is canonical — it's in the Tanakh, accepted by Jews as scripture
- It's classified as **inspired utterance** — the Hebrew word **massa** used in verse 1 is the same word used for prophetic oracles throughout the prophets
- The Septuagint — the Greek translation made by Jewish scholars ~250 BC — includes it fully
- No Jewish tradition disputes its canonical status

So whoever Agur is, what he writes here is treated as divinely inspired wisdom literature by Judaism itself. Your Jewish interlocutor cannot dismiss it as peripheral.

## The Structure Of The Rhetorical Questions

The four questions before the son question are not random. They follow a deliberate pattern that makes the fifth question — about the son — land with maximum force.

**Question 1: Who has gone up to heaven and come down?**

This is the most loaded question in the set. Going up to heaven and returning is not a normal human experience. In the entire Hebrew Bible only a handful of figures are associated with heavenly ascent — Enoch, Elijah, Moses to a degree. But none of them **came back down** in a transformative sense.

The question presupposes a figure who makes a round trip. Up and back. This is not describing a vision or a transport. It's describing someone who inhabits both realms — heaven and earth — and moves between them.

In John 3:13 Jesus says explicitly:

_"No one has ever gone into heaven except the one who came from heaven — the Son of Man."_

Jesus is directly answering Agur's first question. And he uses the exact same conceptual framework — ascent and descent as defining features of his identity.

**Question 2: Who has gathered the wind in the hollow of his hands?**

The Hebrew word for wind here is **ruach** — the same word for spirit and breath. This is the figure who holds and controls the divine breath/spirit. In Genesis 1:2 the ruach of God hovers over the waters at creation. Here the figure holds ruach in his hands like a craftsman holds material.

This is a creator attribute. Not a prophet attribute. Prophets receive the ruach. They don't hold it in their hands as a possession.

**Question 3: Who has wrapped up the waters in a cloak?**

Direct creation language. Compare Job 38:8-9 where God asks Job "who shut up the sea behind doors when it burst forth from the womb, when I made the clouds its garment and wrapped it in thick darkness?"

Agur is attributing to this figure the same creative act God claims for himself in Job. The wrapping of waters is not governance of existing creation — it's the initial establishing of boundaries at the moment of creation.

**Question 4: Who has established all the ends of the earth?**

This is unambiguous. Only the creator establishes the ends of the earth. Isaiah 40:28 says "the Lord is the everlasting God, the Creator of the ends of the earth." Agur is using vocabulary that in Isaiah refers exclusively to Yahweh.

## The Devastating Structure

Do you see what Agur has done?

He has taken four creative and cosmic attributes that the rest of the Hebrew Bible assigns exclusively to Yahweh — heavenly movement, control of ruach, ordering of waters, establishing of earth — and asked **who** does these things.

The implied answer to all four questions is: Yahweh. Only Yahweh.

Then immediately, without pause, he asks:

**What is his name — and what is the name of his son?**

The son question is grammatically and logically connected to the same subject as the four cosmic questions. Agur is not switching topics. He is asking: this cosmic creator figure who does all these divine things — what is his name? And what is his son's name?

The son is not an afterthought. The son is introduced as the climax of the interrogation, directly linked to the figure who performs divine creative acts.

## The Hebrew Grammar Of The Son Question

**Mah shemo umah shem beno ki teda**

Literal word for word:

_"What his-name and-what name his-son surely you-know"_

Three things the grammar establishes:

**First:** The son is presented in parallel with God. "What is **his** name" and "what is the name of **his son**" — the parallelism is deliberate and tight. The son shares the same interrogative weight as God himself. Agur treats knowing the son as equally fundamental to knowing God.

**Second:** The son has a **name** — shem. This is not asking about a category or a role. It's asking about a specific personal identity. The son is a particular named individual, not a metaphor.

**Third:** Ki teda — "surely you know" — is ironic because Agur has just said in verse 3 that **he** doesn't have the knowledge of the Holy One. He's setting up a contrast: I don't know, but surely **you** do — tell me then. The implied answer is: nobody knows. This knowledge is beyond human wisdom. Agur is pointing to a mystery that exceeds normal human understanding.

## What The Jewish Commentators Do With This Verse

This is where it gets fascinating because the rabbis knew this was a problem.

**Rashi — the most authoritative medieval Jewish commentator:**

Rashi interprets "his son" as referring to Israel — based on Exodus 4:22 where God calls Israel "my firstborn son." So on Rashi's reading, Agur is asking: who knows God's name and who knows Israel's name?

Here's why this fails:

1. In Exodus 4:22 God calls Israel his son in the context of the exodus — it's a relational/covenantal metaphor. Agur's context is cosmic creation. The son here is linked to the figure who established the ends of the earth. That's not Israel's role anywhere in the Hebrew Bible.
    
2. If the son is just Israel, the question loses all its force. Everyone knows Israel's name. The ironic "surely you know" doesn't work if the answer is publicly obvious. The rhetorical power of the question requires the son's name to be a genuine mystery — something not yet revealed.
    
3. The parallelism breaks down. "What is God's name" — genuinely mysterious, even Moses asks this in Exodus 3. "What is Israel's name" — completely obvious, not mysterious at all. The parallelism requires the son's name to carry the same weight of mystery as God's name.
    

**Ibn Ezra:**

Offers a similar deflection — the son refers to Israel or to Solomon as a wisdom figure. Same problems apply.

**The Midrash Rabbah:**

Some midrashic sources actually preserve an interpretation that the son here refers to the Messiah. This is significant — it means pre-Christian and early rabbinic Judaism already had a tradition of reading this verse as Messianic rather than just about Israel.

## The Proverbs 8 Connection — Wisdom As A Person

You cannot read Proverbs 30:4 in isolation. The entire book of Proverbs frames it with something explosive in chapter 8.

Proverbs 8:22-31, Wisdom speaking in first person:

_"The Lord brought me forth as the first of his works, before his deeds of old. I was formed long ages ago, at the very beginning, when the world came to be. When there were no watery depths I was given birth... I was there when he set the heavens in place, when he marked out the horizon on the face of the deep, when he established the clouds above... then I was constantly at his side. I was filled with delight day after day, rejoicing before him always, rejoicing in his whole world and delighting in mankind."_

Wisdom here is:

- Present at creation as a distinct figure alongside God
- Not created after the world — present before the watery depths existed
- At God's side as a craftsman/artisan during the act of creation
- Personal — speaks in first person, has delight and rejoicing, is not an abstract quality

Now connect this to Proverbs 30:4.

Agur is asking about a figure who gathered the wind, wrapped the waters, established the ends of the earth — all creative acts. Proverbs 8 presents Wisdom as the figure who was **present and active during all of those same acts**.

The son in Proverbs 30:4 and the Wisdom figure in Proverbs 8 are describing the same reality from two angles — one as a question, one as a revelation.

And then John 1:1-3 opens:

_"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. He was with God in the beginning. Through him all things were made; without him nothing was made that has been made."_

John is consciously writing in the framework of Proverbs 8. The Word — Logos — present with God at creation, through whom all things were made. John 1 is the answer to both Proverbs 8 and Proverbs 30:4 simultaneously.

The name of the son: Jesus. The Word made flesh.

## The Job 38 Echo

God's speech to Job in chapters 38-41 is the closest parallel to Agur's questions in the entire Hebrew Bible. God asks Job:

- "Where were you when I laid the earth's foundation?" (38:4)
- "Who shut up the sea behind doors?" (38:8)
- "Have you comprehended the vast expanses of the earth? Tell me, if you know all this." (38:18)
- "What is the way to the abode of light?" (38:19)

The rhetorical structure is identical to Proverbs 30:4. God asking unanswerable cosmic questions to establish that only the creator can answer them.

But Job 38 stops at God. Agur goes further. He adds: **and what is the name of his son?**

Agur is deliberately extending the Job framework. He's saying: not only is there a God who did all these creative things — there is also a **son** of that God, and his name is knowable in principle even if we don't yet know it.

This is an extraordinary claim for a Jewish wisdom author to make. He's not just asking about God. He's asserting that God has a son who shares in the same divine creative identity — and that son has a specific name.

## The Colossians 1 Answer

Paul in Colossians 1:15-17 answers Proverbs 30:4 with precise vocabulary:

_"The Son is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn over all creation. For in him all things were created: things in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible... all things have been created through him and for him. He is before all things, and in him all things hold together."_

Match this against Agur's questions:

- Who established the ends of the earth → all things created through him
- Who gathered the wind in his hands → all things hold together in him
- Who went up to heaven and came down → firstborn over all creation, image of the invisible God
- What is his name → the Son, Jesus Christ

Paul is not doing creative reinterpretation. He is giving the name Agur said existed but couldn't provide.

## The Irony Of The Last Line

Come back to **ki teda** — "surely you know."

Agur says he lacks wisdom. He doesn't have the knowledge of the Holy One. He can't answer his own questions.

Then he asks: tell me the son's name. Surely **you** know.

The irony is that for a thousand years after Agur wrote this, nobody knew the answer. No Jewish tradition could name the son. The rabbis deflected to Israel or Solomon — answers that don't actually fit the questions. The mystery remained open.

Then Jesus arrives, is asked by the high priest "are you the Son of God?" and says yes. And his followers begin proclaiming the answer to Agur's question: the son's name is Jesus.

For the first time in history someone gave a direct answer to Proverbs 30:4. And the answer resolves every aspect of the question — the cosmic creative role, the heavenly origin and descent, the named personal identity, the divine parallel.

---

## The One Move Against Jewish Deflection

When your Jewish interlocutor says "the son just means Israel":

Ask them to do one thing. Read Proverbs 30:4 inside its own context:

Agur has just said in verse 3 that he does **not** have the knowledge of the Holy One. He's positioning himself as genuinely ignorant of divine mystery. Then he asks the cosmic questions — all of which only God can answer. Then he asks for the son's name.

If the answer is just "Israel" — a name every Jewish child knows — why does Agur present it as unanswerable mystery? Why does "surely you know" land as irony if the answer is completely obvious?

The rhetorical force of the question **requires** the son's name to be as mysterious and unknown as the answers to the cosmic questions. Israel's name is not mysterious. The Messiah's name before his coming — that is mysterious.

Agur is pointing forward to a revelation not yet given. And the irony of ki teda is that no one did know — until they did.
