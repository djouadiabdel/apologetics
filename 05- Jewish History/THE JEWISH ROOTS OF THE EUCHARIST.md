# 1. The Mystery of the Last Supper: Jesus’ Words in Jewish Context

## 1.1 The Shocking Nature of Jesus’ Claims

Jesus of Nazareth was a Jew who spoke to Jews in a thoroughly Jewish setting. Yet his statements about eating his flesh and drinking his blood (John 6:53–58) and his declaration at the Last Supper that the bread “is my body” and the wine “is my blood of the covenant” (Matthew 26:26–28) appear to violate core Torah commandments against consuming blood (Leviticus 17:10–12; Deuteronomy 12:16; Genesis 9:4).

As the Jewish scholar Géza Vermes noted (and Pitre cites), the imagery of drinking a man’s blood “strikes a totally foreign note in a Palestinian Jewish cultural setting.” The disciples themselves reacted: “This is a hard saying; who can listen to it?” (John 6:60). Many walked away (John 6:66).

## 1.2 The Solution: Ancient Jewish Eyes

Pitre’s method is to remove modern “eyeglasses” and read the New Testament through the eyes of first-century Jews who knew the Torah, the prophets, the Temple liturgy, and the living traditions preserved in the Mishnah, Talmud, and midrashim. When this is done, Jesus’ words are revealed as a deliberate claim to be the fulfillment of Israel’s deepest hopes for redemption.

# 2. What Were First-Century Jews Actually Waiting For?

## 2.1 Diversity of Messianic Hope

Contrary to the popular modern stereotype that Jews were waiting only for a political/military Messiah to overthrow Rome, the sources reveal rich diversity:

- Political/Military strand: Zealots and figures like Judas the Galilean expected armed liberation.
    
- Prophetic/Miraculous strand: Figures like Theudas (Josephus, Antiquities 20.97–98) promised to part the Jordan and lead a new Exodus — exactly the type of claimant the user has highlighted.
    
- Temple-centered / Priestly strand: Qumran (Dead Sea Scrolls) expected two Messiahs — one kingly, one priestly.
    
- New Moses / New Exodus strand: Widespread in midrashic tradition and directly relevant to the Eucharist.
    

## 2.2 The New Exodus Paradigm (Pitre’s Central Framework)

Drawing on the prophets (especially Isaiah 40–55, Ezekiel 20, 36–37, Hosea 2, 11), Pitre demonstrates that many Jews expected God to repeat the pattern of the first Exodus from Egypt:

1. A New Moses — Deuteronomy 18:15–18 (“a prophet like me”) interpreted messianically.
    
2. A New Covenant — Jeremiah 31:31–34; Ezekiel 36:26–28.
    
3. A New Temple — Ezekiel 37:26–28; Isaiah 2:2–4.
    
4. A New Promised Land / New Creation — Isaiah 65–66; Revelation 21 (echoing Jewish hope).
    

Key Midrashic Evidence (Ecclesiastes Rabbah 1:28 / 1:9):

“As the first redeemer [Moses] was, so shall the latter Redeemer [the Messiah] be… As the former redeemer caused manna to descend… so will the latter Redeemer cause manna to descend.”

This is the exact tradition the user has emphasized and which Pitre places at the heart of his argument for the Eucharist as “the manna of the Messiah.”

# 3. The New Passover and the Lamb

## 3.1 Passover at the Time of Jesus

Pitre (Chapter 3) reconstructs the Jewish Passover meal as it was celebrated in the late Second Temple period using the Mishnah (Pesahim 10), Josephus, and the Torah. The four cups of wine, the lamb, the unleavened bread, the bitter herbs, and the retelling of the Exodus story were central.

## 3.2 Jesus as the New Passover Lamb

At the Last Supper (a Passover meal), Jesus identified the bread as his body and the wine as his blood “poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins” — language echoing the sacrificial blood of the Passover lamb and the covenant at Sinai (Exodus 24:8). Paul later explicitly calls Jesus “our paschal lamb” who “has been sacrificed” (1 Corinthians 5:7).

This fulfills the Jewish hope for a new Passover in the new Exodus.

# 4. The Manna of the Messiah: The Heart of the Argument

## 4.1 The Biblical and Midrashic Foundation

Pitre devotes an entire chapter (Chapter 4) to the manna tradition because it is one of the strongest links between Jesus’ words in John 6 and first-century Jewish expectation.

Key Texts:

- Exodus 16 — Manna in the wilderness as “bread from heaven.”
    
- Psalm 78:24–25 — “He rained down on them manna to eat, and gave them the grain of heaven. Man ate the bread of the mighty ones.”
    
- Ecclesiastes Rabbah 1:28 (quoted above) — Explicitly states the Messiah will cause manna to descend again.
    
- Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 17a (epigraph of Pitre’s book) — In the World to Come the righteous feast on the brightness of the divine presence (citing Exodus 24:11).
    

## 4.2 Jesus’ Claim in John 6

When Jesus says “I am the bread of life… This is the bread that comes down from heaven… Whoever eats my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life” (John 6:35, 51, 54), he is claiming to be the new manna — the miraculous, life-giving bread that the Messiah was expected to provide.

Pitre notes that the crowd’s reaction (“How can this man give us his flesh to eat?” John 6:52) mirrors the very skepticism one would expect from Jews familiar with both the Torah prohibition on blood and the tradition that only the Messiah would bring manna again.

## 4.3 The Eucharist as the New Manna

At the Last Supper, Jesus does not merely teach about manna — he gives his own body and blood as the new, eternal manna. This is the “hard saying” made intelligible through Jewish eyes: the Messiah has arrived, and the manna is now available in the form of the Eucharist.

# 5. The Bread of the Presence (Showbread)

Pitre (Chapter 5) connects the Eucharist to the Temple’s Bread of the Presence (Exodus 25:30; Leviticus 24:5–9). Every Sabbath, twelve loaves were placed on a golden table in the Holy Place. According to the Mishnah and Talmud (Menahot 29a, cited in Pitre’s epigraph), the priests would lift the table and show the bread to pilgrims, declaring: “Behold, God’s love for you!”

Jesus presents the Eucharist as the new Bread of the Presence — the visible sign of God’s love now given in his own body and blood, available not just to priests in the Temple but to all who believe.

# 6. The Fourth Cup and the Death of Jesus

One of Pitre’s most original contributions (Chapter 6) is his analysis of the Jewish Passover structure and Jesus’ cry from the cross: “I thirst” (John 19:28).

The Passover Seder involved four cups of wine. Jesus drank the first three at the Last Supper but deliberately refrained from the fourth cup. He then went to Gethsemane, was arrested, and on the cross said “I thirst,” was given wine vinegar, and declared “It is finished” (John 19:30) — finally drinking the fourth cup.

This completes the Passover sacrifice. The Last Supper and the crucifixion are one continuous act: the new Passover lamb is offered, and the new covenant is ratified in his blood.

# 7. Addressing the Halakhic Question

The user rightly notes that after 70 CE rabbinic authority grew significantly. However, the distinction between aggadah and halakha is crucial:

- Aggadah (Pitre’s focus): The new-Moses, new-manna, new-Exodus traditions are interpretive and homiletical. They are ancient (pre-70 CE) and provide the typological framework for understanding Jesus.
    
- Halakha (biblical criteria): The legal test for the Messiah — Davidic king who rebuilds the Temple, gathers the exiles, and brings universal peace — comes directly from the prophets (Isaiah 11, Ezekiel 37, etc.), not from post-70 rabbis. These texts predate the destruction by 500+ years.
    

Pitre does not deny the halakhic criteria. He argues that Jesus fulfilled the aggadic hopes in a profound way that was recognizable to first-century Jews familiar with the midrashim, even while the full halakhic program awaits the Second Coming (a common Christian reading of the “already / not yet” tension).

# 8. Conclusion: Why This Matters

Brant Pitre’s book, read alongside the ancient Jewish sources it cites (Ecclesiastes Rabbah, Talmud Berakhot 17a & Menahot 29a, Josephus, the prophets), demonstrates that the Eucharist is not a later Christian invention but the logical and deeply Jewish fulfillment of Israel’s hope for a new Moses who would give new manna, a new Passover lamb, and a new sign of God’s presence.

For those who, like the user, emphasize the “prophet like Moses” and “everlasting manna” expectations, Pitre provides the scholarly bridge: Jesus deliberately claimed to be that figure, and the Eucharist is the manna he promised.

The halakhic criteria remain important for understanding why most Jews did not (and do not) accept Jesus as the Messiah in his first coming. But the aggadic traditions preserved in the very sources Pitre cites show that the idea of a new-Moses Messiah bringing manna was alive and well in the first century — and that Jesus spoke directly into that hope.

“Take, eat; this is my body… Drink of it, all of you; for this is my blood of the covenant.”

— Jesus of Nazareth, the New Moses, at the Last Supper
