**The short ones (do these)**

- _Notes from Underground_ — Dostoevsky — ~140 pages. **Start here.** It's the single best return on investment in the whole list. It's where Girard gets "underground mysticism" and "the perverse saint" — exactly the chapter 7 stuff you said you didn't get. Read this and chapter 7 will unlock retroactively. Pevear/Volokhonsky translation if English, Markowicz if French.
- _Madame Bovary_ — Flaubert — ~330 pages. You're already reading Flaubert with audiobook, so this might already be on your list. Emma is Girard's textbook case of "bovarysme" — desiring through borrowed romantic novels. If you read it in French it doubles as French practice. Highest synergy with what you're already doing.
- _Eternal Husband_ — Dostoevsky — ~150 pages. Short, brutal, and it's basically a clinical case study of the rival-as-mediator. Girard adores it. Almost nobody reads it, which is a shame.

**The medium ones (pick one)**

- _The Red and the Black_ (Le Rouge et le Noir) — Stendhal — ~550 pages. Yes, Stendhal is exhausting about love — but that's the _point_ Girard is making. Julien Sorel is the prototype of vanity-as-mimetic-desire. If you can stomach one Stendhal, make it this one, not _Charterhouse_.

**The big one (skip unless you fall in love with Girard)**

- _In Search of Lost Time_ — Proust — ~4,000+ pages across 7 volumes. Don't. If you ever want a taste, read just _Swann's Way_ (~450 pages) or even just the "Swann in Love" section (~200 pages standalone). Girard's Proust chapters will make sense from that alone.

**The one you've already vetoed**

- _Don Quixote_ — Cervantes — ~900+ pages. Fair veto. Honestly, Girard's points about Quixote are the easiest to grasp from his own summaries — you don't need the novel itself to follow him.
