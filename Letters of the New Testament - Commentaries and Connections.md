# Letters of the New Testament — Commentaries and Connections

Companion to [[Plan Till EVC PSYCHIATRY]]. Two things live here:

1. Which saints and Fathers actually wrote something worth reading on each letter, and where to find it.
2. What each letter, and for James each chapter, connects to in what you are already studying. The point of Part 3 is the readings nobody in the comments would notice but you would, because you have Girard, Chesterton, and the divided will in your hands.

Scripture quoted from the NIV.

## Part 1 — Commentaries by saints and Fathers

### Honest note about James first

There is no great standalone commentary on James by a first-rank ancient Father the way there is for Paul. In the early centuries James was received slowly in both the Greek East and Latin West, so it got less attention than Romans or the Gospels. That is a historical fact, not a gap in your searching. What you do have is very good:

- **St. Bede the Venerable, _Commentary on the Seven Catholic Epistles_.** This is the one to get. Bede is a saint and a Doctor of the Church, and this is the classic patristic-era commentary covering James, 1–2 Peter, 1–3 John and Jude in one volume. Complete, sober, pastoral. Translated in the Cistercian Studies series (a book, not free online, but not expensive).
- **_Ancient Christian Commentary on Scripture_ (ACCS), the James/1–2 Peter/1–3 John/Jude volume, ed. Gerald Bray.** This is a modern book but every line inside it is the Fathers themselves, arranged verse by verse (Origen, Didymus the Blind, Chrysostom fragments, Cyril, Augustine, Bede and others). For your purpose, reading a passage and then seeing what six Fathers said about it, this is the single most useful thing you can own.

### For the letters of Paul

- **St. John Chrysostom, the _Homilies_.** The gold standard. He preached through Romans, 1–2 Corinthians, Galatians, Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians, 1–2 Thessalonians, 1–2 Timothy, Titus, Philemon and Hebrews. A saint and Doctor, and the most alive of the ancient commentators. Free in full online in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers (search "Chrysostom homilies Romans NPNF ccel").
- **St. Thomas Aquinas, the _Commentaries on the Pauline Epistles_ (Lectures on Paul).** A saint and Doctor, medieval rather than ancient, extraordinarily precise. Note: Aquinas did not write on James, so do not go looking for it.
- **St. Augustine** on Romans and Galatians for shorter pieces, but his real treasure is on John's letter, below.

### For John's letters

- **St. Augustine, _Ten Homilies on the First Epistle of John_.** The classic. This is where "Love, and do what you will" comes from. Free online in the NPNF.

### For the catholic epistles as a set (James, Peter, John, Jude)

- Back to **Bede** and the **ACCS** volume named above. They are the natural home for all seven.

### Practical stack

If you want the smallest kit that covers everything: Bede for the catholic epistles, Chrysostom for Paul, Augustine for 1 John, and the ACCS volumes as your verse-by-verse "what did the Fathers say here" lookup. Chrysostom and Augustine are free; Bede and ACCS are books worth buying.

## Part 2 — James, chapter by chapter, mapped to what you study

You are starting here, so this is the detailed part.

### James 1 — the divided man and the good gift

This is your first video already. The two threads that matter most to you are both here.

- **Desire that conceives.** "Each person is tempted when they are dragged away by their own evil desire and enticed. Then, after desire has conceived, it gives birth to sin" (1:14–15). This is your mimetic reading. Desire is not a neutral appetite, it has a genealogy, it comes from somewhere and grows into something.
- **The good model.** "Every good and perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of the heavenly lights" (1:17). This is the second half of the same thesis. If desire is caught from a model, then the whole question is which model. The Father of lights is the one model whose giving creates no rivalry, because he gives without competing. That is the non-rivalrous good model your James 1 video was reaching for.
- **The double-minded man** (_dipsychos_, 1:8), "like a wave of the sea, blown and tossed by the wind." This is your bridge into the plan's Part V and Part VI, the divided will and the unstable self. Chesterton's maniac is exactly this man, perfectly logical inside a system with no fixed point, spinning. Hold this word _dipsychos_, it comes back in chapter 4.

### James 2 — partiality and the eyes of others

> Opened up into a full episode in [[James 2 - Reading Plan and Video Mine]]: the reading chain (Leviticus 19 is the key), the five Greek words, and the Girard / Chesterton / Zizioulas mines. The load-bearing find is that `prosōpolēmpsia`, James's word for the sin, literally means face-receiving, and `prosōpon` is Zizioulas's mask.

- **Favoritism in the assembly** (2:1–9), honoring the rich man's gold ring and shaming the poor. Read this next to Girard's "men become gods in the eyes of each other." Deference to the rich is mimetic prestige, valuing a person by the borrowed shine others put on them.
- This is also the plan's recognition theme (Rashed). Treating someone as worth less because of their status is misrecognition, the same move by which a psychiatric label can make a patient less credible.

### James 3 — the tongue, and wisdom from above

- **Envy and selfish ambition.** "For where you have envy and selfish ambition, there you find disorder and every evil practice" (3:16). The Greek _eritheia_ is rivalry, faction. This is almost a clinical description of a mimetic crisis, the moment rivalry dissolves order. Against it James sets "wisdom that comes from heaven," which is "peace-loving," non-rivalrous by nature.
- The tongue as a fire that "sets the whole course of one's life on fire" is a moral psychology of speech, and a natural companion to how you already think about scandal and contagion.

### James 4 — the most Girardian chapter in the New Testament

If you make one video that ties your whole project together, it might be this one.

- "What causes fights and quarrels among you? Don't they come from your desires that battle within you? You desire but do not have, so you kill. You covet but you cannot get what you want, so you quarrel and fight" (4:1–2). This is mimetic desire, rivalry and violence written in a single breath. Coveting the object, failing to get it, turning on the rival. Girard could have used it as an epigraph.
- "You double-minded" (_dipsychos_ again, 4:8). The word from chapter 1 returns, now tied explicitly to the war of desires. The divided will and mimetic rivalry are the same wound seen from inside and from outside.

### James 5 — the cry of the defrauded, and confession

- "The wages you failed to pay the workers who mowed your fields are crying out against you" (5:4). The victim has a voice and God hears it. This is Girard's endpoint, the biblical uncovering of the victim, the thing myth always hid.
- "Confess your sins to each other and pray for each other so that you may be healed" (5:16). This is your careful line for the plan's Part V, scrupulosity. Healthy confession to one another versus compulsive confession as a symptom. Same act, two very different interior structures, and knowing the difference is both spiritual and clinical.

## Part 3 — the other letters, the layered read

For each letter, the connections that only surface once you have the tools. Labeled by lens so you can grab the one you want for a given video. When you reach a letter, go verse by verse and this becomes your seed list.

---

### Romans (Chrysostom, then Aquinas)

- **Girard.** Romans 7:7 is a mimetic bomb hiding in plain sight. "I would not have known what coveting really was if the law had not said, 'You shall not covet.'" The prohibition does not restrain the desire, it names the object and inflames it. The law points at the thing and desire lights up. This is mimetic desire and the psychology of the forbidden fruit in one verse, and almost nobody preaches it that way. Then Romans 5, the Adam and Christ typology, two men, two humanities, sin through "one man" and grace through "one man." That is mimetic anthropology at cosmic scale, we become the humanity of whichever man we imitate.
- **Chesterton.** Romans 1:21, "they neither glorified him as God nor gave thanks to him, but their thinking became futile." Chesterton's whole account of madness is here in order: the loss of gratitude and wonder comes first, and the darkened reason comes second. "Professing to be wise, they became fools" is the maniac exactly, the mind that keeps all its logic and loses the world. Then Romans 11:33, "Oh, the depth of the riches," the doxology where the mind bows instead of totalizing. That bow is Chesterton's sanity, the reason that knows its own edge, against the suicide of thought.
- **The divided will.** Romans 7:15, "For what I want to do I do not do, but what I hate I do." This is the locus classicus for the whole Part V, the will against itself. Augustine built his psychology of the will on this verse. Every OCD and akrasia question in the plan has its root here.
- **The anti-retaliation ethic.** Romans 12:17–21, "Do not repay anyone evil for evil," "do not take revenge," "overcome evil with good." This is the mimetic cycle of vengeance broken on purpose. And Romans 14–15, the strong and the weak told not to despise or judge each other, is anti-scapegoating written as church discipline.

### 1 Corinthians (Chrysostom)

- **Girard.** The factions in 1:12, "I follow Paul," "I follow Apollos," are rivalrous discipleship, communities forming around competing models, which is mimetic conflict wearing a spiritual coat. Then the master term arrives: the cross is a _skandalon_, "a stumbling block to Jews and foolishness to Gentiles" (1:23). Scandal is Girard's word. And 1:27–28, "God chose the weak things," "the lowly things and the despised things," is the scapegoat mechanism turned inside out, God siding openly with the rejected instead of the crowd.
- **Chesterton.** "The foolishness of God is wiser than human wisdom" (1:25), and "if any of you thinks you are wise... become a fool so that you may become wise" (3:18). This is the ethics of elfland, the upside-down sanity, the fool who sees and the clever man who is blind.
- **Love against envy.** 1 Corinthians 13:4, "love does not envy, it does not boast, it is not proud." Read literally as the direct antidote to mimetic rivalry. Envy is wanting because another has, boasting is provoking the want in others. Paul's love is the exit from the whole triangle.
- **Recognition.** The body in chapter 12, where "the parts that we think are less honorable we treat with special honor" (12:23), is the exact inverse of James 2. It is a community that reverses the eyes of others instead of obeying them.

### 2 Corinthians (Chrysostom)

- **Girard.** Power made perfect in weakness (12:9) and "we do not wage war as the world does" (10:3–4). Paul refuses rivalrous strength on principle. And 5:19, God "not counting people's sins against them," is the end of accusation itself, the accuser disarmed.
- **Chesterton.** "We have this treasure in jars of clay" (4:7), and "sorrowful, yet always rejoicing" (6:10). Chesterton's paradox as a way of life, the sane mind that can hold two opposite things at once, against the maniac who can only hold one.
- **Two kinds of sorrow.** 7:10, "godly sorrow brings repentance... but worldly sorrow brings death." This is a genuinely phenomenological distinction, two griefs that look identical from outside and run opposite directions inside. It maps straight onto the plan's melancholia and pathological guilt material, the difference between sorrow that opens a future and sorrow that closes it.

### Galatians (Augustine, Jerome, Aquinas)

- **Girard.** "If you bite and devour each other, watch out or you will be destroyed by each other" (5:15). Mimetic rivalry eating a community alive. And the works of the flesh list in 5:19–21 is startling once you notice it: "hatred, discord, jealousy, fits of rage, selfish ambition, dissensions, factions and envy." That is not a list of private vices, it is the vocabulary of a mimetic crisis, the sequence of a community tearing itself apart.
- **Chesterton.** "It is for freedom that Christ has set us free" (5:1), but "do not use your freedom to indulge the flesh" (5:13). Chesterton's freedom always has a shape, the fence around the cliff that lets the children play. The maniac's freedom is a padded cell, endless and closed. Paul's is the opposite.
- **The divided will.** 5:17, "the flesh desires what is contrary to the Spirit, and the Spirit what is contrary to the flesh... so that you are not to do whatever you want." The divided will again, with a twist worth a whole video: the conflict is not only the disease, it is also what keeps you from mere whim. A useful correction to any therapy that treats all inner conflict as pathology.

### Ephesians (Chrysostom)

- **Girard.** "Our struggle is not against flesh and blood, but against... the powers of this dark world" (6:12). These are Girard's Powers, the impersonal mimetic structures that run larger than any individual. And 2:14, Christ "has destroyed the barrier, the dividing wall of hostility," abolishing the Jew and Gentile divide. Every scapegoat community needs a constitutive enemy, an us and a them. Paul says the wall is down, which is the end of the mechanism at its root.
- **Chesterton.** The armor of God in chapter 6, the Christian as a knight in a real and dangerous cosmos, is Chesterton's romance of orthodoxy exactly, the world as a story with actual dragons and not a machine.
- **Person against nature.** 4:22–24, "put off your old self... put on the new self." A whole doctrine that the person is not fixed by their nature but can be re-clothed. This is the spine of your [[Project: Person vs Nature channel]] thesis stated by Paul, the self as changeable rather than a verdict.

### Philippians (Chrysostom)

- **Girard.** This letter holds the single best anti-mimetic text in Scripture. 2:3, "do nothing out of selfish ambition or vain conceit" (the empty glory of the rival), then the hymn: Christ "did not consider equality with God something to be used to his advantage" (2:6), often rendered "grasped." Set it against Eden, where the man grasps at being like God. Here the one being who actually could grasp lets go and empties himself. That is the perfect good model, the cure for acquisitive desire shown in a single person. Then, tiny and human, Euodia and Syntyche in 4:2, a real mimetic quarrel Paul names by name.
- **Chesterton.** "Rejoice in the Lord always" written from prison (4:4), and "I have learned to be content whatever the circumstances" (4:11). Joy unhooked from conditions, which is the exact opposite of the mimetic man whose peace depends on getting the object. Chesterton's gratitude that does not wait for good news.
- **Anxiety.** 4:6–7, "do not be anxious about anything... and the peace of God, which transcends all understanding, will guard your hearts and minds." The word "guard" is a garrison posted around the mind. A clean text for the plan's anxiety material, peace as a stationed defense rather than a feeling.

### Colossians (Chrysostom)

- **Girard.** 2:15, "having disarmed the powers and authorities, he made a public spectacle of them, triumphing over them by the cross." This is Girard's most important single claim about the cross, that it does not just forgive, it exposes. The scapegoat mechanism worked only while hidden; the cross drags it into public and it dies of the light. And the verse before it, 2:14, "canceling the charge of our legal indebtedness... nailing it to the cross," is the accusation itself nailed up where everyone can see it, the accuser silenced.
- **Chesterton.** The cosmic hymn, "in him all things hold together" (1:17). Chesterton against the materialist whose universe explains everything and means nothing. Here is the center that keeps the cosmos a story rather than a machine.
- **The hidden self.** 3:3, "your life is now hidden with Christ in God." The true self is not the observable behavior. A quiet but sharp weapon against reductive psychiatry, the person is more than the symptom, and the realest part of them may not be on the chart.

### 1 and 2 Thessalonians (Chrysostom)

- **Girard.** 2 Thessalonians 2, "the man of lawlessness... who sets himself up in God's temple, proclaiming himself to be God." This is the ultimate rival, the mimetic double of God, the counterfeit model that imitates the original so well it takes the throne. And notice the opposite pole in 1 Thessalonians 1:6, "you became imitators of us and of the Lord." Positive mimesis named outright, imitation as the mechanism of salvation when the model is good.
- **Chesterton.** 1 Thessalonians 5, the thief in the night, "let us be awake and sober." Chesterton's wakefulness and wonder against the sleep of the man who takes the world for granted, the drowsiness that is the real death of the mind.

### 1 and 2 Timothy, Titus (Chrysostom, Aquinas)

- **Girard.** The recurring warning about "quarrels about words" (1 Timothy 6:4, 2 Timothy 2:14), which breed "envy, strife, malicious talk" and "constant friction between people of corrupt mind." Mimetic rivalry moved indoors, teachers competing for prestige inside the church. And 6:10, "the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil," money being the mimetic object par excellence because its whole value is what others want it for.
- **Chesterton.** 1 Timothy 6:20, "guard what has been entrusted to your care... avoiding what is falsely called knowledge." Chesterton against the fashionable clever system, orthodoxy as the handed-down thing that keeps you sane when the new cleverness would drive you mad.
- **Psychiatry, pastoral.** 2 Timothy 1:7, "God gave us a spirit not of fear but of power and love and self-discipline," written to a young, timid, easily-frightened man. A small real psychology of courage, and a reminder that Paul pastors one fragile person, not a diagnosis.

### Philemon (Chrysostom)

- **Girard and recognition.** Onesimus the runaway slave sent back "no longer as a slave, but better than a slave, as a dear brother" (v.16). This is recognition in its purest form, the person stepping out from behind the role. Paul refuses to command and appeals through love instead, the deliberate refusal to use power over another. And "if he has done you any wrong... charge it to me" (v.18) is a tiny reversal of the scapegoat, the strong one absorbing the debt of the weak rather than expelling him.

### Hebrews (Chrysostom)

- **Girard.** This is the sacrifice book, and it is Girard's territory. "It is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins" (10:4) is the critique of the sacrificial system spoken from inside the temple. Christ "once for all" (10:10) is the last sacrifice that ends the machine rather than feeding it. The sharpest line is 12:24, Christ's "sprinkled blood that speaks a better word than the blood of Abel." Abel is the first victim of the founding murder; his blood cried for vengeance. Christ's blood, the ultimate victim, speaks mercy instead. The exact inversion of the founding murder, named in one verse. (Honest note for a video: Hebrews still uses sacrificial language for the cross, so the Girardian reading is that this is the anti-sacrifice that reveals and ends sacrifice, not a simple denial of it. Worth stating the tension rather than smoothing it.)
- **Chesterton.** "Faith is confidence in what we hope for and assurance about what we do not see" (11:1), the cloud of witnesses, the pilgrim mid-journey. Chesterton's romance of orthodoxy, the sense of being a character inside a true story that is already underway.

### 1 Peter (Bede, ACCS)

- **Girard.** 2:23, "when they hurled their insults at him, he did not retaliate; when he suffered, he made no threats." The innocent victim who refuses to feed the cycle back, the mimetic chain snapped from inside. And 2:7, "the stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone," which is Girard's exact figure, the scapegoat cast out by the crowd who turns out to be the foundation. Then 5:8, "your enemy the devil prowls around like a roaring lion," satan the accuser again, hunting.
- **Chesterton.** Written to "foreigners and exiles" (2:11). Chesterton's man from elfland, the stranger who sees this world with fresh and grateful eyes precisely because he does not fully belong to it.

### 2 Peter (Bede, ACCS)

- **Girard.** The scoffers of 3:3–4, "Where is this 'coming' he promised?" Mockery as a contagion that dissolves the fear of judgment, a mimetic spread of cynicism through a community. The mocker recruits mockers.
- **Chesterton.** 3:5, the scoffers "deliberately forget." Willed forgetting, the loss of wonder chosen on purpose, which is Chesterton's madman who has kept his reason and thrown away everything else. Against it, 1:4, "participate in the divine nature," the person raised rather than reduced.

### 1 John (Augustine's homilies)

- **Girard.** 3:12, Cain "who belonged to the evil one and murdered his brother. And why did he murder him? Because his own actions were evil and his brother's were righteous." The founding murder with its mimetic motive spelled out, the innocent brother killed for being righteous. Then 3:15, "anyone who hates a brother or sister is a murderer," hatred read as latent violence. And 4:18, "perfect love drives out fear," which is the end of the accuser's whole regime, because fear is the accuser's instrument.
- **Chesterton.** "God is light; in him there is no darkness at all" (1:5). Real clarity against the maniac's dark clarity, and the test of that clarity is love, not logic.
- **Scrupulosity, the gold verse.** 3:20, "if our hearts condemn us, God is greater than our hearts, and he knows everything." This is a direct word to the self-accusing, condemning conscience, the pathological guilt of the plan's Part V, and the answer is that God outranks the accusing heart. Hard to overstate how useful this one is for the scrupulosity work.

### 2 John (Bede, ACCS)

- **Girard.** A short letter that sits right on the hard line, warning not to receive the deceiver (v.10) while holding the command to love. Worth a careful video precisely because it is near the scapegoat edge, exclusion held together with love, and getting that balance is the whole art of not turning boundary into scapegoating.

### 3 John (Bede, ACCS)

- **Girard.** "Diotrephes, who loves to be first" (v.9), who "spreads malicious nonsense about us" and "refuses to welcome" others. A prestige-seeker who accuses and excludes, a scapegoater in a single church, in a single verse. Gaius is his opposite, the hospitable one. A tiny two-person case study of the mimetic posture and its cure.

### Jude (Bede, ACCS)

- **Girard.** The accuser motif at its clearest: "even the archangel Michael, when he was disputing with the devil... did not himself dare to condemn him for slander, but said, 'The Lord rebuke you'" (v.9). Even the archangel refuses to take up the accuser's role. And v.16, "these people are grumblers and faultfinders," the accusing crowd itself. Cain, Balaam and Korah in v.11 are three archetypes of rivalry, greed and rebellion stacked together.
- **Chesterton.** v.3, "contend for the faith that was once for all entrusted to God's holy people." Orthodoxy as the received thing you guard against the clever innovators, Chesterton's entire posture in one line.

### Optional, past the letters

The seven messages to the churches in Revelation 2–3 are letters in their own right, each a diagnosis of one community. They read well as a set once you have the epistles under you.

## How to use this with your daily loop

When you sit down with a letter, do the three moves you already do naturally. Read the passage. Pull the Father's comment on it from Bede, Chrysostom or the ACCS lookup. Then reach for the lens in Part 3 that the passage lit up, Girard or Chesterton or the divided will, and that connection is usually your video's one idea. The commentary keeps you honest to the text, the lens makes it something only you would have said.
