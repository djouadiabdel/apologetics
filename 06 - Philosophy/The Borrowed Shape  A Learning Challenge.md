# The Borrowed Shape  A Learning Challenge

> The one question this whole thing answers:
> **Why does the shape keep showing up, in a light novel and in Osiris and on Golgotha, and what makes one of them different from the other two?**

This started with an anime. You watched a man you loved walk toward a ruin you already knew was coming, and something about the grief of watching that felt like it was showing you God's position. That instinct is worth more than it looks. But right now it is a feeling with three separate claims hidden inside it, and you cannot write the piece until you can pull them apart and put them back together yourself.

 It is reps. Six modules, each one a different pressure on the same idea. The writing is the rep. The essay is downstream of the writing, and the video is downstream of the essay.

The goal is stated in your own words: to build the argument from thin air. So the capstone is not an essay. The capstone is you reconstructing the whole thing on paper with every book shut.

## Module 1 — Why you needed a story at all

Start here, not with the fathers. Lewis is not going to tell you about God in this essay. He is going to tell you about *you*, and about why a proposition you have assented to for years can sit in your head doing nothing.

- **READ** — Lewis, *Myth Became Fact* (in *God in the Dock*, Part I). Short. You can do the whole thing in twenty minutes, but read these ranges twice.

**BEAT 1 — the dilemma. This is the engine of the entire essay.**
From "To explain this we must look a little closer at myth in general" to "But once it stops, what do I know about pain?"
The hinge: *"either to taste and not to know or to know and not to taste."* And then the sharper form of it, that as thinkers we are cut off from what we think about, and as lovers and haters we do not clearly understand. Mark the toothache line. It is the whole problem in one joke.

**BEAT 2 — myth as the partial solution.**
From "Of this tragic dilemma myth is the partial solution" to "It is only while receiving the myth as a story that you experience the principle concretely."
Watch what he does with Orpheus and Eurydice. He states an abstraction, admits he has made heavy weather of it, then tells the story instead and the abstraction becomes imaginable. Note that he insists you were *not* looking for a meaning. The moment you look for the meaning it stops being myth and becomes allegory.

**BEAT 3 — reality against truth.**
From "When we translate we get abstraction" to "nor is it, like direct experience, bound to the particular."
The distinction to carry with you for the rest of your life: *"truth is always about something, but reality is that about which truth is."* Myth as the isthmus. Write that sentence out by hand.

**BEAT 4 — the payload.**
From "Now as myth transcends thought, incarnation transcends myth" to the end.
"The heart of Christianity is a myth which is also a fact." And the last line, which is the one you will end up quoting: the marriage of heaven and earth, perfect myth and perfect fact, addressed to the savage, the child, and the poet no less than to the moralist, the scholar, and the philosopher.

- **FIND** — Lewis converted on Addison's Walk, Magdalen College, the night of 19 September 1931, after Tolkien and Hugo Dyson walked him around until three in the morning. Find his own account in *Surprised by Joy*, chapters 14 to 15, and Carpenter's narration of the same night in *The Inklings*. You want the scene, not just the argument. You will need it for the piece.

- **THINK**
  - Lewis says the dilemma is permanent, not a defect you can train out of. If that is true, what is catechesis actually for?
  - He says a man who disbelieves the story but feeds on it as myth may be more spiritually alive than one who assents and never thinks about it. Sit with how offensive that is before you decide whether he is right.
  - Was your Rudeus experience "tasting" in his sense? Or was it something lower that only felt like it. What test could tell you apart?

- **DELIVERABLE** — `01 - Why the doctrine was inert.md`. 400 to 800 words. Your own account of why a sentence you believed for years did nothing until a story got underneath it.

## Module 2 — Why a story could do it

Lewis explained why you needed the story. Tolkien explains why the story was capable of it. These are different claims and most people collapse them. Do not.

- **READ** — Tolkien, *On Fairy-Stories*.

**BEAT 1 — sub-creation. The load-bearing claim of the whole challenge.**
The Fantasy section, from "Fantasy is a natural human activity" to "made in the image and likeness of a Maker."
His claim is not that stories point at God. It is that human world-making is a derivative instance of divine creation, performed by a creature who cannot stop making because he was made by a Maker. Not analogy. Participation.

**BEAT 2 — the poem, which is the same claim compressed.**
From "Dear Sir," I said—Although now long estranged" to "we make still by the law in which we're made."
Two words to circle: *"used or misused."* He put them in a parenthesis on purpose and you are going to need them in Module 6.

**BEAT 3 — eucatastrophe.**
From "But the 'consolation' of fairy-tales has another aspect" to "He heard and turned to her."
Note that he refuses to call it a happy ending. It is the sudden joyous turn, "never to be counted on to recur," and it does not deny sorrow, it requires it. The possibility of dyscatastrophe is necessary to the joy of deliverance. Then read the four lines of ballad he ends on and notice he does not explain them.

**BEAT 4 — the Epilogue. Read the whole thing, it is two pages.**
From "This 'joy' which I have selected" to "Legend and History have met and fused."
The Birth of Christ as the eucatastrophe of Man's history, the Resurrection as the eucatastrophe of the story of the Incarnation. And the line that will cost you the most to think about honestly: *"There is no tale ever told that men would rather find was true."*

- **FIND** — Read "Leaf by Niggle," which is printed with the essay in *Tree and Leaf*. It is ten pages. It is Tolkien doing the argument as a story instead of as an essay, which is itself the argument. Do not analyse it. Just read it and write down what happened to you.

- **THINK**
  - "There is no tale ever told that men would rather find was true." An atheist hears that as a confession. Wish fulfilment. Write out the atheist's version of that sentence in its strongest form before you try to answer it.
  - Tolkien distinguishes Enchantment from Magic. Enchantment is art and seeks "shared enrichment, partners in making and delight, not slaves." Magic "produces, or pretends to produce, an alteration in the Primary World" and its desire is "power in this world, domination of things and wills." Hold that distinction. In Module 5 you will find it maps onto something Girard says, and you will want to have seen it first.
  - If sub-creation is real, what exactly is Rifujin doing when he breaks Rudeus. Is he imitating God, or imitating something else.

- **DELIVERABLE** — `02 - Sub-creation, not analogy.md`. Argue Tolkien's claim in your own words, including why "participation" is a stronger word than "analogy" and what it costs you to say it.

## Module 3 — What you actually tasted

Now the fathers. This module is the content of the experience, not a defence of it. Read for the thing itself.

- **READ** — five short texts. None of these is long. All are free online.

**A. The grief is real.**
Origen, *Homilies on Ezekiel*, Homily 6, section 6. A few paragraphs. He argues that the Father "suffers something of love," and, crucially, that this suffering of love is the *cause* of the Incarnation rather than a decoration on it. Notice he holds this alongside impassibility and does not think he has contradicted himself. Work out how.

**B. He knows without causing.**
John of Damascus, *An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith*, Book II, chapters 29 and 30. Two pages total, at newadvent.org/fathers/33042.htm. The formula you want: God foreknows all things but does not predetermine all things, and "it is not His will that there should be wickedness nor does He choose to compel virtue."

**C. He made us anyway, with all of it in view.**
Irenaeus, *Against Heresies*, Book IV, chapters 37, 38, and 39. This is the longest read in the module and the most important. 37 is freedom, and why compelled good is not good. 38 is why humanity was made immature and had to grow. 39 is the knowledge of evil by experience.

**D. It was never plan B.**
Maximus the Confessor, *Ad Thalassium* 60, on 1 Peter 1:20, the mystery of Christ foreknown before the foundation of the world. Then *Ambigua* 7 if you have the appetite. Maximus is hard. Read him slowly and expect to fail once.

**E. He loves the same regardless.**
Isaac the Syrian, the "merciful heart" homily. **Verify** the number: it is Homily 71 in the Holy Transfiguration Monastery translation and is cited as 81 in other numberings. Read it for the feeling, not the argument. It is the only text in this module that is not trying to prove anything.

- **FIND** — John 11:1 to 44, NIV. Read the whole thing in one sitting, slowly.
He is told Lazarus is sick and deliberately waits. He says outright, "Lazarus is dead, and for your sake I am glad I was not there, so that you may believe." He arrives knowing exactly what he is about to do. He sees Mary weeping. He is "deeply moved in spirit and troubled." Then, "Jesus wept."
`[ ]` Write down what that verse is doing there, given verse 15. Take your time. This may be the whole essay.

- **THINK**
  - Origen says the grief causes the Incarnation. Most people would say the Incarnation displays the grief. Which way round is it, and does it matter?
  - Irenaeus says compelled good is not good. Test it hard. Is that obviously true, or is it a thing theologians say because they need it to be true?
  - Does Isaac's God love the sinner *despite* the sin or *without reference to* the sin? These are not the same and one of them is much harder to defend.

- **DELIVERABLE** — `03 - The grief that does not cancel.md`. What the fathers say is happening, in your words, with John 11 as the anchor.

## Module 4 — Where your feeling is wrong

Every module so far has been building you up. This one takes something away, and you should let it. A piece that never corrects its own starting instinct reads as advertising.

- **READ**

**A. Your grief is temporal. His is not.**
Boethius, *The Consolation of Philosophy*, Book V. Prose 3 for the problem stated at full strength, prose 6 for the answer. The concept is *totum simul*, the whole at once. God does not fore-see. There is no "in advance" for him, so his knowledge is closer to sight than to prediction.
This dismantles the most emotionally satisfying part of your original thought. You were imagining God as a viewer who already knows what episode 12 does. On Boethius that is exactly wrong. It is not a wince before the blow. It is presence to the blow while it lands.

**B. Your picture is a translation.**
John Chrysostom, *Homilies on Genesis*. **Verify** and record the numbers yourself: find the homily treating Genesis 6:5 to 7, where "the Lord regretted that he had made human beings on the earth, and his heart was deeply troubled," and also the homily treating Genesis 3:8, where God walks in the garden. The concept is *synkatabasis*, condescension. God speaks in the register of human grief because you have no other register to hear him in.
This does not deny the grief. It warns you that when you picture it, you are picturing a scaled-down translation, and that mistaking the translation for the thing is how people end up with a small sentimental God.

- **THINK**
  - If Boethius is right, is the word "foreknowledge" doing you any good at all, or should you drop it from the piece?
  - Chrysostom and Origen appear to be pulling opposite directions. Are they, actually? Write the strongest version of each and see whether they touch.
  - Which of the two corrections costs you more, and is that a reason to trust it or to distrust it?

- **DELIVERABLE** — `04 - Two corrections I have to take.md`. Concede both in writing, then state what survives. If everything survives you have not conceded properly.

## Module 5 — The enemy at full strength

This is the module the whole thing exists for. Same move Gregory of Nyssa pulls in *On Not Three Gods*, where he hands the objector a bigger weapon on purpose so his own answer has to hold firmer. You are going to arm two opponents and neither of them is a fool.

### Opponent 1 — Girard against Lewis

Lewis, in the essay you already read, says this:

> "We must not be nervous about 'parallels' and 'pagan Christs': they ought to be there, it would be a stumbling block if they weren't."

Balder and Osiris are good dreams. Refracted light. Humanity half-remembering something.

- **READ** — Girard, *I See Satan Fall Like Lightning*. Locate and read the chapters on mythology and on the uniqueness of the biblical account, including the Apollonius of Tyana material. Then *Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World*, Book II, the section on the Judeo-Christian scriptures.

Girard's answer is that the dying god is not a preview of anything. He is a victim whose murder was sacralized by the crowd that committed it, and the entire function of the myth is to hide that. On this reading the Gospel is not the fulfilment of the dying-god myth. It is its demolition, the same event finally narrated from the victim's side with the machinery exposed.

`[ ]` Write both positions out at full strength, in their own best words, before you attempt any reconciliation. If Girard's version does not genuinely threaten Lewis on the page, you have written it too weakly and you have to do it again.

**The complication you already know is coming**, because it is your own project: Girard's handling of Greek tragedy. *The Bacchae*, *Prometheus Bound*, *Oedipus at Colonus* all come uncomfortably close to victim-sided narration, and his answer is that tragedy is a partial or interrupted revelation. Decide for yourself whether that is an insight or an escape hatch, and remember your own rule from `~/Myths`. Never tune the instrument to pass.

- `[ ]` State the weak form and the strong form of the claim, and commit in writing to which one you will defend on camera:
  - **Strong**: myth *never* sides with the victim. Probably false, and unfalsifiable in the way it is usually rescued.
  - **Weak**: myth typically narrates from the crowd's position and presents the destruction as necessary or deserved, and reversals of that default are rare and traceable. Falsifiable, codeable, and it is what your lexicon study already measures.

### Opponent 2 — Feuerbach against Tolkien

This is the harder one and nobody will hand it to you.

- **READ** — Feuerbach, *The Essence of Christianity*, the Introduction and chapters 1 to 2.

Tolkien says we make gods because we were made by a Maker and cannot help imitating him. Feuerbach says we make gods because we project ourselves, and theology is anthropology that has forgotten what it is. **Same data. Opposite direction of the arrow.** Every parallel Tolkien reads as a fingerprint, Feuerbach reads as a self-portrait.

- **THINK**
  - Is there any observation that would distinguish the two, or do they explain the identical evidence with equal economy? Answer honestly. The honest answer may hurt.
  - Does the resemblance between myth and Gospel actually favour either one of them, or is it neutral ground both sides have claimed?
  - Where did you first notice yourself wanting one of them to win. Name the moment.

- **DELIVERABLE** — `05 - The two strongest objections.md`. Write it as their lawyer, not yours. No rebuttals in this file. The rebuttal is Module 6 and it does not get to start early.

## Module 6 — Your own move

You already have this one. You said it in a sentence and it is better than the synthesis I offered you, so it goes in the challenge as yours.

> Pagan myth is a lynching told by the lynchers, using the only method known to them, which is the method of the image of the Creator who made them. Of course it can have a shape like that.

The claim underneath it: the sub-creative faculty does not switch off when it is being used to cover a murder. So the crowd, needing a story that makes the killing holy, reaches into the only toolkit it has, and pulls out death-that-gives-life, the one who dies for the many, the victim who becomes the god. Not because they glimpsed anything. Because that is the grain of the wood and they cannot carve against it.

This gets you both things at once. It explains why the parallels exist, which is Lewis's data, without granting that the pagans had a preview, which is Girard's objection. The myth resembles the Cross the way a forgery resembles the original, and the forgery is only possible because the forger has the original's hand.

**Tolkien already wrote your paragraph.** Go back to the Fantasy section and find it:

> "Fantasy can, of course, be carried to excess. It can be ill done. It can be put to evil uses. It may even delude the minds out of which it came. But of what human thing in this fallen world is that not true? Men have conceived not only of elves, but they have imagined gods, and worshipped them, even worshipped those most deformed by their authors' own evil... *Abusus non tollit usum.*"

Abuse does not abolish use. "Even worshipped those most deformed by their authors' own evil" is the sacralized lynching, written forty years before Girard, by a man who was not arguing with him.

- **FIND** — the Cauldron of Story section, Ingeld and Freawaru and the god Frey. From "The great enemy of King Hrothgar was Froda" to "spell means both a story told, and a formula of power over living men." The line to take: *"History often resembles 'Myth,' because they are both ultimately of the same stuff."* Decide whether that helps you or hurts you. It could be either.

- **THINK, and these are the ones I am not answering for you**
  - Your move explains the resemblance. Does it prove a Maker, or does it only work once a Maker is already granted? Answer this before anyone asks you, because someone will.
  - As stated, can your move absorb any counterexample? Myth looks like the Cross, the image leaked through. Myth looks nothing like it, the fall deformed it. If nothing can count against it, it is an explanation and not a test. Are you comfortable presenting it as an explanation? Say so out loud in the piece if you are.
  - If pagan myth has the true shape borrowed and the Gospel has the true shape truly, the difference has to be more than "one of them happened." What is it? Girard hands you an answer. Work out whether you accept it before you use it.
  - The authorship objection, which is the one that will actually get thrown at you: Rifujin wrote the suffering *for the effect*. If God did that, God is a monster who wrote a tragedy to be admired for having written it. Irenaeus and Maximus are where the answer lives. Find it and state it in one sentence you could say on camera.

- **DELIVERABLE** — `06 - The borrowed shape.md`. Your move, stated, defended, and limited. The limits are not a weakness in the essay. They are the reason anyone will believe the rest of it.

- `[ ]` Reread `00 - My naive answer (borrowed shape).md`. Note every place it changed and every place it did not.

**A note on what the piece is.** It is not an argument for the Incarnation with an anime as the hook. It is a confession with an argument inside it. You knew the doctrine, it was inert, a Japanese light novel about a broken man made you understand what you already believed, and that is not embarrassing, it is how a human mind works, and there are old men who explained exactly why. The fathers and Tolkien and Lewis are not your evidence. They are the people who show up partway through and tell you what happened to you.

Rough shape, hold it loosely:

1. What you felt watching. Plain. The smile fading.
2. Naming it. Foreknowledge, love, non-intervention.
3. The turn. You already believed this and it had never once landed.
4. Lewis. Taste and know.
5. Tolkien. Why an author can be in this position at all.
6. The fathers. What you actually tasted, and Boethius correcting you about the dread.
7. Where it breaks. Rifujin wrote the suffering. God wrote the freedom and then walked into what it produced.
8. John 11:35.
